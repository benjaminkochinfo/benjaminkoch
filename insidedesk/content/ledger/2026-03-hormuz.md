On 28 February the United States and Israel launched joint air strikes on Iran [1]. On 2 March Iran's Revolutionary Guards announced the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, and traffic fell to an average of about five vessels a day, against roughly a hundred before the war [4]. By the time the IEA published its March oil report on the 12th, flows of crude and products through the strait had gone "from around 20 mb/d before the war to a trickle", and Gulf producers had cut output by at least 10 million barrels a day [1]. The agency called it "the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market" [1].

**The screen and the cargo.** The physical price moved first and furthest. On the EIA's spot series Brent was $71.32 on 27 February and $126.69 on 31 March, a rise of about 78% in a month on the desk's arithmetic [5]. Futures were calmer: the IEA noted Brent futures had traded "within a whisker of $120" before easing to around $92 as it went to press [1]. Two prices, one barrel, and the gap between them was the cost of actually having the oil.

**The cupboard opened.** On 11 March all 32 IEA member countries agreed unanimously to make 400 million barrels of emergency stocks available [2]. It was the sixth collective action since the agency was founded in 1974, after 1991, 2005, 2011 and twice in 2022 [2], and by the IEA's own description "by far the largest ever" [3]. Members held more than 1.2 billion barrels of emergency stockpiles, plus 600 million barrels of industry stocks held under government obligation [2].

**Same units, please.** Four hundred million barrels sounds like an answer. Measured against a disruption of nearly 20 million barrels a day [1], it is about twenty days of the missing flow, on the desk's arithmetic, released over months rather than days [3]. The IEA said as much, in calmer words: the release was "a significant and welcome buffer", and the most important factor for stable flows was "the resumption of regular transit of shipping through the Strait of Hormuz" [3].

**Hindsight.** March taught the year's central lesson early. Stocks buy time; corridors decide prices. Every later month in this ledger is, in one way or another, a footnote to that sentence. If you run a business with a fuel bill, the useful question from March was never "how much is being released?" but "when do ships move again, and who insures them?"
