April began with the year's highest print and a ceasefire. On the EIA spot series Brent reached $138.21 on 7 April, the day the United States and Iran agreed a two-week ceasefire [6], and fell to $122.11 the next day [4]. A week later, on 14 April, the IMF published its spring World Economic Outlook under the title *Global Economy in the Shadow of War* [1].

**The honest part was the format.** The Fund did not call its central number a baseline. It called it a "reference forecast", predicated on a war of "limited duration, intensity, and scope, such that the disruptions will fade by mid-2026", consistent with futures prices as of 10 March [1]. On that basis world growth would be 3.1% in 2026, 0.2 percentage point lower than in January, with headline inflation rising to 4.4% [1]. Without the war, the Fund said, it would have revised growth up, to 3.4% [2].

[[chart:imf-2026-scenarios]]

**Then it printed the alternatives.** In an adverse scenario, growth falls to 2.5% and inflation rises to 5.4%; in a severe one, where disruptions extend into 2027, growth falls to 2% this year and next and inflation exceeds 6% [2]. Reuters reported the same day that the world was "already drifting toward a more adverse scenario", and that the reference case assumed oil averaging $82 a barrel in 2026, well below the futures price of about $96 [3].

**Why this matters for you.** A reference forecast is not a prediction; it is a conditional sentence with the condition in small print. If your budget, your pay round or your mortgage plan borrowed the 3.1%, it also borrowed the assumption that the strait would reopen by summer. The useful reading of April was to price the condition, not the headline.

**Hindsight.** The condition failed; the number, curiously, held. In October the IMF still projected 3.1% for 2026, "broadly unchanged from April, despite a more persistent energy market disruption than anticipated" [5]. The economy bent around the shock through rerouting, inventories and an investment boom [5]. The footnote was wrong and the headline survived, which is the most dangerous kind of right: it teaches people to skip the footnote.
