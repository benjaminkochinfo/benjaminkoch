On Friday 22 May Kevin Warsh took the oath of office as chairman of the Federal Reserve, and the Federal Open Market Committee unanimously chose him as its chair [1]. He had been nominated on 4 March and confirmed by the Senate as chairman on 13 May, by 54 votes to 45 [1][2]. Jerome Powell's term as chair ended the same day; he had led the Board since 5 February 2018 and remains a governor, in a seat that runs to January 2028 [3]. For the week in between, the Board named him chair pro tempore, "consistent with past practice" [4].

The ceremony was not the usual one. NBC News noted that the last Fed chair sworn in at the White House was Alan Greenspan, in 1987 [5]. If you read institutions by their rituals, that was the detail to notice. If you read them by their votes, it was not.

**The votes.** At the new chair's first meeting, on 16–17 June, the committee held the federal funds target at 3.5% to 3.75% by twelve votes to none, in a statement that ended: "The Committee will deliver price stability" [6]. In July it held again, but three members, Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari and Lorie Logan, dissented because they wanted a quarter-point increase [7]. In September the committee raised the range to 3.75% to 4%, unanimously, saying the move "will support a timelier return to the Committee's 2 percent goal" [8].

**The arithmetic did not change with the chair.** Inflation was, in the committee's own words, "elevated relative to the Committee's 2 percent goal, in part reflecting supply shocks… including energy" [6]. A chair can change the tone, the length of the statement and the setting of the oath. He cannot change what an oil shock does to a price index, or how eleven other voters read it.

**Hindsight.** Whatever you expected of the change at the top, the record from May to September is one of a committee tightening into a supply shock, not loosening around a personality. That is not a judgement on anyone's independence. It is a reminder of where the decision sits: in a room with twelve votes and published minutes. Institutions beat influencers, including when the influencer holds the chair.
