After more than a year and over 50 sessions, the expert commission on reforming Germany's code of criminal procedure handed justice minister Stefanie Hubig its roughly 500-page final report on Monday, with 90 recommendations [1]. Among them: courts would rely more often on earlier police and prosecution interviews instead of re-hearing witnesses, witnesses abroad could be heard by video, and trials would be audio-recorded and transcribed automatically, at first as a five-year pilot at selected courts [1][2]. Appeal courts would decide more often whether to take a case at all [2]. Hubig called the work "extraordinarily constructive", adding that the rule of law "needs compromises again and again" [1]. The German Judges' Association is disappointed: regional-court trials now take two years on average, longer than ever, and prosecutors carry more than a million open cases [1][2]. A transcript nobody has to type is not glamorous. It is how a court gets its afternoons back.
