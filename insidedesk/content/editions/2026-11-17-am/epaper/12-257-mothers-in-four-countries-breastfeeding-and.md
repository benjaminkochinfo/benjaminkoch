Researchers at the University of Sharjah pooled Demographic and Health Survey data on 12,257 mothers aged 15 to 49 in Lesotho, Mozambique, Nepal and Bangladesh [1]. Depression was measured with survey items adapted from the Patient Health Questionnaire, and the models adjusted for age, education and household wealth [1]. The paper, by Amna Khalid and colleagues, is in Discover Mental Health [1].

The headline finding: breastfeeding went with fewer depressive symptoms overall, but the strength and significance of the link varied, with the most consistent effect in Bangladesh and in the pooled sample [1].

Two numbers deserve a second look. Only 5.7% of the mothers reported depressive symptoms, well below the 19.8% the authors cite for maternal depression in low- and middle-income countries generally [1]. And 96.1% were breastfeeding, from 92.3% in Lesotho to 98.9% in Nepal [1]. When almost everyone breastfeeds, the comparison group is small, and the mothers who do not may differ in many other ways: illness, workload, a difficult birth.

The authors are careful. They call for longitudinal studies to establish which comes first, and warn against overinterpreting [1]. Their practical point is the useful one: breastfeeding counselling already reaches most mothers in these countries, so it could be a door for mental-health support, especially for women struggling with feeding or mood who "may be less visible within routine services" [1].
