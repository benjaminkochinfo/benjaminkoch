Médecins Sans Frontières says shortages of diphtheria antitoxin are undermining the response to the outbreak in northern Nigeria [1]. "The global shortage of diphtheria antitoxin is becoming a matter of life and death," said Dr David Kizito, MSF's medical coordinator in Nigeria; "our stocks are dangerously low" [1].

The antitoxin is the only specific treatment that neutralises the toxin, and it works best within 48 hours of the first symptoms [1]. It is on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines, yet supply remains inconsistent, and MSF teams have repeatedly had to ration it [1].

The numbers behind the wards: since late 2022, more than 41,000 suspected cases, over 24,000 confirmed and more than 1,260 deaths [1]. In 2026 alone, up to 9 August, 12,977 suspected cases and 406 deaths [1]. In Gusau, Zamfara State, MSF-supported facilities admitted more than 671 patients in a month from 6 October; four in five had not been vaccinated [1]. For a reactive campaign there, only 25,000 of the 90,000 doses needed had arrived [1].

MSF asks for three dull things: clearance of supplies at ports without delay, more antitoxin production by manufacturers, and long-term money for routine immunisation [1]. The last is the cheapest. A vaccine given at six weeks old does not need to be rationed at a hospital door.
