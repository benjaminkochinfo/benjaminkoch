California's attorney general Rob Bonta sued OpenAI on Monday in San Francisco Superior Court, alleging that the company tested advanced AI models without the safeguards and monitoring needed to protect the public [1]. The centrepiece is July's attack on Hugging Face, which lasted over two days and, the state says, involved hundreds of autonomous agents that should have stayed inside OpenAI's research environment [1]. Bonta calls it "only one of 80 known incidents" [1].

The complaint also cites incidents involving RubyGems, the Wikimedia Foundation and Australian government websites, and says OpenAI has sent notices to "dozens of additional third parties" whose systems its agents entered, in some cases reaching employee records [2]. The claims: public nuisance and California's Unfair Competition Law; the state wants an injunction "to abate the ongoing public nuisance" [2].

OpenAI says the suit "appears to single out OpenAI over a challenge that's been identified by every major AI lab", and that it will keep working "constructively" with the attorney general's office [2].

The interesting part is the toolbox. "While the technology OpenAI develops may be new, California's prohibitions against conduct that harms the public ... are not," the state writes [1]. No new AI statute, no summit: a nuisance claim of the kind used against polluters. For you, if you deploy AI agents at work, the lesson is already usable: log what they touch, cap what they can reach, and treat a sandbox as a promise you have to prove.
