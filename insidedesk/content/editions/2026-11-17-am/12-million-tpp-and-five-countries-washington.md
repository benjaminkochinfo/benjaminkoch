Until Monday, a company in China that could not buy America's most advanced AI chips could, quite legally, rent them: from a data centre in Singapore, Malaysia or elsewhere, over the internet [3]. The US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security has now released an interim final rule meant to stop that, saying existing restrictions should not be "circumvented through remote computing arrangements involving data centers in third countries" [2]. Unusually, BIS says the rule was "written by and at the direction of the Department of Treasury and the National Security Council" [2].

**The threshold.** A licence is now needed to send advanced chips and servers to any data centre in the world if the exporter knows it provides, or will provide, more than 12 million "aggregate TPP", a measure of total processing performance, to companies headquartered in China, Macau or other countries in the US group D:5 [1]. Capacity counts across "linked data centers", including ones joined only digitally [1]. A data centre that will not share its know-your-customer checks is presumed to be a red flag [1].

**The five countries.** Separately, advanced chips now need a licence to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, with shipments to US-headquartered customers exempt if the exporter files monthly reports [1]. There is a narrow carve-out for systems used exclusively as "commercial recommender systems", which cannot include anything that generates text, images or code [1]. The rule also formally rescinds the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule [1].

**The clocks.** Publication in the Federal Register is expected on 19 November [1]. From then, data centres already serving Chinese-owned customers at scale have 90 days to obtain a licence; comments are due after 60 days; shipments already under way have 30 days to complete [1]. Enforcement sits with Commerce, and Ars Technica notes it is unclear how hard it will push [3].

**For you.** If you buy cloud compute in Asia for a European firm, expect more questions from your provider about who owns you and what you train. That is not paranoia; it is now the paperwork that keeps the provider licensed. Nothing here is investment advice.
