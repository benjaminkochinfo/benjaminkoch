On Tuesday evening one of the American retail brokers closes its order book for the Anthropic share sale. Fidelity says the offer period is expected to end at 4:00 p.m. New York time, which is 22:00 CET, with the price set on Wednesday evening [1]. Trading on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT is scheduled for Thursday [2]. The offering is for 825 million shares at an estimated $70 to $78 each, which CNBC says would raise more than $64 billion and value the company at $1.405 trillion [2]. Up to 20% is expected to go to retail investors [2].

**The rules at the counter.** Fidelity's minimum indication of interest is 25 shares, in steps of 25, and customers need at least $25,000 in certain assets there to take part [1]. Desk arithmetic: 25 shares at the bottom and top of the range cost $1,750 to $1,950. Fidelity is blunt about the queue: an indication of interest "does not entitle you to receive an allocation", and customers "may receive fewer shares than they requested or, in some cases, no shares at all" [1]. Requesting more does not help; allocation ranks customers by their assets and revenue with the firm, not by the size of the order [1].

**The rule after the counter.** Sell within 15 calendar days of the first trade and Fidelity labels you a flipper: blocked from new issues for six months the first time, a year the second, permanently the third [1]. If you are allocated shares, the cash must be there by settlement, usually the next business day [1]. That is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It is the machinery that lets a share sale of this size clear in an orderly way.

**The demand figure, read properly.** Bloomberg reported orders at about seven times the shares on offer, CNBC TV18 notes [3]. Demand at the institutional desk is what makes small allocations likely at the retail one. A popular sale is not a cheap one, and the price is set after the book closes, not before [1].

**For you.** If you are in Germany, these particular US broker rules may not apply to you; read your own bank's terms, and the prospectus, before you place anything [1]. The point stands everywhere: a share sale has a price, a date and a set of rules, and only the rules are known tonight. Nothing here is investment advice.
