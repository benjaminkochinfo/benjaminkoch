Asia opened Tuesday where Wall Street left it on Monday, which is to say lower. On Monday the S&P 500 lost about 1.9% and the Nasdaq Composite 2.5%, and a gauge of US chip stocks sank 5.3% [5]. By 04:41 CET, Newsquawk had Australia's ASX 200 down 1.6%, Japan's Nikkei 225 down 1.2%, the Kospi and the Hang Seng each down 0.8% and the Shanghai Composite down 0.2% [1]. These are intraday snapshots, not closes.

[[chart:asia-tuesday-17nov]]

**The figures, with their times.** AFP's table at around 03:30 CET had the Nikkei down 1.2% at 58,956.67, the Kospi down 1.1% at 5,043.51, the Hang Seng down 1.3% at 23,457.40 and Shanghai down 0.7% at 3,576.57 [2]. Reuters reported the Kospi down 2% shortly after the open and Taiwan down 1.4% [3]. The Kospi opened 1.81% lower at 5,008 and then regained some ground, RTHK reports, with Samsung Electronics down 2.41% [4]. In Tokyo, Kioxia, Advantest and SoftBank were among the worst hit [1]. Note how the numbers move between sources: a falling market is not one number but a series of them, each with a clock.

**Two causes, not one.** The first is the calendar. Nvidia publishes its results for the quarter to 25 October at about 1:20 p.m. Pacific time, which is 22:20 CET, with a call at 23:00 CET [6]. It reports days after Jensen Huang's resignation as chief executive, and Reuters notes investors will watch whether its AI spending continues into 2027 [3]. "With a roughly 7.8% implied move and a strong beat-and-raise already embedded, the bar looks increasingly tied to forward growth, AI ecosystem financing and export-control risk rather than the quarter alone," UBS's Torsten Sippel told Reuters [3].

The second cause is a document. On Monday the US Commerce Department published a rule requiring licences for advanced AI chips shipped to data centres in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil, aimed at stopping Chinese firms reaching the chips remotely [2]. "This is a meaningful escalation because it extends restrictions from physically buying advanced chips to accessing their computing power remotely," said Saxo's Charu Chanana [4]. A licence requirement is not a mood. It is a form, a queue and a compliance department, and it touches the region's data-centre hubs whatever Nvidia says tonight.

**What was not the cause.** Australia fell most, and not because of chips: Newsquawk blames commodity-related sectors, and Reuters reports copper at its lowest in more than four months [1][3]. The Reserve Bank of Australia's minutes, released on Tuesday, said inflation was still too high [1]. Mainland China fell least, cushioned, Newsquawk says, by the central bank's liquidity operations [1]. A region that moves together for different reasons is not panicking; it is sorting.

**The dollar did the louder work.** The dollar index touched 103.69, its highest since April 2025, on bets on further Federal Reserve rate rises; the euro touched $1.0985, also its lowest since April 2025, and the yen hovered near 160 per dollar [3]. Brent crude slid 0.9% to $91.62 a barrel and spot gold edged 0.1% higher to $3,860.30 an ounce [3]. US stock futures edged higher in Asia, Nasdaq futures up 0.35% and S&P 500 futures 0.13%, while Euro Stoxx 50, DAX and FTSE futures were down roughly 0.6% each [3].

**The scenario everyone quoted.** Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh on Monday sketched one illustrative outcome in which artificial intelligence could "disappoint": "After significant capital investment and stock market gains, expected returns could turn down and equity valuations could fall" [7]. Read the next sentence too. He called these "illustrative scenarios, not forecasts", and set beside it a scenario in which productivity gains are "shared broadly across labor and the owners of capital" [7]. A central banker listing contingencies is doing his job. It is not a sell signal.

**For you.** Tonight's numbers from Nvidia are a single company's invoice to its customers; the Commerce rule is a standing change to who may buy. If you own a broad index, you own both, and the second will still be in force next quarter. Desk arithmetic: the 1.4-point gap between Sydney (-1.6%) and Shanghai (-0.2%) this morning is wider than the gap between Seoul and Hong Kong, which was zero [1]. Read the sectors before the headline. Nothing here is investment advice.
