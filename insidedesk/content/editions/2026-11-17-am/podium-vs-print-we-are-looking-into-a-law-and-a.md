**The podium.** Last Thursday Malaysia's trade minister, Johari Abdul Ghani, said his government was drafting a law requiring data centres to disclose their operators and hyperscalers, their locations, their electricity use and the number of graphics processors they import [1]. The aim, he said, is that chips brought into Malaysia are used there, not re-exported. "What happened previously was that some companies would rent space in data centres and import GPUs into Malaysia, but did not actually use the chips," he said; the government was "looking into whether there are laws that would allow us to take action" [1].

**The print.** Four days later Washington did not wait for the law. The Commerce Department's interim final rule, released on Monday and written, the department says, at the direction of the Treasury and the National Security Council, requires a licence to export, re-export or transfer advanced chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand or Brazil [2][3]. It also restricts shipments to overseas data centres that remotely supply "significant volumes of compute" to companies headquartered in China and other countries of concern, and requires data centres, foundries and exporters to check who ultimately owns their customers [2]. Chips for US-headquartered companies are exempt if reporting rules are met [2]. The rule takes effect on publication in the Federal Register, with comments due within 60 days [2].

**The gap.** It is not one of intent; both sides say the same thing. It is one of tense. Malaysia's minister spoke in the conditional, and the separate data-centre bill is expected at cabinet only next year [1]. The American text is in the indicative and comes with consequences: Commerce says it will consider import restrictions, new licence requirements and Entity List designations for countries that "tolerate evasion" [2]. What is at stake for Malaysia is not small. Data centres accounted for 9.28% of its electricity consumption as of September, a share the Energy Commission projects could reach 31% by 2035 [1].

**For you.** A licence requirement is dull, and dull is the point: it moves a decision from a minister's sentence to a form with a reference number. Saxo's Charu Chanana warned that licensing uncertainty "could make financing new projects harder" [4]. If you hold funds exposed to Southeast Asian data-centre builders, the paperwork, not the speeches, is what to read this quarter. Nothing here is investment advice.
