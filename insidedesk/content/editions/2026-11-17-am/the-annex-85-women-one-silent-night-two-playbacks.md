The Annex reads the paper behind the headline. The headline says sleeping on an embarrassment does less for people with borderline personality disorder [1]. The study, by researchers at University Psychiatric Hospital Duffel in Belgium and accepted in eClinicalMedicine, says that too, with clauses worth reading [1].

**Clause one: the sample.** 44 women with borderline personality disorder and 41 women without a psychiatric disorder [1]. All women, so the result says nothing yet about men.

**Clause two: the task.** Each sang "Silent Night" wearing noise-cancelling headphones, so they could not hear themselves [1]. They then watched the recording in the evening and again the next morning, knowing researchers were watching too [1]. Sensors recorded their sleep in between [1].

**Clause three: the result.** Both groups were more distressed after the evening playback [1]. By morning the comparison group felt less stirred up; the women with the disorder did not show the same easing [1]. Measured against how they felt before each playback, distress reactivity fell by around 40 percentage points overnight in the comparison group and rose by around 27 in the women with the disorder [1]. Their post-playback distress stayed about six points higher [1].

**Clause four: the small print.** Everyone slept, so there was no awake comparison, and the study cannot show that sleep caused the difference [1]. The REM eye-movement findings were too uncertain to conclude anything [1].

**For you.** "Our findings suggest that 'sleep on it' may work differently in people who already struggle with intense emotions," said researcher Mariana Mendoza-Alvarez [1]. The supervising psychiatrist, Livia De Picker, asks clinicians to treat bad sleep "as a real part of their care" [1]. If someone you love lives with the disorder, the advice to wait until morning may need company: a call, a plan, not just a pillow.
