The US Food and Drug Administration has approved gefurulimab, sold as Klygefa by Alexion and AstraZeneca, for adults with generalised myasthenia gravis who test positive for antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor [1]. The company says it is the first drug to combine C5 complement inhibition with once-weekly self-injection by autoinjector [1]. Until now, the class has mostly relied on intravenous infusion [1].

Myasthenia gravis is a rare autoimmune disease that interrupts the signal between nerve and muscle: drooping eyelids, double vision, slurred speech, and in severe cases trouble swallowing and breathing [1]. An estimated 98,500 people in the US are diagnosed with the generalised form, and about 85% are antibody-positive [1].

The evidence is one phase 3 trial, PREVAIL, run at 113 sites in 20 countries: 260 adults, 131 on gefurulimab and 129 on placebo, for 26 weeks [1]. On the daily-living score, symptoms fell by 4.2 points on the drug against 2.6 on placebo, a difference of 1.6 [1]. On the clinician-rated score, 46.8% of treated patients improved by at least five points, against 28.0% on placebo [1]. Desk arithmetic: placebo patients improved too, and the drug's margin on the five-point line is 18.8 percentage points, roughly one patient in five.

The safety table is unremarkable in the good sense. Adverse events were reported in 75.6% on the drug and 80.6% on placebo; serious ones in 9.2% and 11.6%, none judged treatment-related, and no meningococcal infections [1]. The class risk does not disappear, though: the CDC says people on complement inhibitors face a meningococcal risk up to 2,000 times higher, and recommends MenACWY and MenB vaccination, while warning that vaccination protects incompletely [1].

For you, if you or someone close lives with the condition, the practical news is logistical. A weekly pen at home replaces trips to an infusion centre, and shifts the work to pharmacists: injection technique, site rotation, the weekly rhythm, and the vaccination check before the first dose [1]. That is less dramatic than a breakthrough and, for a chronic disease, often more useful.
