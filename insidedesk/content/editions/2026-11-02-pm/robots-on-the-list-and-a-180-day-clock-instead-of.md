The Federal Register published a proclamation on Monday that a lot of factory managers will read twice. Under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, the US Commerce Secretary has found that robotics, industrial machinery and their parts are "being imported into the United States in such quantities and under such circumstances as to threaten to impair the national security", and the President concurred [1]. It was signed on 28 October and appeared in print today [1].

The finding is broad. The text says robots and industrial machines are used across all 16 US critical infrastructure sectors, that the country "relies on foreign imports for most types" of them, and that machines from "certain foreign entities" carry cybersecurity risks because they process and store sensitive data [1]. It puts the stakes at about $7.1 trillion in shipments of manufactured goods in 2024 and about $86 billion of equipment produced in 2024 [1]. Desk arithmetic: the equipment itself is about 1.2% of what it helps make. That is the point of the sector. Small in value, everywhere in use.

Now the part that matters for your order book. The remedy is not a tariff. The Commerce Secretary and the US Trade Representative are told to "pursue or continue pursuing negotiations of agreements" with trading partners, and to report on progress within 180 days [1]. Only "depending on the status or outcome" of those talks may the President consider tariffs, duties, quotas, fees, or a programme to encourage new US production [1]. The law lets him act if no agreement is reached within 180 days, or if one is reached and not carried out [1].

So the European machine-tool maker, the Japanese robot arm and the German gearbox supplier have a date rather than a duty. That is more useful than it sounds. A negotiation produces paperwork: lists, quotas, investment pledges, all of which can be costed and planned around. A speech cannot.

The sensible reading for a buyer in Stuttgart or Ohio is dull: no rate, no list, no start date has been published [1]. Keep the invoices as they are, and keep a copy of the 180-day clock on the wall.
