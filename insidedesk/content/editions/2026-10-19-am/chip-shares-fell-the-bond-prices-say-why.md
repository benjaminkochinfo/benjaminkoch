Friday's selling in chipmakers, which took the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index down 3.8% [2], is usually told as a story about mood. The more useful version is told in bond prices.

CoreWeave's most-traded bonds, paying 9.75% and due in 2031, traded as low as 78 cents on the dollar on 14 October, a yield of 16.39%, having started October at about 85 [1]. Its 9.625% bonds due 2032 slumped to 75, for a 16.5% yield, against 103.625 on 2 June [1]. Both recovered some ground the next day, to 83 and 80 [1]. In the same week the data-centre developer Cipher Digital announced an $850 million offering of zero-coupon convertible notes, and JPMorgan analysts cut Applied Digital's project bonds to neutral, citing a looming "supply overhang" of debt still to be raised [1].

That is the mechanism. Much of the AI build-out is financed by lenders who expect to be paid back in fixed instalments. When the price of that debt rises, the cost of every future data centre rises with it, and the shares of companies selling into those data centres get marked down in sympathy. It is not a verdict on the technology. It is a repricing of the loan.

The same report carries the counter-signal. CleanSpark's bonds held up better, helped by good news from its client Meta, after investors read early retention data for Meta's Muse assistant as evidence that the company can earn money on its AI spending [1]. Meta reports on Wednesday, alongside Tesla, Lam Research and Texas Instruments [3].

Those reports are the invoices. Read three lines: capital spending, cash flow, and who is paying whom. The rest is the podium.
