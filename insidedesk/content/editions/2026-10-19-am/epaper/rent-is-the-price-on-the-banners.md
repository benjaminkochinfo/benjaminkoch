Portuguese house prices rose by 124% between 2015 and 2024, roughly twice as fast as the EU average, according to official data cited by the Guardian [1]. About a quarter of Portugal's more than 11 million people live in greater Lisbon, where the pressure is sharpest [1]. "We are all here together to try to explain that we are suffocating," said Elena Costa, a 46-year-old social assistant at the Rossio Square camp [1].

In Spain the trigger was one household: an 87-year-old woman evicted from her flat after the investment fund that bought it sought a 230% rent increase; María del Carmen Abascal died about a fortnight later [1][2]. Protesters there ask for lower rents, an end to evictions and a ban on investment funds buying homes [2].

The ordinary point is that rent is the largest single invoice in many households' month, and it rarely shows up in the energy headlines. A fuel price can fall with a ceasefire. A rent falls only when somebody builds something. Both of Spain's main parties now promise more homes [2]; the voter's job on 29 November is to ask how many, where and by when.
