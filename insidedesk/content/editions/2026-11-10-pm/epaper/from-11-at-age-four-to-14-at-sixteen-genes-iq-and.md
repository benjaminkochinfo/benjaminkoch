A study in Nature Human Behaviour, led by Daniel Malawsky and Hilary Martin of the Wellcome Sanger Institute, followed 6,495 children from the Avon cohort in Bristol with IQ tests at ages 4, 8 and 16, PsyPost reports [1]. It also generated new exome data for 16,103 children and 10,140 parents [1].

**Two directions.** Polygenic scores built from common variants explained about 11% of IQ variation at age 4 and 14% by 16 [1]. Rare gene-disrupting mutations went the other way: their negative association with IQ was strongest in early childhood and weakened by adolescence [1]. Among the 5% of children with the highest rare-variant burden, about 7% had an IQ below 70 at age 8, against roughly 3% of other children [1]. Most carriers, in other words, were not severely affected.

**The small print.** At age 4, about nine in ten scores in the main analysis were statistical estimates, not measured results [1]. An independent psychologist, Liana Machado of Otago, told PsyPost the imputation left her "with some uncertainty", though she found the analysis thorough [1]. The sample was British and of European ancestry, and the scores "cannot predict the cognitive development of an individual child" [1].

**For you.** One possible explanation the authors offer is that early delays partly reflect health problems that improve with care [1]. That is a reason for patience with a slow four-year-old, and for good paediatric services.
