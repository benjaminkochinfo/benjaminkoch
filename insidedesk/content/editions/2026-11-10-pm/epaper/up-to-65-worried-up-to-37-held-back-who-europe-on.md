WHO/Europe released a report on Tuesday, timed to the climate summit in Antalya, on what countries are doing about the mental-health side of climate change [1]. It reviews 20 interventions across 15 countries in the region, mostly support after floods and work on climate-related distress [1].

The headline numbers come from an earlier survey in The Lancet Planetary Health: up to 65% of young people in Finland, France, Portugal and the United Kingdom said they worried about climate change, and up to 37% reported effects on their daily functioning [1].

The most sensible sentence is a careful one. "These feelings are not, in themselves evidence of a mental health disorder," said Ledia Lazeri, WHO/Europe's regional adviser for mental health [1]. Support matters when distress becomes overwhelming or leaves people feeling powerless [1].

The examples are practical rather than grand. After Slovenia's 2023 floods, mobile mental-health teams, hotlines and community activities supported thousands of households, and kept going after the clean-up [1]. An Estonian-led project trained youth workers and produced self-help workbooks for 16- to 25-year-olds, without treating climate anxiety as an illness [1].

The report is also honest about what it does not know: responses are fragmented, with gaps in evidence and evaluation [1]. For you, that is the right order of things. Write down what was tried, measure whether it helped, then build it into emergency plans [1].
