**The podium.** Governor Christopher Waller spent most of his London speech on Tuesday not on rates but on churn. "Does a stable labor market necessarily mean it's a healthy labor market?" he asked [1]. His image was a hospital: if a doctor says your mother is stable, you are relieved, but you would not call her healthy [1]. He described a "low hire, low fire" market, with the 6- and 12-month average payroll gains closer to 80,000 a month, and a share of unemployed looking for work for more than 27 weeks that has risen from around 18% at the end of 2022 to more than 27% through September 2026 [1]. He said plainly that he does not have the answers [1].

**The print.** Later the same day the Bureau of Labor Statistics published a release that rarely gets a headline. Real average hourly earnings for all employees were unchanged from September to October: hourly pay rose 0.4%, and so did the consumer price index [2]. Real weekly earnings were also unchanged [2]. For production and nonsupervisory employees, real hourly pay rose 0.1%, but real weekly earnings fell 0.3% because the average workweek shrank 0.3% [2]. The usual year-on-year comparison is missing, for the same reason as in the CPI: no October 2025 price data were collected during the shutdown [2].

**The gap.** The governor's question and the bureau's table meet at one line. A labour market can hold its employment level and still leave the person in it treading water. Desk arithmetic: a 0.4% raise minus 0.4% inflation is zero; a slightly better hourly rate times a shorter week is a smaller pay packet. That is what "stable but not healthy" looks like on a payslip.

**For you.** The constructive point is that both halves exist in public: a governor willing to say he is unsure, and a statistical office that prints the uncomfortable number on the same morning as the famous one. If you negotiate pay, compare your raise with the month's prices, not with the headlines. The next reading comes on 10 December [2]. Nothing here is investment advice.
