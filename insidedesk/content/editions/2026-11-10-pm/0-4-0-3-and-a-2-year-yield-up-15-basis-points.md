The morning edition warned you that October's consumer price index would arrive with a hole where its headline usually sits. It did. At 14:30 CET the Bureau of Labor Statistics published the table, and in the column for twelve-month changes most cells simply read "-" [1]. No October 2025 survey was collected during last year's lapse in appropriations, so for most series there is nothing to compare against [1]. Gasoline is one of the exceptions: up 37.4% on the year, unadjusted [1].

What the bureau could publish was the month, and the month was firm. All items rose 0.4% on a seasonally adjusted basis after 0.6% in September [1]. Prices excluding food and energy rose 0.3%, "as it did in September and August" [1]. Economists surveyed by Dow Jones had looked for 0.3% and 0.2% respectively [2]; Reuters' poll had the same 0.3% for the headline and 0.2% for core [3]. One tenth of a point on each, which sounds small until you remember what a tenth compounds into.

[[chart:us-cpi-monthly-10pm]]

**Where the tenth came from.** Shelter rose 0.3% and accounted for over thirty per cent of the monthly increase [1]. Food rose 0.5%, with beef up 0.9% and snacks up 1.0% [1]. Energy rose 0.7%, far slower than September's 5.2%, with electricity up 0.9% and piped gas down 0.8% [1]. Transportation services rose 0.8%; motor vehicle insurance rose 0.7%; hospital services rose 0.8% [1]. Used cars and trucks fell 1.2% [1]. That is the useful part of the release: not one shock, but many small invoices moving the same way. Before seasonal adjustment, gasoline actually fell 0.7% in October; the adjusted index still shows a 1.0% rise [1]. Both are true, and the bureau prints both.

**How the market read it.** Within the hour, the US two-year yield, the maturity most sensitive to the Fed, rose 15 basis points to 4.69%, and the ten-year rose 9 basis points to 4.96%, according to Reuters [3]. The Nasdaq fell 1% and the S&P 500 0.6%; the dollar index rose 0.3% to 102.01; gold fell 2% to $4,057 an ounce [3]. CME FedWatch went to a fully priced quarter-point hike at the 8–9 December meeting, with odds of a half-point move at 13.6%, from around zero before the release, CNBC reported [2]. LSEG data showed traders fully pricing two 25-basis-point rises by March [4].

The speech had come first. In London on Tuesday morning, Fed Governor Christopher Waller said he would back a 25-basis-point rise in December even if October improved, and added that if core inflation came in "around 0.3 percent or even higher", a 50-basis-point hike "should be considered", since hiking only 25 would "put our credibility at risk" [5]. It came in at 0.3% [1]. Desk arithmetic: by the governor's own test, the condition for discussing the bigger step has been met; whether the committee takes it is a decision for 9 December, not for a Tuesday afternoon.

**Europe kept most of its morning.** The day had started with relief: Brent fell more than 3% and dipped below $90 a barrel for the first time since late August, and the STOXX 600 traded within 1% of its record [4]. After the print, Reuters had the STOXX 600 up 0.6%, off its session highs [4]. The DAX, which closed Monday at 26,267.19, touched 26,633.78 during the day; the latest quote this desk fetched before writing was 26,473.63, up 0.81% [6]. A settled close was not yet available at the time of writing.

**What this means for you.** Resist the two loud readings. One says the missing annual figure makes the report meaningless; it does not, because a third 0.3% core month is a pattern, not a rounding error [1]. The other says a hot print is a crisis; it is not, because the machinery worked exactly as designed: data at 14:30, yields repriced within the hour, a committee meeting in four weeks with the evidence on the table. If you carry a dollar loan or a variable rate tied to US policy, the number that moved today is the two-year, not the index. If your savings are in euros, the more useful line is that oil fell further on the same day [4].

The next instalments are already scheduled: producer prices on Friday 13 November at 14:30 CET [7], and November's CPI on 10 December, the day after the Fed decides [1]. Between them sits a holiday, a meeting and a great many invoices. Nothing here is investment advice.
