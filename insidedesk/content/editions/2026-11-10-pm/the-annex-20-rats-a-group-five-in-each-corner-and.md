The Annex reads the method behind the headline. Today's: a University of Cincinnati press release saying a stress-hormone pattern "may signal vulnerability" to PTSD in females, and could help explain why women are diagnosed at roughly twice the rate of men [1]. The paper, by Emily Devine, James Herman and colleagues, appeared in Frontiers in Cellular Neuroscience [2].

**The design.** Young adult Sprague-Dawley rats were first given a mild stressor, six minutes in a light-dark box, and their blood hormone corticosterone was measured at 15, 30, 60 and 120 minutes [2]. Three weeks later half of them went through "single prolonged stress": two hours of restraint, a 20-minute group swim and ether to unconsciousness, a standard rodent model of trauma [2]. Then came anxiety and fear tests, including how well the animals "unlearned" a fear after the tone stopped predicting a shock [2]. There were 20 animals per group [2].

**The result.** In females, those with the strongest hormone response to the mild stressor showed more anxiety-like behaviour, worse fear extinction and stronger return of fear after trauma [2]. Low resting hormone plus a high spike predicted the worst outcomes [1][2]. In males, stress reactivity did not predict outcomes in the same way [1].

**The footnotes that matter.** The headline comparison used only the top and bottom quarters of responders: five animals per group [2]. In the correlation analysis, "none survived" correction for multiple testing, which the authors put down to many variables and a small sample; they then used permutation tests to check the direction of the effects [2]. Males and females were run in separate cohorts three months apart, so, the authors write, "we can only observe differences in stress reactivity within each sex" [2]. Oestrous cycle was not tracked [2]. And the findings are "largely correlational" [2].

**What the authors themselves say.** Herman calls the hormone pattern a candidate biomarker, "not a diagnostic test", and says the work is preclinical [1]. The press release's own FAQ answers "Does this mean there is a blood test for PTSD?" with "Not yet" [1].

**For you.** This is careful, honest work, and its honesty is in the small print. The useful lesson is not about rats. It is that recovery from fear is a learning process, and learning can be helped. That is why trauma therapies exist, and why early, ordinary support after a bad event is worth giving.
