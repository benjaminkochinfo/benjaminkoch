On Sundays this desk puts the week back in order. Not by size of headline, but in the order in which prices moved, because the order tells you which worry was driving and which was being dragged along.

**1. The chips went first.** On Thursday the Philadelphia semiconductor index fell almost 7% and the Nasdaq 2.6%, its worst day since June [1]. The worry was not demand. It was the cost of borrowing to meet it: data centres are increasingly paid for with debt, and debt has a price [1].

**2. The bonds caught them.** The same day investors ran to US government bonds, giving Treasuries their biggest rally in more than three years [1]. Almost a full quarter-point of Fed rate rises for next year was priced out during the week [1]. When one half of a balanced portfolio falls and the other rises, that is not luck. That is the design.

**3. Oil rose third, and hardest.** On Friday Brent settled at $116.72 a barrel, up $3.55 on the day and 6.24% on the week, its highest settlement since 29 April [2][3]. Dow Jones Market Data counts five weekly gains in a row and a rise of 12.74% so far this month [3]. WTI settled at $103.27 [2]. Reuters tied Friday's move to Houthi attacks in Saudi Arabia and an oil spill off the UAE [2].

**4. Everyone else paid fourth.** On Friday the Dow fell 652.82 points, or 1.23%, to 52,215.16; the S&P 500 lost 0.52% to 7,623.68; the Nasdaq slipped 0.21% [4]. Technology steadied, rising 0.35%, while consumer staples, consumer discretionary and financials each fell more than 1% [4]. American Express fell more than 3% after announcing it would cut about 5% of its global workforce, citing the need to "prepare for AI" [5]. On the week, the S&P 500 lost 0.5% and the Nasdaq 0.7%, while the Dow gained 0.4%, its third weekly gain in a row [4].

**What the order says.** The week began as a technology story and ended as an oil story. By Friday the selling had moved from the companies that build the future to the companies that sell groceries and lend money today, which is what a higher fuel bill does: it taxes everything a little. "There is still fallout from the questioning of the AI trade," Kristina Hooper of Man Group told Reuters [4]. True, and by Friday it was no longer the main fallout.

**What it does not say.** It does not say a crash has begun. A half-per-cent weekly loss after a seven-per-cent day in chips is a market absorbing news, not panicking about it. The harder test arrives this week, when about a third of S&P 500 companies report, among them Alphabet, Microsoft and Amazon [4]. Their capital-spending forecasts are the invoice the whole AI trade is waiting to read.

**For you.** If you hold a broad fund, this was a week in which it did what it says on the tin. Nothing here is advice.
