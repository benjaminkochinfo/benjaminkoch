**The podium, in three voices.** On Friday Tom Barrack, the US ambassador to Türkiye, was asked whether there could be a US strike on Iran this weekend. "Absolutely," he said, while adding that President Trump remained open to a diplomatic agreement and that efforts involving Pakistan and Qatar were under way [1]. Asked about it after landing in Florida, the president said: "If that's the case, I don't know anything about it" [1]. On Saturday Iran's ambassador to Pakistan, Reza Amiri Moghadam, said Tehran remained open to talks under the interim deal Pakistan brokered in June, and that it had told Washington through Pakistan it was prepared to reopen the Strait of Hormuz if the US lifted its naval blockade [3].

Then the fourth voice. Iran's armed-forces chief, Major General Ali Abdollahi, said Iran would "under no circumstances and at no time" allow US military vessels into the Persian Gulf [1]. The IRGC's navy claimed that two supertankers carrying more than 4.5 million barrels between them had struck mines on what it called an unauthorised route; the claim could not be independently verified [1].

**The print.** The oil market voted on Friday afternoon, New York time, before Saturday's statements were made. Brent settled at $116.72, up 6.24% on the week, the largest weekly gain since the week ending 11 September and the fifth weekly rise in a row [2]. It was the highest settlement since 29 April, and just 1.38% below this year's settlement high of $118.35 on 31 March [2]. Al Jazeera put the week's move down to continued attacks on ships in and around the strait together with "the increased odds of renewed war" [4].

**The gap.** The podiums disagree with each other. An ambassador says yes, his president says he does not know, an envoy says talks, a general says never. You cannot average those into a forecast, and this desk will not try. What you can do is notice that the print does not need to know which of them is right. It prices the probability that any of them is, and it does so in dollars.

**What the print does not tell you.** A futures price is a bet on the next few weeks, not a measure of how much oil actually moves. The physical picture comes from ship counts and port data, and those are patchy in a war: some tankers cross with their transponders off. The $116.72 says the market is paying for risk. It does not say how much oil is missing.

**Verdict.** The podium offered four positions in two days. The print offered one number, settled and published, with a weekly change you can check. On a weekend of strike talk and talks talk, the dull rule holds: when officials disagree, read the price they are all trying to move. Nothing here is advice.
