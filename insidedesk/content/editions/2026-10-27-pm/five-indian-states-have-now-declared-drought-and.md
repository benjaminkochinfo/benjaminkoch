India's drought map grew on Tuesday. Rajasthan has declared drought in 80 tehsils (sub-districts) across 14 districts, and Gujarat in four districts: Kutch, Devbhumi Dwarka, Jamnagar and Porbandar [1]. They follow Karnataka, Maharashtra and Andhra Pradesh [1]. Karnataka's government says its latest estimates put 80% of the kharif crop, the monsoon-season harvest, as destroyed [1].

In Rajasthan, Jodhpur has the most drought-hit tehsils at 12, followed by Barmer and Jalore with 10 each [1]. Of the 80, 69 are classed as severely and 11 as moderately hit [1]. Bajra, jowar, moong, moth beans and sesame have been lost, and Jodhpur's water storage stood at 15.15% on 16 October [1].

[[chart:jodhpur-rain-27pm]]

The season total alone would not tell you this. A modelled grid point over Jodhpur shows 257.1 mm of rain since 1 June, against a 1991–2020 average of 277.5 mm for the same days [2]. Desk arithmetic: about 7% short, hardly a catastrophe on paper. The shape is the story. By 15 August the grid point was ahead of normal at 231.6 mm; from 16 August to 26 October it recorded 25.5 mm, against an average of 80.1 mm [2]. Desk arithmetic: about a third of the usual late-season rain. One grid point is not a district, and model grids smooth out local storms, so read it as a shape, not a measurement.

What a declaration buys is dull and useful. Rajasthan has announced cattle camps in affected areas and a six-month suspension of farm-loan recovery [1]. Gujarat says it has been selling fodder at cheaper rates since July [1]. None of that makes rain. It does stop a bad season from turning into a forced sale of the herd or a defaulted loan, which is how one dry autumn becomes three poor years.

If you buy Indian rice, pulses or spices, nothing in Tuesday's reports puts a number on prices, and the desk will not invent one. The quieter point is for anyone who thinks of climate as an averages problem: farms live on timing, and a month's rain arriving early is not the same as a month's rain.
