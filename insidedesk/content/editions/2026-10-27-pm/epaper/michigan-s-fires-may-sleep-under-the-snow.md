Wildfire season in Michigan usually means spring, after the snow melts and before the leaves come out [1]. This year it is late October, and the worry is the coming spring too.

At least three large wildfires have been burning in the state's Upper Peninsula since early October, covering about 1,400 acres, and they remain only partially contained [1]. Desk arithmetic: about 570 hectares. Lingering drought has let embers burn several feet down into the duff, the layers of decaying plant matter on the forest floor [1]. Officials fear the fires could smoulder through winter and flare up again in spring, like the "zombie fires" of Alaska's and Canada's peatlands [1].

"At this point in time, I wouldn't count it out," said Keith Murphy, the state natural-resources department's protection manager for the region [1]. "Usually, we have the droughty August and we run into deep burning, but it's just continued on through October now," he said [1]. Only saturated soil will finish the job, and Murphy said heavy, wet snow would do more than light, fluffy lake-effect snow [1].

There is precedent, and it is not frightening. A peat fire in Allegan County smouldered for nearly a year before it was put out in autumn 2013, and the 1976 Seney fire, which burned eight feet into peat, was finally put out by melting snow the next spring [1].

The useful part is the plan: officials will monitor how rain and snow affect the fire sites in the coming months [1]. Fire crews who keep watching a fire nobody can see are the adaptation story.
