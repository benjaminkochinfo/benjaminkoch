Speeches at international meetings are usually skipped, and usually rightly. Tuesday's opening address at WHO Europe's Regional Committee in Copenhagen is worth reading for its numbers, not its rhetoric [1]. Corinne Capuano, the officer-in-charge of the regional office, delivered it while Regional Director Hans Kluge is on special leave, after what she called "a year of hard change", with staff leaving in a reorganisation [1].

Start with the dull machinery. Between January and October, the region's teams analysed 48,000 pieces of information on possible health threats; 35 needed an operational response [1]. Desk arithmetic: about one in 1,400, and 47,965 that did not need one. This year's responses included a hantavirus outbreak on a cruise ship in May, dengue and West Nile virus in the summer, and the first documented human infection with Shamonda virus [1]. For the first time, every state in the region filed its annual report under the International Health Regulations, and the region now has 38 classified emergency medical teams [1]. A sorting office that reads every letter and acts on a few is doing its job.

The routine wins are larger than they sound. Across the region, 91% of children get all three doses of the diphtheria, tetanus and whooping-cough vaccine, measles cases fell by 75% in 2025 compared with 2024, and 92% of countries have introduced HPV vaccination [1]. Latvia restricted alcohol availability and alcohol-related deaths fell significantly within five months, she said [1].

The gaps were stated just as plainly. One in five tuberculosis cases goes undiagnosed or unreported, half of HIV diagnoses come too late, and HIV deaths are rising [1]. WHO Europe's analysis counts 1.8 million deaths among people under 75 that prevention or treatment could avoid [1]. A quarter of children aged 7 to 9 are overweight or living with obesity, based on data from 37 countries [1].

The workforce line deserves the most attention. Women make up 77% of the health workforce yet earn on average up to 28% less per month than men, and nursing and carer shortages hit hardest in rural areas and smaller countries [1]. Women in the region also spend 81.3% of their lives in good health against 83.5% for men, which means more demand for long-term care from a workforce already short of people [1]. If you want to know where Europe's health systems will strain, you do not need a forecast. You need the payroll.
