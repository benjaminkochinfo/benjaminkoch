By 16:00 local time, 15:00 in Berlin, 55.7% of Israel's eligible voters had cast a ballot, just over four million people [1]. The Central Elections Committee said that was the highest figure at that hour on its books; at the same point in 2022 it was 47.5% [1]. The records had been falling all day: 31.5% by noon, the highest at that hour in 53 years, and 44.9% by 14:00, about 3.29 million ballots [2].

Desk arithmetic: 55.7 minus 47.5 is 8.2 percentage points ahead of 2022 at the same hour, and the gap widened at every reading the committee published [3].

**One figure, two fears.** Watch what the campaigns did with that number. Benjamin Netanyahu told right-wing voters to "race to the polls", warned of high turnout in Arab and left-wing areas and claimed "they are trying to steal the election from us", pointing to three Ra'am activists arrested on suspicion of bribing voters [1]. Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar answered that the prime minister was laying the groundwork to contest the result, and told its own voters the margin "must be enormous" [1]. The committee said it had found no evidence of systematic tampering and was handling individual reports as they came in [1].

This is the old behavioural loop of election days. A turnout figure is neutral; it does not say who voted. Yet each camp reads the other's queue as a threat, and the threat becomes the reason to join the queue. Fear is a cheap mobiliser. It also leaves a residue: voters primed all day to expect theft are slower to accept a count that disappoints them.

**The dull machinery did its job.** The more useful story is the committee's. Faced with lengthening lines, it changed its guidance so that polling officials could begin identifying the next voter while the current one was still behind the booth, and it made clear that anyone who arrives by 22:00 will be able to vote [2]. Not everyone had a smooth day. Haaretz and the Arab legal aid group Adalah relayed reports of waits of several hours and slow identity checks in Bedouin towns in the Negev and in Arab communities in the Galilee [1]. Those reports are now on the record, where complaints belong, rather than in a speech.

**What you will see tonight, and how to read it.** Israel has had a poll blackout since Friday night: no new poll results could be published until the stations close [3]. At 22:00 local, 21:00 CET, the television channels release exit polls, which are estimates from samples of voters, not results [3]. In 2022 the three main channels' first exit polls gave the right-religious bloc 61 or 62 seats; it won 64 [3]. Meretz was given four or five seats by all three and finished below the threshold with none [3]. Soldiers' and other double-envelope ballots are counted later at the Knesset, and official results are due by 4 November [3].

Why does that matter? Because the first number sticks. In a Brennan Center experiment with 1,994 US registered voters, showing a candidate with 84% odds on a betting market raised the share who thought that candidate would win by 20 percentage points, whatever the polls said [4]. And a warning to be cautious about the odds made no difference to how much people relied on them [4]. Scepticism, in other words, is not a shield. A habit is.

The habit is boring and it works: treat tonight's 21:00 figures as a forecast for a count that has not happened yet, and notice when a politician tells you the result before the committee does.

And give the queue its due. People do not stand for hours to post a ballot into a system they think is rigged. Whatever the speeches said today, four million votes by mid-afternoon are four million small acts of trust in an envelope, a ballot box and a count. That trust is the institution's best asset. The way to keep it is to let the slow part, the counting, finish.
