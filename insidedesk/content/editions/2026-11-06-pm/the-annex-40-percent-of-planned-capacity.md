About 40% of planned US data-centre capacity is in states where governors or governors-elect have pledged or already imposed a pause on new projects, according to market intelligence firm Cleanview as reported by Euronews [1]. That number sat under Friday's equity narrative alongside Thursday's local scoreboard: voters rejected or restricted data-centre projects in 40 of 41 ballot measures counted around the midterms [2].

**What the figure is not.** It is not a federal ban. It is not even, in every case, a signed order. A pledge on the campaign trail and a pause already in force are different legal objects, and Cleanview's share mixes them [1]. Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez proposed a federal moratorium earlier this year; a proposal is still a proposal [1].

**What it does to attention.** Local ballots are concrete: a megawatt figure, a water permit, a town hall. National policy is abstract until a committee marks up a bill. Behavioural research on availability bias is useful here without being mystical: the vivid local "no" is easier to retrieve than the distant legislative calendar, so it weighs more in a Friday morning sell-off than a careful reading of statehouse procedure would justify. Markets can over-weight the vivid cue and still be right that politics has shifted; the error is treating a pledge share as a capacity haircut already delivered.

For you, the Annex's habit is small. When a percentage like "40%" arrives attached to a scare, ask whether it measures law, pledge, or plan. The answer changes what the number can do.
