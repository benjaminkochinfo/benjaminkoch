Most of England and Wales are still in drought even after Storms Austen and Boelo, Regius Professor Hannah Cloke of the University of Reading wrote on Friday [1]. Parts of Cornwall, Devon, Dorset, Somerset and Sussex sit in severe drought — the Environment Agency's highest level — after the driest April-to-September in southern and south-eastern England in more than 100 years [1]. Wimbleball reservoir on Exmoor, which supplies around 400,000 people, fell from full in March to about 13% by October [1].

**Why a wet week is not enough.** Winter is when the UK refills: shorter, colder days mean less evaporation and less plant uptake, so rain can soak into groundwater [1]. The Environment Agency estimates England needs about 120% of normal rainfall between September and April for every area to leave drought [1]. Desk arithmetic: that is half a year of consistently wetter-than-normal weather, not two named storms. Heavy bursts overwhelm drains and floodplains quickly while reservoirs and chalk aquifers respond over months [1]. This is already England's third drought in five years [1].

**Europe's split map.** Copernicus's October bulletin on precipitation, soil moisture and river flow shows the broader continental contrast: dryness lingering in parts of central and eastern Europe while western and Mediterranean zones take storm hits [2]. COP31 opens in Antalya on 9 November with adaptation finance still the unresolved bill [prior desk]. For you in England, the calm habit is dull and useful: a water butt, a meter conversation with your supplier, shorter showers — and patience with a ledger that fills slowly [1].
