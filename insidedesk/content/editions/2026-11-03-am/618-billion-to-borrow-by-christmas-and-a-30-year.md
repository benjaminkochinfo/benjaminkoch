While the campaigns made their last speeches, the US Treasury published a table. It expects to borrow $618 billion in privately held net marketable debt in October to December, assuming it ends December with $850 billion in cash [1]. That is $10 billion less than it estimated in August [1]. For January to March it expects $800 billion [1].

**The smaller number is not quite a saving.** Treasury says the lower estimate comes mainly from starting the quarter with more cash than assumed: $984 billion instead of $950 billion [1]. Leave that out, and this quarter's borrowing estimate is $24 billion higher than in August [1]. Desk arithmetic: $618 billion plus $800 billion is $1.418 trillion of net new borrowing from the public in six months.

[[chart:ust-long-end-03nov]]

**The price of that borrowing has eased.** The 10-year Treasury yield closed at 4.91% on Monday and the 30-year at 5.28%, according to Treasury's daily par yield curve [2]. Both were far higher in early October: 5.31% for the 10-year on 5 October and 5.67% for the 30-year on 7 October [2]. The 30-year is now one hundredth of a point above its 5.27% of 1 September [2]. The short end moved the other way on Monday: the 3-month bill yield rose to 4.21% from 4.16% [2].

**Why this matters to you.** The 10-year yield is a reference rate for mortgages, company loans and, eventually, the interest on your savings. A government that borrows $1.4 trillion in two quarters is not unusual in the US; that the auctions are planned, published and held on time is the machinery that keeps the price orderly. The detail that counts comes on Wednesday afternoon our time, when Treasury says what it will sell, and in which maturities [1]. Nothing here is investment advice.
