Hospitals in Germany lost a line on their invoices this weekend. The one-year "Sofort-Transformationskosten" surcharge of 3.25% on hospital bills expired on 31 October 2026 [1]. It cost €4 billion and was paid out of the federal special fund (Sondervermögen) [1]. The government is planning a new surcharge, worth €450 million a year [1]. Desk arithmetic: that is about one-ninth of the old one.

The German Hospital Institute (DKI) asked clinics what that means for them. In a snap survey of 267 general hospitals between 22 and 27 October, 76% said they expect a very heavy financial burden; among hospitals in thinly populated rural districts the figure was 85% [1]. Seventy per cent do not think they can make up the loss on their own, and 26% think they can partly [1].

**What they say they will cut.**
- Hiring freezes or leaving posts empty: 76% [1].
- Postponing or cancelling investment: 65% [1].
- Reducing staff: one in two [1].
- Restricting or dropping services: about one in three [1].
- Many also plan to cut spending on materials [1].

Read the survey for what it is. The DKI says it is not representative, and an expiring payment is exactly the moment when a sector's own institute asks its members how much it hurts [1]. Intentions in a survey are not decisions in a board meeting. But the direction is plain, and it falls hardest outside the cities, where the nearest alternative hospital is furthest away.

The constructive part is that this is now a question of budgets and dates, not of slogans. A surcharge is a number in a law; so is its replacement. The €450 million plan can be argued over line by line, and the gap between it and €4 billion is the honest size of the argument [1]. If your local hospital matters to you, the place to watch is not the speech about hospital reform but the next set of board minutes and the district council budget.
