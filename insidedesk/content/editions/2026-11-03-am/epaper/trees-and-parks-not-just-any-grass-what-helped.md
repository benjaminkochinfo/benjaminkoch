If you have ever come back from a walk in the woods able to think again, psychologists have a name for it: attention restoration theory. A team at Washington University in St. Louis has tested it on children, and the result is published in Environment International [1].

They used the ABCD study, which follows roughly 12,000 American children with a median age of 10 [1]. Their green-space measure was the average share of tree canopy and park cover in each child's census tract, so a verge of mown grass counts for little [1]. The analysis controlled for income, ADHD diagnosis, social-media use and how safe children felt [1].

"The greater the proximity to green space, the better kids did on objective assessments of their attention performance," said Carina Fowler, the lead author [1]. Achievement scores followed the same pattern [1].

What might explain it? The team found a link with air quality, but not with physical activity [1]. "There is something to the idea that air quality is partially explaining why green space is helping our attention," Fowler said [1]. Fowler adds that proponents of the theory say you have to feel safe in the space, and that the effect is weaker if you are on social media while you are in it [1].

It is an observational study, so it shows an association, not proof that a park causes better concentration. But it adds weight to some unglamorous parts of a council budget: tree planting, park upkeep and clean air. They may belong in the same conversation as school budgets.
