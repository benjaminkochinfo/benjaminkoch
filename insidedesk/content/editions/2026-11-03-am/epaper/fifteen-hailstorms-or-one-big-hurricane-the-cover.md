The weather that costs insurers most is no longer the weather their protection is designed for. That is the argument of a new report from Marsh Re, the reinsurance arm of the broker Marsh [1]. Hail, wildfire, winter storms and floods, the "secondary perils", have become a major part of insured catastrophe claims in North America, it says, while many reinsurance programmes still mainly protect against the rare, very large disaster [1].

The numbers it cites are plain. US insured losses from severe convective storms have stayed above $30 billion a year since 2020, and four of those years topped $40 billion [1]. Its comparison is starker still: 15 medium-sized hailstorms could hit an insurer's annual earnings harder than a hurricane expected once in 100 years [1]. Since the market reset of 2023, insurers have also had to keep a bigger share of each loss before their reinsurance pays out [1].

Marsh Re's remedies sound like bookkeeping: check the models against your own claims history, record roof age and construction, and buy cover that responds to the sum of many events, such as annual aggregate or second-event layers [1]. That kind of cover is still hard to buy at acceptable prices, it says [1]. Prices for the big-event cover are still falling, if more slowly: RenaissanceRe's underwriting chief expects property catastrophe rate cuts at the 1 January renewals to be smaller than this year's mid-teens [2].

For you, the practical end of this is the roof survey and the excess on your home policy. The losses come from hail and storms that arrive every year, not just from the rare great one.
