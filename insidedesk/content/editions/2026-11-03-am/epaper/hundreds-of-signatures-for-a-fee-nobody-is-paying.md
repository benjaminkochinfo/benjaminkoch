Some of the most consequential trade policy is a fee that is not being charged. The US Section 301 port fees aimed at China-linked vessels, born of a probe into China's targeting of the maritime, logistics and shipbuilding sectors, are suspended until 9 January 2027 after the Trump–Xi summit [1].

A coalition of hundreds of stakeholders has now asked for more than a delay. In a 30 October letter to US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, they welcomed the two-month extension and asked the administration to "permanently suspend the fees to provide certainty to the global supply chain" [1]. New fees, they wrote, "would create immediate and far-reaching consequences" for US firms that ship by sea [1].

The signatures are worth reading as a list of who carries the risk: the World Shipping Council, the International Chamber of Shipping, the US Chamber of Commerce, the National Association of Manufacturers, the National Retail Federation, the American Farm Bureau Federation and the American Association of Port Authorities [1]. Importers and exporters, farmers and port operators rarely sign the same page.

Their complaint is a ledger, not a slogan: higher costs for ocean freight, trucking, warehousing, insurance and inventory, shifting carrier capacity, equipment shortages and congested inland networks [1]. The letter also says strengthening US maritime capacity is "an important national objective" [1]. That is the constructive part. Nobody is arguing against shipyards; they are arguing about who pays for them, and when.

For you, the date to note is 9 January. A suspension that lapses is a fee that returns, and carriers price what they expect.
