Every Monday from April to the end of November, the US Department of Agriculture publishes a few pages on how far the country's farms have got. Last autumn, the pages stopped: because of a lapse in federal funding, the reports for the weeks ending 5 October to 9 November 2025 were cancelled, and this year's sheet still says so in a special note at the top [1]. So the "previous year" column reads "NA", and the five-year averages fill the 2025 gap with imputed estimates [1]. A missing number is a cost too, and farmers, grain buyers and insurers paid it last year.

This year's report for the week to 1 November arrived on schedule on Monday evening [1]. Corn was 74% harvested across the 18 main states, up from 60% a week earlier and behind the 2021–2025 average of 77% [1]. Soybeans were 88% in, ahead of the 84% average [1]. Cotton is ahead, sorghum and peanuts behind [1].

[[chart:us-harvest-progress-03nov]]

The state lines show where the gap is. Iowa's corn is 66% harvested against an average of 79%; Nebraska's 66% against 75%; South Dakota's 63% against 75% [1]. Illinois, at 86%, is ahead of its 83% average [1]. Sorghum is furthest behind: 64% harvested against an average of 78%, with Kansas at 49% against 70% and Nebraska at 42% against 71% [1].

The weather part is in the soil tables. Across the 48 states, 38% of topsoil was rated short or very short of moisture, the same as a week earlier, and only 6% had a surplus [1]. Next year's crop is already in the ground: winter wheat is 83% planted, against an 84% average, and 65% has emerged [1]. In its first condition rating of the season, 44% of that wheat was rated good or excellent and 18% poor or very poor; in Texas the poor and very poor share is 34%, and in Oklahoma 25% [1]. Desk arithmetic: the shares add USDA's separate categories, very short plus short for soil, good plus excellent and poor plus very poor for the wheat.

None of this is a verdict on prices or yields, and the report offers none. It is a weekly survey of about 3,600 people whose work brings them into contact with farmers in their counties, weighted by acreage and run from early April to the end of November [1]. The constructive point is the dull one: the counting happened, it was published on time, and you can read it state by state.
