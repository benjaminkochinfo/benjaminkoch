Three researchers in Turkey ran a randomised experiment with 462 working adults who had used generative AI in the previous three months [1]. Each made six judgements about which of two countries had the larger population, then saw an AI recommend the other one [1].

Half saw a neutral message. The other half saw a package that acknowledged their view, called their first answer understandable, personalised the wording and was somewhat longer [1].

**Result.** When participants had been right and the AI was therefore wrong, 24.2% switched in the neutral condition and 32.3% with the warm package [1]. When they had been wrong and the AI was right, switching rose from 26.3% to 39.4% [1]. The warm framing raised the odds of following bad advice by about half (odds ratio 1.507) and good advice by about four-fifths (1.800); the difference between those two effects was not statistically reliable [1].

**The honest limits.** The authors stress that the AI's correctness depended on each person's first answer rather than being randomised, so the study cannot say whether warmth makes people better or worse at telling good advice from bad [1]. The warm package also bundled several features at once [1]. People with higher self-reported AI literacy reported checking more and suspending judgement less; that is an association, not a cure [1].

The plain reading: a friendly tone made the machine more persuasive in both directions. Persuasive is not the same as reliable, and the only thing that separates them is checking.
