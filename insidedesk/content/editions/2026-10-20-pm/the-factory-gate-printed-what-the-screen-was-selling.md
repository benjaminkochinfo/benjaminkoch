Tuesday had two kinds of market news, and only one of them made a noise.

**The noise.** European shares fell for a second day. Reuters reported the STOXX 600 down 0.7% in the afternoon after a fall of close to 1% on Monday, with chipmakers including ASML and ASMI down almost 3% and French stocks off almost 1% [4]. By the close the index stood at 634.78, 0.75% lower on the day [5]. The explanations were the familiar ones: Brent at a five-month high above $111, nervousness about whether money poured into artificial intelligence will earn its cost, and French bond yields after a weekend of riots [4]. All of it is true. None of it is a number you will pay directly.

**The receipt.** At 08:00 the Federal Statistical Office in Wiesbaden published something you will pay, eventually. Producer prices for industrial goods sold in Germany were 6.7% higher in September than a year earlier, and 1.9% higher than in August [1]. It was the steepest annual rise since March 2023, when the rate was 7.5% [1]. Economists polled beforehand had expected 6.2% [3]. That is half a point of surprise in a single month, at the factory gate, before anyone has printed a price tag.

**Where the heat is.** Read the release for one paragraph more and the picture becomes narrower, and calmer. Energy was 14.9% dearer than a year ago and 5.8% dearer than in August [1]. Mineral-oil products were up 51.3% on the year, light heating oil 91.8%, motor fuels 48.6% [1]. Strip energy out and producer prices rose 3.2% on the year and just 0.1% on the month [1]. Consumer goods, the things that end up in a shopping basket, were 1.8% cheaper than in September 2025 [1].

So the shock is real and it is specific. It sits in barrels, tonnes of gasoil and the invoices of anyone who heats with oil or drives for a living. It has not yet spread through everything else.

**The sequence, from the table.** The statistics office's own series shows how the year turned. In February producer prices were 3.3% lower than a year earlier [2]. They crossed zero in April at +1.7%, reached 3.0% in July, 4.6% in August and 6.7% in September [2]. Energy went from −12.5% in February to +14.9% in September [2]. That is not a gentle drift. It is the war in Iran and the Gulf arriving in German factory accounts, month by month, which is exactly what Destatis says it is [1].

**Why the dull number beats the loud one.** A falling share price tells you that someone, somewhere, changed their mind about the future. It may change back by Friday; on Tuesday Nasdaq futures slipped 0.7%, a day after finding some support [4]. A producer price is different. It is what a buyer paid on the 15th of the month for a real delivery, recorded on a fixed survey day under published methods [1]. It does not reverse because sentiment improves. It feeds through, with a lag, into the consumer-price index, which already showed fuels up 37.1% and heating oil up 74.9% on the year in September [6].

There is a constructive reading here, and it is not wishful. The consumer-goods line is still negative and the ex-energy monthly change is a tenth of a point [1]. Inflation that stays inside the energy bill is painful but bounded. The thing worth watching is not the headline 6.7%; it is whether that 0.1% becomes 0.3%, 0.5%, then a habit [1].

**What this desk is not saying.** It is not saying the market is wrong to worry, or that prices will fall, or that you should do anything with your savings. It makes no forecast. It is saying that on a day when the screens were red, the most useful number in Europe came from a statistics office's press release, published on time, with a methods note, a base year and a survey date at the bottom [1]. That is the dull machinery working. It is worth reading before the commentary.

Nothing here is investment advice.
