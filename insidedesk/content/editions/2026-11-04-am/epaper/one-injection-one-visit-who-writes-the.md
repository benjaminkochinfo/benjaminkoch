Tuberculosis is usually cured with tablets. The trouble is the calendar: most prevention and treatment programmes rely on daily oral medicines taken over several months, and keeping that up is hard for patients and for the clinics that follow them [1]. On Tuesday the World Health Organization published target regimen profiles for long-acting injectable medicines, a list of what such products would have to achieve [1].

For prevention, the target is a complete course in a single injection, given at one visit to a health worker [1]. For active disease, WHO wants a regimen of no more than four months with three dosing visits or fewer [1]. Both profiles ask for small injection volumes, products that need no cold chain, injections that trained staff can give at the lowest feasible level of the health system, and safety and efficacy comparable to today's pills [1].

"Long-acting injectables could make TB prevention and treatment much simpler for the people who need them," said Tereza Kasaeva, who leads WHO's HIV, TB, hepatitis and STI department [1]. The profiles were drawn up with regulators, national TB programmes, people who have had TB and civil society groups, after a public consultation in April and May [1]. Several candidates are entering or approaching clinical development [1].

WHO also says work on better oral regimens should continue, so that patients keep a choice [1]. A specification is not a medicine. It is the drawing that comes before the factory, and the people who will pay for the factory now know what to build.
