Researchers at Copenhagen University Hospital gave 15 healthy adults a single strong dose of psilocybin and used PET scans to measure SV2A, a protein that serves as an indirect marker of synapses, before the dose and a week after [1]. The study, in *Translational Psychiatry*, found no statistically significant overall rise. Average changes were around 2% to 3%, and the trial stopped early after an interim analysis showed it was unlikely to reach its target [1].

The more interesting part is exploratory. The first five participants took the drug lying in an MRI scanner; the other ten took it in a dedicated room with a curated playlist, with two facilitators present in both settings [1]. The room group reported more intense mystical-type experiences and more positive lasting effects at three months, with no difference in negative effects [1]. Their SV2A rose by about 5% to 6% in the frontal cortex and hippocampus, while the scanner group showed no increase; the gap between groups was significant only in the frontal cortex [1].

The authors themselves call the setting differences "preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive" [1]. There was no placebo, the groups were not randomised, the scanner group had five people, and some authors have ties to companies developing psychedelic drugs [1]. Still, the old clinical intuition gets a small data point: the room is part of the treatment.
