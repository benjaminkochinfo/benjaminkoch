The AI boom is usually described in chips. On election day, analysts described it in Texas permits. Wells Fargo estimates that 27% of US data-centre gigawatts are in Texas alone, CNBC reports [1]. More than 80% of Texas voters say they worry about data centres' effect on water supply and electricity prices, according to a University of Houston survey of 2,000 likely voters conducted 10–14 October [1].

The state already has a pause. Governor Greg Abbott halted data-centre permitting in September, saying "Texans must come first" [1]. He sought a fourth term against the Democrat Gina Hinojosa. Evercore ISI, citing its colleague Sarah Bianchi, wrote that Abbott is likely to lift the moratorium, while Hinojosa is expected to keep it [1]. In the Senate race, the Democrat James Talarico has proposed ending some tax subsidies and tightening standards on labour, the environment and grid use, according to The Texas Tribune as cited by CNBC [1].

Not every analyst reads a Democratic advance as bad for the industry. Rob Rains of Washington Analysis expects a Democratic Congress to push harder to "fence off" Chinese competitors [1]. TD Cowen's Paul Gallant expects President Trump to veto any AI safety law that creates "material risk to the AI trade" [1].

The useful point survives whatever the count shows by the time you read this. Data centres are no longer only a technology story. They are a water story, a grid story and a ballot story, decided by permits, tariffs and voters, which is how big infrastructure has always been built.
