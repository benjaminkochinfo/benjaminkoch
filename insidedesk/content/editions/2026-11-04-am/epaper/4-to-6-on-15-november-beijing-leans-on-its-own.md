Most central banks spend their effort stopping a currency from falling. The People's Bank of China is trying to stop one rising too fast. On Tuesday it said financial institutions must hold 6% of their foreign-currency deposits as reserves from 15 November, up from 4%, to strengthen "the management of foreign exchange liquidity" [1]. Reuters calculates that this takes about $24 billion out of the system [1].

The reason is in the ledger of trade. The yuan has gained more than 5% against the dollar this year, Reuters reports, to its strongest since 2022, carried by robust exports, big trade surpluses and companies converting their dollar earnings [1]. The onshore yuan had risen for 13 days in a row, the longest run since 1996, before the announcement ended it, according to Bloomberg as cited by CNBC TV18 [2]. On the day it weakened as much as 0.37%, to 6.6728 per dollar [1]; by early Wednesday the offshore rate was 6.6782 [3].

Not everyone expects the step to bite. ANZ's Zhaopeng Xing said it would "carry greater symbolic significance", because the excess supply of dollars from exporters is larger than the ratio can absorb [1]. It also reverses the bank's September 2023 cut, made when the problem was a falling yuan [1].

For you, the dull lesson is that a currency is a queue of invoices. When exporters bring home more dollars than anyone wants, the price moves, and a central bank can slow it but rarely stop it. Nothing here is investment advice.
