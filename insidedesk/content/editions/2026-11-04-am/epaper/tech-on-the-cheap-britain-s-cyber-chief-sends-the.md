Launching the National Cyber Security Centre's annual review on Tuesday, its chief executive, Richard Horne, warned of the "real prospect of an unprecedented rise in the number of cyber incidents" in the coming months [1]. He pinned the risk on the backlog, not on clever new attacks: "vulnerabilities that have been tolerated for far too long can now be discovered and exploited at a scale that was once unimaginable" [1].

The evidence is in the patch notes. Major technology providers are now fixing hundreds of vulnerabilities in a single update, and the Linux kernel project alone patched more than a thousand last month, Horne said [1]. The NCSC judges it "highly likely" that AI-powered malware will be used in a ransomware attack on Britain's critical infrastructure by 2028 [1]. Even state-linked hacks, it says, often succeed through internet-facing applications where patches were available but were not deployed in time [1].

Horne's sharpest line was aimed at the boardroom. "For decades, we have collectively got away with doing tech on the cheap," he said, pointing to end-of-life systems kept running for "cost efficiency" [1]. The defence is mostly budget lines. A £70 million NERV Centre will use AI to look for vulnerabilities across critical infrastructure. A further £60 million goes to the first phase of Cyber Shield, a national network of AI defence agents [1]. The NCSC still believes the "structural advantage will remain with defenders", because they own the environment, but it says that advantage "is not a given" [1].

The one thing to do this week is unglamorous: list what you run that no longer gets updates, and give it a retirement date.
