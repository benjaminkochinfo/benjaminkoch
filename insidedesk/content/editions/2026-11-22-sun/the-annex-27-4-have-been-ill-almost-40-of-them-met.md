The Federal Anti-Discrimination Agency published two studies on Tuesday: a representative online survey of about 8,000 people in work, which it calls the largest of its kind so far, and a qualitative study with people affected and with works-council and company representatives [1]. The work was carried out by the Institute for Work, Skills and Training at the University of Duisburg-Essen [1].

**The base figure.** More than one in four respondents, 27.4%, said they had a mental illness now or in the past [1]. Of employees with a mental illness, almost 40% had experienced discrimination at work during their careers [1].

**The annex is in the forms it takes.** The most common was having one's performance rated worse or belittled (46.6%), followed by being treated disrespectfully or inappropriately (44.6%) [1]. About a third experienced bullying (31.7%) or found that their qualifications were not properly taken into account (31.4%) [1]. In 30% of cases colleagues or managers raised the illness without being asked [1].

**And in what follows.** More than half of those discriminated against, 52%, felt insecure and withdrew; 46.8% reported lower motivation and performance; 42.3% fell ill again and were off for a time; and 39.5% left their employer [1]. The agency's finding with most practical weight: the risk is especially high once an illness becomes known at work, and it hardly matters whether people disclosed it themselves or it came out [1]. That leaves people with a dilemma the report names plainly: openness can bring support, but also disadvantage; silence often means going without help [1]. "Nobody should have to be afraid to talk about a mental illness at work," said Ferda Ataman, the independent federal commissioner [1].

**What respondents would change.** 85% of those affected by discrimination support legal parity of chronic mental illness with a recognised disability; 81.7% say more flexibility in hours, breaks or home office would help; around 80% want more knowledge among works councils, disability representatives and occupational health management [1].

**For you.** If 39.5% of those discriminated against left, a firm that handles disclosure badly does not avoid a cost, it converts a sick-leave question into a recruitment bill. If you manage people, the cheapest protection is a dull one: a written, known procedure for what happens to a disclosure, who hears it and who does not.
