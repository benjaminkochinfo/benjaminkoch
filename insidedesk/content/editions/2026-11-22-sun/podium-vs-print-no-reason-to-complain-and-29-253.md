**The podium.** On Saturday morning, on Onda Cero, Pedro Sánchez announced what he called a "surprise" for the last cabinet meeting before Spain votes on 29 November: the tax on banks will be extended "until 2027", raising, he said, €1,400 million for housing, from protected homes to tax relief for tenants and renovation [1]. Then the line aimed at the banks themselves: "I don't think Spanish banks have any reason to complain," he said, adding that a look at their income statements shows they have made their money under his government (our translation) [1]. Within the hour he repeated the pledge in a video: "In the game, the bank always wins. But democracy is not a game" (our translation) [1].

**The print.** Spain's six largest banks earned a combined €29,253 million between January and September, 15.09% more than a year earlier, according to EFE's tally of their results [2]. Santander alone booked €12,405 million, up 20%; BBVA €9,066 million, up 13.6%; CaixaBank €4,962 million, up 12.9% [2]. In the third quarter alone the six made €9,088 million [2].

**The gap.** On the headline, the print backs the podium. Desk arithmetic: €1,400 million is about 4.8% of what the six earned in nine months; the reports do not say over how many years the €1,400 million would be collected, so read that ratio as scale, not as a rate [1][2]. And the print has small print. Santander's figure includes a net gain of €1,895 million from selling its Polish arm; its ordinary profit was €11,176 million, still a record, up 16% [2]. Sabadell's profit fell 1%, and 5.3% on an underlying basis [2]. "The banks" are not one balance sheet.

**What the podium does not say.** Government sources told EFE the money could fund more than 6,000 affordable homes, renovate 52,000 and give tax breaks to almost 120,000 tenants "during the next legislature" [1]. That is a programme for a parliament not yet elected, announced in the last week of a campaign. The extension still needs Tuesday's cabinet to become law at all [1].

**For you.** If you bank in Spain, the levy is not a line on your statement, but it is a line on your bank's, and banks set fees and deposit rates with their whole statement in view. If you rent, the question is not whether €1,400 million sounds large beside €29,253 million. It is how many of those 6,000 homes have a plot, a permit and a contractor. Nothing here is investment advice.
