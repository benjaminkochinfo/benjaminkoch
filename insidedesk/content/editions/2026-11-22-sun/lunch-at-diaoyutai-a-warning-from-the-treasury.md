Two meetings, one city-state, one week. On Saturday Lawrence Wong, Singapore's prime minister and finance minister, met Li Qiang at Diaoyutai in Beijing and stayed for lunch [1]. Earlier in the week, US Treasury officials met Singapore's and Malaysia's regulators and national security bodies to warn of the risks of not complying with US sanctions on Iran [2]. You could read that as a squeeze. The documents read more like two ledgers being kept in parallel.

The Beijing ledger is infrastructure. Singapore's readout lists the three government-to-government projects in Suzhou, Tianjin and Chongqing, and, under the Chongqing Connectivity Initiative, a search for "synergies" between the International Land-Sea Trade Corridor and China's new Pinglu Canal [1]. The two sides also agreed to improve interoperability between their AI governance frameworks through "a joint mapping exercise", and looked to ASEAN–China cooperation, including the upgraded free-trade area, during Singapore's ASEAN chair year in 2027 [1]. They "reiterated the importance of international law and the right to freedom of passage in international waterways" [1].

The Washington ledger is compliance. Officials asked for extra scrutiny of "high-risk" sectors, maritime transport, aviation services and procurement networks, to make sure firms are "not fronting for Iran" [2]. One official said: "it's not going to be a negotiation when we find a lifeline that needs to be targeted" [2]. Tankers have long used the waters off Malaysia, Singapore and Indonesia for ship-to-ship transfers of Iranian oil, and in July Treasury sanctioned individuals, vessels and companies in places including Singapore [2]. Singapore's government said it "is firmly committed to doing our part" [2].

Neither ledger is dramatic, and that is the point. A canal link and a sanctions checklist are both about the same asset: being the place where trade is clean enough to be trusted by both sides. That reputation is kept by port inspectors, bank compliance desks and company registrars, not by summits.

The numbers arrive this week. The advance estimate put third-quarter growth at 5.4% year on year, against an official 2026 range of 4.5% to 5.5%; the revised figure and the first 2027 forecast are due on Tuesday [3]. Read the forecast before you read anyone's speech about where Singapore stands.
