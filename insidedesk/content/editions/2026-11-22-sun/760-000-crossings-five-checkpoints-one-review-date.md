Last weekend Estonia's foreign minister announced a border closure. On Friday the government took the formal decision, and the document is both wider and more careful than a headline [1][2]. Road crossings for people and vehicles are suspended at all five checkpoints, Narva-1, Narva-2, Koidula, Luhamaa and Saatse, from 07:00 on 1 December [1][2]. After six months the government will assess whether the restriction is still needed [1]. Rail freight continues under existing restrictions and strict customs controls [1].

**The reasons are listed, not shouted.** Defence companies in Estonia have been attacked twice this year, and the Internal Security Service says both attacks were commissioned by Russian intelligence [1]. "Estonia is acting calmly and deliberately, but decisively," said prime minister Kristen Michal [1]. Foreign minister Margus Tsahkna insisted the closure "is not a reaction to a single incident" and named similar attacks on defence industry in Bulgaria, Poland and Germany this year [4]. The government's own claim is modest: the closure "will not eliminate all threats", but removes "one significant security vulnerability", recruitment and sanctions evasion across the border [1].

**The numbers behind the gate.** Annual crossings fell from more than four million before 2022 to around 760,000 in 2026, ERR's figures cited by Euronews show; about 447,000 of them went through Narva–Ivangorod [3]. Reuters counted 307,000 crossings by Russian nationals this year, 175,000 by Estonian citizens and 54,000 by Finnish passport holders [3]. Desk arithmetic: Narva carried nearly six in ten of this year's crossings, which is why the bill lands there. Goods exports to Russia are down to around a third of their 2021 level, so the government calls the economic impact limited [1].

**The cost is local.** By Saturday people with suitcases and plastic bags were queuing in Ivangorod to get across before the deadline [3]. Narva's mayor Jaan Toots has asked for humanitarian exemptions for funerals and visits to elderly relatives and warned of losses for hotels and restaurants [3]. Moscow called the accusations unfounded; foreign ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova spoke of a new "Iron Curtain" and threatened countermeasures [3].

**For you.** If you have family, freight or a trip that relies on these crossings, plan now for other routes, chiefly via Latvia, which the government warns may be longer and costlier [1][3]. The detail worth respecting is the six-month clock: a security measure that schedules its own review is easier to trust, and easier to end.
