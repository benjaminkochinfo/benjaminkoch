The COPSY study at the University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf has been asking the same families how their children are since 2020; this autumn's ninth round covered about 1,500 families with children aged 7 to 21 [1].

The overall picture is still slightly worse than before the pandemic. Around 25% have anxiety symptoms, against 15% before; around 21% report reduced quality of life, also against 15% [1]. Parents report signs of mental-health problems in 24% of 7- to 21-year-olds, against 18% before the pandemic [2]. Yet seven in ten are psychologically stable: "not a whole generation" is suffering lasting effects, says co-author Ulrike Ravens-Sieberer [1].

The worries have moved. About one in six young people has become more anxious in recent years, linked to wars, economic worries and climate; 42% said they worry about affordable housing [1]. "Young people's worries have shifted from the pandemic to the crises of the present," Ravens-Sieberer says [1].

The gap that matters is between groups. Around 6% have been doing badly continuously since 2020; they started with fewer personal, family and social resources and more often had parents under strain [1]. Girls report anxiety symptoms at 33% against 17% for boys, and depressive symptoms at 17% against 9%, a gap widening again since 2023 [1].

The psychotherapists' chamber asks for prevention in Kitas and schools joined up with youth services, and welcomes separate planning of therapy places for children and adolescents [2]. Support, in other words, is a matter of addresses and appointments, not slogans.
