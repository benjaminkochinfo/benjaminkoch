"Lying flat" began as a slogan among young Chinese about refusing the endless race. Researchers writing in BMC Psychology studied its negative form, defined as diminished goal pursuit and less willingness to put in effort [1]. They asked 201 university students, average age 21, to fill in a diary on ten weekdays over two weeks, which yielded 1,863 usable entries [1].

The result runs both ways. On days after students felt more anxious, they reported more lying flat; on days after more lying flat, they reported more anxiety [1]. Depressive symptoms showed the same back-and-forth. The effects were small, with standardised coefficients between about 0.08 and 0.11 [1]. Experiential avoidance, the familiar habit of dodging unpleasant feelings, showed no reliable day-to-day link with either anxiety or depression in the same models [1].

The authors are careful. In a statistical comparison, lying flat was not shown to be stronger than avoidance, and in one sensitivity check two of the paths only marginally held [1]. It is one sample of students and shows timing, not cause [1].

What it offers is a scale that suits ordinary life. The loop is daily, which means the place to interrupt it is daily too: one small task finished today, rather than a grand plan for the year. The study did not test that remedy. It only shows where the loop turns.
