Delaware, where most large American companies are incorporated, is working on a new kind of company: one managed by an artificial-intelligence agent rather than a person [1]. Under the draft, the "Artificial Intelligence Company" could have any lawful purpose except banking, the agent would have to keep a log of its activities, and a single human or corporate member would be responsible for keeping it adequately capitalised [1]. That member would not be liable for the company's debts unless it let the capital run short or used the company to break the law [1]. Organisations would have 30 months to apply to a regulatory sandbox [1]. State lawmakers are expected to consider it next year [1].

On Wednesday the chair of the US House Oversight Committee, James Comer, asked the governor's administration for documents. "We are concerned that Delaware's [Artificial Intelligence Company] proposal could be used to protect powerful AI companies and their investors from responsibility for reckless harmful actions," he wrote [1]. He wants communications back to January 2025, including "internal AI chat logs", by 9 November [1]. The governor's office says it is reviewing the letter [1].

Law professors are split. One called the idea "a solution in search of a problem"; another said Delaware "would be irresponsible to ignore this" [1].

The useful question is the old one. A company is a machine for deciding who pays when something goes wrong. Before anyone asks whether an agent can run a firm, ask who signs the invoice when it gets one wrong.
