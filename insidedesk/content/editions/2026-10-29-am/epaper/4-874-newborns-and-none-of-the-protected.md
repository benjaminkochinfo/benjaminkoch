Respiratory syncytial virus, RSV, is the winter cold that sends small babies to hospital. Last season the Canadian province of Saskatchewan offered every newborn a single injection of nirsevimab, a long-acting antibody, before leaving hospital. On Wednesday its health authority published how that went [1].

It gave the antibody to 4,874 newborns, 78% of babies born during the RSV season, plus 472 high-risk children [1]. Desk arithmetic: that implies roughly 6,250 births in the season. Among the newborns who received it, fewer than 0.5% were admitted to hospital with RSV, and none needed intensive care [1]. Historically, more than 30% of infants admitted to hospital with RSV have needed intensive care, the authority says [1]. Healthy babies used to make up 35 to 46% of children admitted with RSV; among protected children last season that fell to 9% [1].

The second season has started. Every baby born in the province between 1 October and 31 March is eligible before leaving the birthing site, and high-risk children up to two can have it too [1]. "Nirsevimab offers an important layer of protection during a baby's most vulnerable months," said Rachel Leavitt, who manages the programme [1].

These are the authority's own figures, not a trial, and a single season can flatter. But the machinery is the point: a dose given in the maternity ward, before anyone has to remember an appointment. If you are expecting a baby this winter, ask your midwife what your country offers against RSV, and when.
