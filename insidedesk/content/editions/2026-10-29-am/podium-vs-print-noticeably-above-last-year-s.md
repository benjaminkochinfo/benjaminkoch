**The podium.** After the Federal Reserve held its range at 3.75% to 4% on Wednesday evening, Chairman Kevin Warsh opened his press conference with the labour market. "The jobless rate is lower than a year ago, and payroll growth is noticeably above last year's pace," he said; "on the employment side of the Fed's dual mandate, conditions are quite positive" [1]. Asked whether the economy was running too hot, he said: "That isn't the judgment of most of my colleagues" [3].

**The print.** The Bureau of Labor Statistics keeps the ledger he was quoting from. Total non-farm payrolls stood at 159,044,000 in September 2026, against 158,432,000 in December 2025 [2]. A year earlier the same nine months ran from 158,316,000 to 158,548,000 [2]. Desk arithmetic: that is about 68,000 jobs a month this year against about 26,000 a month in the same stretch of 2025, and an increase of only 116,000 over the whole of last year. The unemployment rate was 4.2% in September, down from 4.4% a year earlier [2].

**The gap.** There is not much of one, and that is the finding. Both halves of Warsh's sentence check out against the public figures. What the podium leaves out is scale. Sixty-eight thousand a month is "noticeably above" a very slow year; it is not a hiring boom, and the BLS revises these levels as more payroll records arrive [2]. Warsh himself put the weight elsewhere: inflation, he said, has been "running above the Committee's objective for close to 6 years" [1].

**The verdict.** Podium and print agree, which is worth recording precisely because this column so often finds the opposite. For you, the useful reading is the dull one: a labour market adding jobs at a modest pace, with unemployment edging down, gives a central bank room to concentrate on prices, and that is what Warsh said the committee did [1]. The number that will decide whether his sentence survives is the next payroll release, not the next press conference. Nothing here is investment advice.
