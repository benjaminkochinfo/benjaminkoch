On Thursday evening Intel told investors to expect fourth-quarter revenue of $15 billion to $16 billion, against the $17.1 billion analysts had pencilled in, and adjusted earnings of 25 cents a share against 43 [2]. On Friday the shares lost 29%, their worst day since the 1970s [3]. The call explained the mood. The 10-Q, accepted by the SEC late on Thursday, explains the plumbing [1].

**Where the cash came from.** In the first nine months of 2026 Intel's operations brought in $13.18 billion of cash, against $5.41 billion a year earlier, and it spent $10.22 billion on property, plant and equipment [1]. That is real progress. But the bigger numbers sit lower down: $22.6 billion of net proceeds from a public share offering, $13.0 billion of new term debt against $9.0 billion repaid, and $5.0 billion of contributions from its partner in the Arizona fab joint venture [1]. Going the other way were $14.4 billion of partner distributions; the filing's ownership table shows the outside partner's 49% stake in the Ireland venture down to nothing [1]. Cash and short-term investments ended September at $54.0 billion, up from $37.4 billion at the end of 2025, while total debt rose from $46.6 billion to $50.1 billion [1].

**Where it is promised to go.** Commitments for capital spending stood at $19.6 billion at the end of September, against $12.8 billion at the end of 2025, and other purchase obligations at $9.8 billion, against $6.7 billion [1]. "The substantial majority" of the capital commitments fall due within twelve months [1]. After the quarter closed, Intel signed two agreements to secure substrate supply, with total funding requirements estimated at $5.3 billion to $6.1 billion, about $3.3 billion of it payable by the end of 2027 [1]. It made $1.2 billion of supply prepayments this year, and holds $1.2 billion of customer deposits paid to secure future product [1]. The two figures are the same size: Intel is paying its suppliers to hold a place in the queue, and some of its customers are paying Intel to do the same.

**The stockroom.** Inventories rose from $11.6 billion to $13.1 billion, most of it work in progress ($8.8 billion) [1]. That matters because of Israel: the filing says operations at Kiryat Gat stopped for about two weeks after the October accident that killed employees, and that fourth-quarter charges for scrapped inventory, clean-up and under-used capacity are coming [1]. The finance chief put the lost revenue at about $600 million over two quarters [2].

**For you.** None of this tells you whether the forecast was too gloomy. It tells you what kind of company Intel now is: one that raised equity while a partner left its Irish factory venture, and that has signed for more equipment than it had last winter. Those are dull lines, and they will be updated in three months whatever the share price does. Nothing here is investment advice.
