**The podium.** On Friday night, at a rally in Florida, Donald Trump let the audience see the effort. "I thought this was it," he said. "I thought I could relax. Twenty-eight days of this crap in a row is a lot. It's a lot" [1]. The Guardian notes that he has been holding rallies in previously safe states to lift struggling Republican candidates, with a third trip to Texas in a month this week [1]. It was a candid line, and it was about him.

**The print.** By Saturday morning, more than 50.5 million Americans had cast early votes, according to the University of Florida's election lab, as reported by the Guardian [1]. That is already nearly 5 million more than the total number of early votes in the 2022 midterms, with three days still to go [1]. Early voting is up 83% in South Carolina against the last midterms, 44% in Maine, 34% in Kansas and 32% in Michigan, according to the Associated Press [1].

**The gap.** The podium describes a tiring month. The print describes a country that did not wait to be asked twice. In states that record party registration, 10.7 million registered Democrats have voted so far against 7.8 million registered Republicans [1]. Read that carefully, as the Guardian and the lab both insist: registration is not a vote, and no ballot is counted before Tuesday [1][2]. The figure tells you who turned up early, not whom they chose.

**What it does not say.** A record early vote is not a forecast. Some of it is habit shifting from election day to the weeks before, and some is plain enthusiasm; the trackers cannot tell the two apart, and this desk will not pretend to. What the number does show is the machinery working: county clerks, postal ballots, volunteers at folding tables, all processing a record queue before the first result exists.

**For you.** Watching from Europe, the useful habit is the one this format exists for. Hear the speech, then find the count. On Tuesday the speeches will multiply. The count will arrive more slowly, state by state, and it will be the only thing that matters.
