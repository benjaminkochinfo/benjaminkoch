Researchers at the Federal Institute for Population Research in Wiesbaden, writing in BMC Public Health on Thursday, analysed 14,443 adults aged 18 to 51 from the German family demography panel, FReDA [1]. They used a standard scale that separates two kinds of loneliness: emotional, the lack of someone close, and social, the lack of a wider network of people to rely on [1].

**The counts.** 24.3% reported intense emotional loneliness, 29.6% intense overall loneliness and 35.8% intense social loneliness [1]. Overall loneliness barely changed with age. Underneath, emotional loneliness was lower in the older groups and social loneliness higher: the 18–25s scored highest on the first, the 46–51s highest on the second [1].

**What went with it.** Talking about personal matters with friends, acquaintances, neighbours or colleagues was among the strongest links to lower social loneliness; having a partner helped too [1]. Depressed mood went most strongly with emotional loneliness, and a first-generation migration background with social loneliness [1]. Having children made little difference [1].

**The caveats.** The data were collected in autumn and winter 2021/22, during the pandemic, and the design is a snapshot, so it cannot say what causes what [1]. The authors say the observational findings do not show that any intervention would work [1].

**For you.** The useful distinction is practical. A partner does not replace a circle, and a circle does not replace someone close. In midlife, the thinner thread tends to be the colleague, the neighbour and the old friend you mean to call. That is the cheaper one to mend.
