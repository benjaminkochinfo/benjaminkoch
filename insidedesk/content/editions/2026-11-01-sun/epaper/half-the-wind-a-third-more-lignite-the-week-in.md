Put two working weeks side by side, Monday to Saturday, and the German electricity system tells its own story in the regulator's published meter data [1].

**Wind.** From 19 to 24 October, onshore and offshore wind together generated about 4,226 GWh. From 26 to 31 October, about 2,072 GWh [1]. Desk arithmetic: 49% of the week before, so roughly half.

**Lignite.** Up from about 1,201 GWh to about 1,563 GWh [1]. Desk arithmetic: about 30% more. On Saturday alone, lignite plants produced about 304 GWh against 115 GWh from all of Germany's wind turbines [1]. Desk arithmetic: about 2.6 times as much.

**Gas.** Up from about 922 GWh to about 1,344 GWh [1]. Desk arithmetic: about 46% more. Gas is usually the plant that sets the price at the margin, which is why this line matters for the bill.

**Sun and demand.** Solar barely moved, from about 764 GWh to about 796 GWh, and grid load was nearly flat, about 8,089 GWh against 7,954 GWh [1]. The country did not use more power. It made it differently.

The constructive point is that none of this is hidden. Every quarter-hour of every plant type is published, free, by the Bundesnetzagentur. When someone tells you next week what the energy transition costs or saves, you can open the same table and check which week they are talking about.
