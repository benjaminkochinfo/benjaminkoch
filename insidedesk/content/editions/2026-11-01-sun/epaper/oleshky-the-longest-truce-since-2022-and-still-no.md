On Friday the buses left Oleshky. By Saturday the operation was finished. The Institute for the Study of War called it "a remarkably successful humanitarian mission" [1]. Residents of the occupied town in Kherson region flew to Belarus and then crossed into Ukraine's Chernihiv region, Ukrainian officials said; others chose to stay, or went to Simferopol in occupied Crimea [1]. Ukraine's human rights commissioner, Dmytro Lubinets, travelled into Oleshky and through Russia and back, and called the local ceasefire the longest that both sides have observed since 2022 [1].

The truce has held since the morning of 27 October, though not perfectly. A Ukrainian military spokesman, Maksym Kravchuk, reported repeated Russian violations, including drones dropping explosives on Ukrainian positions; a Russian official claimed a Ukrainian drone hit a civilian vehicle near the town [1]. ISW could not verify either claim [1]. Kravchuk also said Russian forces were using the quiet to rotate troops and strengthen positions and logistics [1]. A local ceasefire is never only humanitarian, and nobody should pretend otherwise.

What is still missing is the number. ISW says the exact count of residents who reached Ukrainian-controlled territory "remains unclear" [1]. The right test now is plain: a published list, reconciled between the two rights commissioners, of who left, who arrived and who stayed. A truce that can be counted is a truce that can be copied. Five days in one town is small. It is also a working template.
