Two central banks met this week, a day apart, and did the same thing in different ways. Both held. Both said energy is the problem. Both left December open. The difference is in who wanted to move now.

**Wednesday, Washington.** The Federal Open Market Committee kept its target range at 3.75% to 4% by a vote of 10 to 2 [1]. Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack voted against; both wanted a rise of a quarter point [1]. CNBC noted it was Cook's first dissent since she joined in 2022 [2]. The statement said inflation "remains elevated" and that "the accumulation of more information" will guide the next step; it upgraded growth to "expanding at a strong pace" [1]. Inflation has been above the Fed's 2% goal for more than five years [2].

**Thursday, Frankfurt.** The ECB kept its deposit rate at 2.50%, the main refinancing rate at 2.65% and the marginal lending rate at 2.90% [3]. "Energy prices have continued to rise," the Governing Council said, with upside risks for inflation and downside risks for growth [3]. Euro-area inflation was 3.8% in September [6]. Christine Lagarde said the Council "debated whether a hike was appropriate" and then decided unanimously to wait [4]. After the decision, money markets priced about a 67% chance of a December rise, according to LSEG data cited by CNBC [4].

**The second brake.** The more interesting line was about bonds. Long-term borrowing has already become dearer: Lagarde said the average 10-year sovereign yield in the euro area had risen around 35 basis points since the previous meeting [4]. Reuters reported ECB estimates showing that rise would lower growth by a cumulative 100 basis points and inflation by 90 basis points in 2027–28, compared with the September baseline [5]. In plain words: the market has tightened for the ECB, and the ECB is counting it.

**What it means at home.** For your savings account and your mortgage offer, the policy rate did not move this week; the bond market did, and banks price long fixed-rate loans off the bond market. Read your next offer against that, not against the headline. Nothing here is investment advice.

**The dull reassurance.** Neither bank improvised. Each published a statement, held a press conference, recorded a vote and named what it will watch. "The interim meeting period will be information-rich," Lagarde said [5]. That is a timetable, and a timetable is a kind of promise.
