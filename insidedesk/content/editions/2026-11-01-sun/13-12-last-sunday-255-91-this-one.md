Last Sunday, 25 October, German and Luxembourg buyers paid a daily average of €13.12 per megawatt hour for electricity on the day-ahead market [1]. Storms were crossing the country, and the wind was doing the work. This Sunday, 1 November, All Saints' Day and a public holiday in parts of Germany, the same auction settled at €255.91 [1]. Same day of the week, lower demand than a working day, and a price nearly twenty times higher. By SMARD's daily series, it is the fourth-dearest delivery day of 2026, behind only 29 and 30 October and 14 September [1].

[[chart:de-power-two-sundays-01nov]]

**The week in prices.** The chart is the whole fortnight, taken straight from the regulator's market-data platform [1]. The first week slid from €208.44 on Monday 19 October to €30.12 on the Saturday and €13.12 on the Sunday. The second climbed back: €116.57 on Monday 26 October, €249.21 on Wednesday, €314.85 on Thursday, the dearest day of the year so far, then €268.62 on Friday and €251.42 on Saturday [1]. Desk arithmetic: the average for the seven delivery days from 19 to 25 October was about €135.07; for 26 October to 1 November it was about €230.24, roughly 70% higher.

**The week in wind.** The reason is not mysterious, and it is not a scandal. On Sunday 25 October onshore and offshore wind together generated about 1,112 GWh; on Saturday 31 October, about 115 GWh [2]. Desk arithmetic: about a tenth. The weather service's October review names the storms, Luise and Manja, which brought long, heavy rain to the west on the 23rd and 24th [3]. When the storms passed, the turbines slowed, and the market went back to burning things.

**What it means for you.** Very little, directly, this week. Most households pay a fixed tariff, and the day-ahead price reaches them slowly, through suppliers' purchasing, months later and averaged. A dynamic tariff passes the swing through faster; that cuts both ways, as these two Sundays show. Nothing here is investment advice.

**The constructive reading.** A market that can price the same Sunday at €13 and at €256 is not broken; it is telling the truth about the weather, hour by hour, in public. The data are free, published by the Bundesnetzagentur, and anyone can check them [1]. The useful response is dull: more storage, more cross-border cables, more demand that can wait for the cheap hours. The fortnight above is the business case, already printed.
