On Sundays this desk puts the week back in order: not by the size of the headlines but by when prices moved. This week the order was simple to describe and instructive to read. Three days of buying on a promise, then two days of selling on the accounts.

**Monday: a prospectus.** Anthropic filed publicly for a stock-market listing, and chipmakers rallied on it [1]. The S&P 500 rose 0.7% to 7,676.77 and the Nasdaq 0.82% to 26,169.38 [1]. The iShares semiconductor fund rose nearly 3%, and Brent fell 0.8% to $115.81 a barrel [1]. The filing was not a profit. It was an invitation to imagine one.

**Tuesday: a loan.** Banks and Blackstone announced $63 billion of financing for buying Broadcom technology for Anthropic, and Broadcom rose 4% [2]. The S&P 500 gained 0.6% to 7,722.60 and the Nasdaq 1.18% to 26,477.81 [2]. Brent fell 1.67% to $113.88 [2]. Note the instrument: the good news of the day was a debt arrangement.

**Wednesday: a pause.** The Federal Reserve held rates at 3.75% to 4%, as futures had priced at almost 93% [3]. The S&P 500 rose 0.28% to 7,743.98, its third winning day in a row [3]. Then Alphabet and Microsoft reported after the bell [3].

**Thursday: the first invoice.** Alphabet's revenue missed and its cash burn accelerated; the shares fell 12.6% [4]. The Nasdaq lost 1.37% to 26,184.72, the S&P 500 0.30% [4]. Yet small companies rose: the Russell 2000 gained 1.0% [4]. "Cracks in the AI story" did not mean "cracks in every corner of the market," said Baird's Ross Mayfield [4].

**Friday: the second invoice.** Amazon reported negative free cash flow of $42.4 billion, Intel fell almost 29% on a weak outlook, and the Nasdaq lost 2.86% to 25,437.00, its worst day since June [5]. The S&P 500 fell 1.82% to 7,580.07; staples and healthcare rose [5]. On the week, the S&P 500 lost 0.57% [6]. Desk arithmetic: from Wednesday's close to Friday's, the S&P 500 gave up 2.1%, more than the three good days had added.

**Underneath, the bonds.** While the shares swung, Treasuries rallied all week. The 2-year yield fell from 4.69% to 4.56% and the 10-year from 5.00% to 4.94% between the two Fridays [7]. The middle of the curve moved most; the 30-year barely moved.

**What the order says.** Promises and financing moved prices up; cash-flow statements moved them down. That is not panic. It is the old machinery of quarterly accounts doing its job, a week late for those who bought on Monday. If you hold a broad fund, the bond half did what it is there for. Nothing here is investment advice.
