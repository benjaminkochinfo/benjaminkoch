On Friday the US State Department told Congress it had approved a possible sale to Ukraine of five hundred PAC-3 MSE missiles, the interceptors fired by Patriot batteries, with spare parts and logistics support, at an estimated $2.1 billion [1]. President Volodymyr Zelensky had said earlier in the week that Ukraine had been waiting almost a month for this sign-off [2]. The notice reads like an invoice, and it is worth reading like one.

**Who pays.** The notice is unusually specific. Ukraine will pay "with donations from Canada, Germany, and Norway via the JUMPSTART program and national funds, financed by the European Union's Ukraine Support Loan", and "no U.S. funds are involved" [1]. The principal contractor is Lockheed Martin [1]. Desk arithmetic: $2.1 billion for 500 missiles is about $4.2 million a missile, with spares and support included. So the money is European and Canadian, the factory is American, and the whole arrangement sits on a loan approved in Brussels. That is a coalition you can audit.

**When it arrives.** Here the notice is silent. The BBC understands the order is for newly built missiles rather than existing stock, and cites the US Department of Defense for a production rate of only 600 a year [2]. Reports gathered by the Institute for the Study of War say the new interceptors are not expected until after 2030 [3]. Desk arithmetic: 500 missiles is about ten months of that stated output. The BBC also notes a global shortage of these interceptors because of their use by the US in its war against Iran [2]. Zelensky welcomed the approval and said Ukraine needed the missiles "right now" [2].

**What the night looked like.** The same night, Russia launched 134 drones; Ukraine's air force said 105 were shot down or suppressed, and drones hit 20 locations [4]. Strikes on a heat and power plant in Kyiv and four substations in Dnipropetrovsk region left around 200,000 households there and more than 80,000 in Kyiv region without electricity [4]. The Patriot matters for a different threat: the BBC calls it the only effective weapon for downing Russia's ballistic missiles [2]. And the Ukrainian military stopped regularly publishing missile launch and intercept numbers in August [3]. That gap in the published record is itself a measure of the shortage.

**Why this is still good news.** A sale notified to Congress, with a named contractor and named payers, is harder to reverse than a promise made at a podium. It also hands the factory a funded order, and funded orders are how production lines get bigger. The honest framing is this: Friday's decision helps Ukraine next decade. This winter depends on missiles that already exist, and on whoever is prepared to lend them.
