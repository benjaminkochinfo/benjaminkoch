Monday began with a small relief and a long diary. Brent crude slipped about 1% to $115.48 a barrel in early Asian trade, after reports that Qatari mediators were in Tehran on Sunday in an effort, coordinated with the United States, to calm the region [1]. A later Reuters update had it 1.6% lower at $114.88 [2]. Last week Brent rose 6.2%, its highest weekly close since mid-2022, because Houthi attacks in Saudi Arabia raised the fear for supply [1]. Those attacks did not stop over the weekend; Saudi Arabia said it intercepted drones and ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh and the south on Sunday [4]. So the barrel came down a dollar or two on a flight plan, not on a ceasefire. You should price it that way too.

**The long rate is the referee.** The US ten-year Treasury yield closed Friday at 5.00%, according to the Treasury's own daily curve, after 4.96% on Thursday, the lowest close since 22 September [3]. It peaked at 5.31% on 5 October and started the year at 4.19% [3]. Reuters notes that Treasuries rallied last week before "running into renewed selling on Friday" [1].

[[chart:ust10-26oct]]

That line matters more than any index. A ten-year yield of 5% is the rate against which every other promise is measured: the mortgage, the corporate bond, the data centre that pays back in 2032. When it rises, a distant profit is worth less today. When it falls, as on Thursday, the distant profit looks better and tech shares breathe. Friday's 5.00% says the bond market has not yet decided which week it is in.

**Three committees in three days.** The US Federal Reserve meets from Wednesday; futures imply about a 90% chance it holds its target range at 3.75% to 4.00% [6]. The ECB's Governing Council meets in Frankfurt on Wednesday and Thursday [5]. The Bank of Japan decides this week too [6]. Reuters quotes JPMorgan's Bruce Kasman: "a relatively shallow rate hike cycle is underway", with the big three likely on hold for now [1]. Markets imply about an 80% chance that the Fed moves in December, and "much the same" for the BoJ and the ECB [1]. New Zealand is widely expected to raise its rate on Wednesday [1]. A hold, in other words, is not a cut. It is a pause with the next step already in the minutes.

**Three invoices.** The same week, about a third of the S&P 500 reports, including Alphabet, Microsoft and Amazon [1]. Consensus, per Reuters, is $90.6 billion of revenue for Microsoft, $202 billion for Amazon and $128 billion for Alphabet [1]. Their capital spending is the other half of the AI story: the bills paid to chipmakers, builders and utilities. Asia is already nervous. South Korea's main index has shed almost a fifth of its value this month, and Japan's Nikkei lost 4.0% last week [1]. Tokyo Electron fell 3.24% in early trade on Monday [6].

**What to do with all this.** Nothing quick. The diary this week is a good one, because it is full of dull, scheduled things: decisions at fixed times, statements with numbered paragraphs, earnings reports with audited lines. The war produces headlines at random; the institutions produce documents on time. When the two disagree, the documents usually win, a little later than the headlines suggest.

The one number to watch is not an index level but the ten-year yield on Thursday evening, after the ECB and the Fed have both spoken and the first hyperscaler has shown its capital spending. If it is still near 5%, the market has heard the committees and the companies and stayed calm. If it moves sharply, someone said something that was not in the minutes. Nothing here is advice.
