Methadone and buprenorphine are the two medicines commonly used to treat opioid use disorder in pregnancy [1]. A worry that higher doses bring worse withdrawal in the newborn leads some patients and clinicians to consider lowering the dose or stopping [1]. A research letter in JAMA Network Open, published on 29 October by researchers at the University of Maryland School of Medicine, tested that worry directly [1].

**The method.** The team analysed 92 pregnancies in which the mother had taken methadone or buprenorphine for at least 30 days before delivery, and measured the drugs and their breakdown products in umbilical cord tissue, a marker of what the baby was exposed to over the final trimester [1]. Severe withdrawal occurred in about 44% of the newborns, more often after methadone than buprenorphine [1]. But neither the mother's dose nor the cord concentration was significantly linked to how severe it was [1].

"Cutting the dose doesn't give the baby an easier withdrawal. It only puts the mother at risk," said Courtney Townsel, the lead author [1].

**The limits.** The sample is small and not very diverse, the authors say, and the result does not mean newborns need less monitoring or care [1]. What it does is remove a reason to under-treat the mother.

**For you.** If you or someone close to you is in this position, the finding supports a simple sequence: set the dose for the mother's recovery, and let the hospital look after the baby's withdrawal if it comes. Treatment kept is protection kept.
