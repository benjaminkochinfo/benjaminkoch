Researchers led by Takayuki Osugi of Yamagata University showed computer-generated faces of four racial appearances either standing still or bowing, and asked people to rate attractiveness from 0 to 100 [1]. Japanese raters scored bowing faces roughly 6 to 12 points higher for a short bow and 15 to 28 points higher for a long one, whatever the face's appearance [1]. For female European models, the scores went from 43.2 static to 59.0 with a long bow [1].

American raters, recruited online, barely moved: gaps were typically under three points, and for the same models the averages were 56.5, 57.4 and 56.9 [1]. They could still tell attractive faces from less attractive ones, so they were paying attention [1].

The samples were small, 30 to 49 people per sub-experiment, and the faces were avatars on home screens [1]. The point survives: politeness is learned, and what we learn shapes what we see.
