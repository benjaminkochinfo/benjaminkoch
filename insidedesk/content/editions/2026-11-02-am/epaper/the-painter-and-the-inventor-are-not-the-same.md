The idea that genius and "madness" go together is old; the evidence for it, says Miriam Mosing, co-senior author of a new paper in Molecular Psychiatry, "remains mixed" [1]. Researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Empirical Aesthetics, Karolinska Institutet and other institutions tried to sort it out with twins [1].

**The data.** They used two Swedish surveys, from 2012–2013 and 2022–2023, covering 14,658 twins, 6,419 of whom answered both [1]. The surveys measured achievement in music, writing, dance, theatre and visual arts, and separately in science and invention; mental health came from self-reported symptoms and from psychiatric diagnoses in Swedish medical records [1]. Comparing identical twins, who share almost all genes and their upbringing, lets researchers strip out much of what families have in common [1].

**The finding.** Higher artistic creativity went with "somewhat more mental health problems", and genes played an important role in that link, says first author Penghao Xia [1]. Scientific creativity was not significantly linked to most conditions; some measures even went with fewer difficulties [1]. Earlier depressive symptoms predicted greater openness to experience later, even between identical twins, a hint of a causal path that the authors say still needs confirming [1].

**Why it matters.** The lesson is about measurement: lump all creativity together and the differences cancel out, which may be why earlier studies disagreed [1]. For you, the practical reading is modest. A creative child is not a worry in waiting, and a lab bench is not a cure. The authors stress that environment still matters [1].
