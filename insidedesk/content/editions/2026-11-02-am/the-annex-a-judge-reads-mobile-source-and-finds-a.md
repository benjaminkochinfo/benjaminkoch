**The document.** Late on Friday, the US District Court for the Northern District of Mississippi released an opinion denying xAI's motion to dismiss a Clean Air Act lawsuit brought by the NAACP [1][2]. The suit, filed in April by the Southern Environmental Law Center and Earthjustice, concerns gas-fired turbines in Southaven, Mississippi, that power the company's Colossus data centres across the state line in Greater Memphis [1]. It alleges that between August and December 2025 xAI and a subsidiary installed and ran 27 turbines "without an air permit" [1]. xAI is now owned by SpaceX, which CNBC says has rebranded it SpaceXAI [1].

**The three arguments.** xAI's lawyers, in a motion filed in June, said the turbines were "mobile" and so exempt from permitting; that the NAACP had no right to sue under the Act; and that citizen suits of this kind are unconstitutional [1]. The court's answer fits in one sentence, which CNBC quotes: "Because the plaintiffs have statutory standing under the Clean Air Act, because the combustion turbines are not 'mobile sources' under Mississippi's SIP, and because the citizen suit provision does not violate Article II of the Constitution or the private nondelegation doctrine, the plaintiffs' claims will proceed." [1]

**The line that matters for every data centre.** The plant "was not (and is not) exempted from permitting requirements," the court held, according to Earthjustice, because it is not a mobile source under the federal regulations [2]. Read plainly: a generator on a trailer is still a power station if it behaves like one. For an industry racing to put electricity next to its chips faster than the grid can deliver it, that sentence moves the permit back to the front of the queue.

**What it does not say.** The opinion lets the claims proceed [1]; it is not a finding that xAI broke the law. The court has not yet decided the NAACP's request for a preliminary injunction [2]. About two weeks earlier, the same court rejected a Department of Justice request to end the case [1]. The emissions figures in circulation, more than 5,000 tons a year of nitrogen oxides among them, are the plaintiffs' side's estimates of what the turbines can emit [2].

**The constructive reading.** The dull machinery worked as designed: a permit rule, a citizen-suit clause written decades ago, and a judge who read both. Compute will need a great deal of power. The paperwork that comes with power plants is how the neighbours get a say.
