Tomorrow Americans vote in the midterm elections, and the feeling in the room is not hard to read. In a survey of 1,000 US adults conducted online by Dynata for LifeStance Health in October, 70% said they felt at least some stress or anxiety about the vote on 3 November, and 43% reported a moderate or significant amount [1]. That is the number that will be quoted this week. It is worth reading next to a second one.

[[chart:us-midterm-stress-02nov]]

**The mood.** The stress is not evenly spread. More than half of Gen Z respondents (55%) and 48% of millennials reported moderate or significant stress [1]. Among full-time employees the figure was 51%, and 47% said they would probably or definitely take a mental health day after election day if they could [1]. Asked about the political climate in general, 42% said they felt anxious about the future and a quarter (25%) reported hopelessness or a sense of "doom" [1].

Some of the coping is new. About one in four respondents (26%) said they had used an AI chatbot to talk through political or election stress, rising to 41% of Gen Z and 6% of baby boomers [1]. Fewer than one in three (32%) had a plan to protect their mental health on election night; another 27% said they had none but thought they should [1]. And the dread has a long tail: 37% said they were already dreading political conversations at family gatherings over the holidays, and 31% would rather wash all the dishes [1].

A caution before going further. This is a company survey, by a mental health provider, with a stated interest in the subject [1]. Its numbers describe feelings honestly reported, not diagnoses. They are a thermometer, not a chart from a clinic.

**The machinery.** Now the second number. Pew Research Center surveyed 5,062 US adults, including 3,917 registered voters, from 28 September to 4 October [2]. It found that American voters "generally expect" the midterms to be run and administered well, and that this view is shared by similar majorities on both sides [2]. Nearly eight in ten voters (79%) were at least somewhat confident that votes cast in person would be counted as voters intend; among those backing Republican candidates it was 84%, among those backing Democrats 81% [2].

The trust is not unconditional, and Pew is careful to say where it thins. Confidence that mail and absentee ballots will be counted as intended was 53% overall [2]. Among Democratic voters it was 64%, down from 88% in the 2022 midterms; among Republican voters it was 42%, modestly higher than before [2]. Roughly half of voters (52%) were confident the systems are free from hacking [2]. And the two sides worry about different things: 19% of Republican voters mentioned fraudulent or illegitimate votes, while Democratic voters were more likely to raise legitimate voters being denied access (10%) or interference in election administration by the Trump administration (11%) [2]. Pew notes its survey was taken before the Justice Department sued California on 15 October over mailed ballots [2].

Still, the core holds. Two-thirds of voters were confident that people who try to intimidate voters at polling places will be stopped [2]. Seven in ten Democratic voters (70%) and 61% of Republican voters expected Americans to know who won within a day or two of election day [2].

**What the two numbers say together.** Desk arithmetic: the share of adults stressed about the vote (70%) [1] and the share of voters confident in the in-person count (79%) [2] cannot both describe separate minorities. They come from different surveys and different samples, so they cannot be added or netted. But taken side by side, they imply that a great many Americans hold both views at once: anxious about who wins, and fairly calm about whether the win will be counted properly.

That is a healthier combination than the headlines suggest. Anxiety about outcomes is what people feel when an election matters. Distrust of the count is what they feel when the institution has failed. The surveys show plenty of the first and, for the in-person ballot at least, a minority of the second. The weak spots are specific and named: postal ballots, hacking, and a partisan split over who might be wrongly let in or kept out [2]. Specific worries can be answered by specific procedures, and that is the dull work of county clerks, poll workers and canvassing boards this week.

**For you.** If you follow the count from Europe tomorrow night, the useful habit is the one the clerks use: wait for the official tally, and separate how you feel about the result from what you know about the process. Your stress about the winner is your own business. Your confidence in the counting can rest on evidence, and the evidence, read carefully, is steadier than the mood.
