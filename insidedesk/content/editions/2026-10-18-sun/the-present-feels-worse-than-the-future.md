The University of Michigan's preliminary October survey put consumer sentiment at 46.3, down from 48.1 in September and 53.6 a year ago [1]. That headline hides a split worth naming.

Current economic conditions fell 12.2% in a month, to 44.7. Consumer expectations rose 2.2%, to 47.3 [1]. People rate today worse and the months ahead slightly better. It is the same shape as this week's oil market: an expensive present and a hoped-for calmer future.

The survey's director, Joanne Hsu, said buying conditions for durable goods "plummeted amid high prices and borrowing costs", and that sentiment fell steeply among lower-income consumers and those with smaller stock portfolios, the groups with fewer resources to absorb price rises [1].

The figure committees will read twice is inflation expectations: 4.7% for the year ahead and 3.5% for the long run, both the highest since May [1]. Expectations are a behaviour, not a feeling. People who expect prices to rise ask for raises and buy early, which is why central banks guard this number more carefully than any podium line. The constructive read: the long-run number rose by a tenth of a point in a month, from 3.4% to 3.5% [1], not by a full point. The public is pricing a shock, and not yet a spiral. That is a sensible public.
