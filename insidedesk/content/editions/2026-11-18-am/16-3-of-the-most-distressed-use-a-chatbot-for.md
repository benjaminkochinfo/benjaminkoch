A research letter published in JAMA Network Open on Tuesday looks at a question most debates about chatbots skip: who actually uses them for emotional support. Researchers at Tohoku University and Japan's National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry used a large Japanese internet survey and grouped 6,852 adults by their level of mental-health need [1].

Regular use – generative AI for emotional support a few times a month or more in 2025 – climbed steeply with need: 4.6% in the lower-need group, 9.4% with elevated need, 16.3% with high need and 20.7% among those with both indicators [1]. Loneliness or social isolation climbed alongside, from 24.7% in the lower-need group to 89.9% in the highest [1]. Past-year use of mental-health services ran from 1.0% to 35.7% [1].

Read the gradient carefully. It does not show that chatbots make people unwell, nor that they help. The design is a survey snapshot, and the models adjust for loneliness, income and professional contacts precisely because so much travels together [1]. What it shows is where the demand sits: among people who are already struggling and often alone.

That is an argument for dull institutions rather than against new tools: a GP's waiting list, a counselling service with an evening slot, a helpline that picks up. If one in five of the most distressed are talking to software, the useful response is to make sure a person is also within reach.
