On the day the Nasdaq touched an eight-month low, the US Commerce Department reported that retail sales rose 0.2% in October after an unrevised 0.8% in September, in line with forecasts, Reuters reports [1]. Sales were 6.8% higher than a year earlier, not adjusted for inflation [1].

The headline hid a stronger core. Sales excluding cars, petrol, building materials and food services – the measure closest to consumer spending in GDP – rose 0.6%, against a forecast of 0.4%, after an upwardly revised 0.5% in September [1]. Car dealers' receipts fell 1.6% and furniture 2.3%; non-store retailers rose 1.2%; miscellaneous stores, including florists and pet shops, jumped 4.7% [1].

"More consumers are clearly struggling with rapidly rising prices but are not yet ready to throw in the towel," said BMO's Scott Anderson [1]. Goldman Sachs economists raised their fourth-quarter growth estimate by 0.6 percentage point to 2.4% annualised, according to Reuters [1]. Import prices rose 1.1% for a second month [1].

The ledger's point is small and stubborn: a payroll is still being spent, mostly on ordinary things, while the market argues about chips. That is not a reason for complacency about prices. It is a reason not to confuse a share index with a household.
