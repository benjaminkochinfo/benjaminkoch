Last night, at about 22:20 Berlin time, the most watched company report of the autumn came out, and it was large. Nvidia booked revenue of $115.8 billion for the quarter to 25 October, up 20% on the previous quarter and 103% on a year earlier [1]. Data-centre revenue alone was $107.6 billion [1]. Gross margin was 74.8% on the official (GAAP) measure [1]. Analysts polled by LSEG had expected $109.10 billion and adjusted earnings of $2.48 a share; Nvidia reported $2.68 [2]. In August the company itself had guided to $108 billion [4].

[[chart:nvidia-quarter-18nov]]

**The guide is the second invoice.** For the current quarter Nvidia expects $130.0 billion, plus or minus 3%, and it assumes no data-centre compute revenue from China [1]. LSEG's consensus had been $125.1 billion [2]. Read the footnote before the headline: the quarter includes an extra week, and Melius Research estimates that the extra week accounts for $9 billion to $10 billion of the sequential jump, CNBC reports [3]. Take that out and the guide is roughly in line with what the market had already pencilled in. That is not a disappointment. It is arithmetic.

On the call, interim chief executive Colette Kress went further and raised the growth outlook for the next financial year, which ends in January 2028, to 75% from 70% [2]. "What we need in terms of chips, what we need in terms of advanced packaging or memory, all of that is also coming together," she said [2]. Notice what she listed: chips, packaging, memory. Not demand. Supply. For two years the question about this company has been whether customers would keep ordering; the answer on paper is still yes, and the bottleneck she describes is factories, not appetite.

**Then the morning.** Nvidia's shares rose roughly 5% in after-hours trading, CNBC and AFP report [3][6]. Asia did not follow. By about 03:30 CET the Nikkei was down 0.3% at the lunch break, the Hang Seng down 0.5% and the Kospi down 0.2%, AFP's table shows [6]. "The momentum in Japan that was lifted by a strong outlook for Nvidia did not last long," Kazuaki Shimada of IwaiCosmo Securities said, citing worries about the company's leadership and a possible slowdown in US data-centre construction [5]. Nvidia is reporting a week after its co-founder Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive [2]; TechCrunch reports that the Securities and Exchange Commission is examining the company's disclosures, according to The Wall Street Journal [7]. Those are matters for the regulator and the board, not for a morning paper to pre-judge.

**What the numbers do and do not tell you.** They tell you that customers paid. Net income was $65.3 billion in a single quarter, Investopedia notes [4]. They tell you that the company expects them to keep paying for at least another fourteen weeks. They do not tell you whether the people paying – cloud providers, AI laboratories, sovereign buyers – will earn back what they spend. That answer comes from other invoices: the ones those customers send to their own customers. "Investors should be ready for volatility in the coming weeks," eToro's Josh Gilbert told CNBC, even while calling the outlook a clear signal of confidence [3].

There is also a quieter line in the release. Nvidia says it shipped its new Vera Rubin rack systems and lists packaging and substrate partners – Amkor, Ibiden, Samsung Electro-Mechanics, Unimicron and others – with whom it plans to scale manufacturing [1]. That is the dull machinery: substrates, test houses, packaging lines in Taiwan, Korea and Japan. It is also where a market that has fallen four days in a row may eventually find its footing, because orders for packaging lines are harder to talk away than a chief executive's speech.

So treat this morning's shrug as information, not as a contradiction. A company can beat its own forecast and still not settle the argument about the whole industry. The beat is a fact. The argument continues on other people's balance sheets. Nothing here is investment advice.
