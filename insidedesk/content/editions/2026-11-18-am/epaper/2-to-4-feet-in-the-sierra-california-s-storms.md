A series of Pacific storms is moving through the US West this week, with winter storm warnings calling for 2 to 4 feet of snow across the Sierra Nevada, The Weather Channel reports [1]. Along the California coast 3 to 6 inches of rain are possible, and more than 6 inches in the foothills [1]. Ridge-top gusts could reach 100 mph in the northern Sierra [1]. Thursday is expected to be the worst travel day through the mountains [1]. USA Today describes the set-up as an atmospheric river bringing rain and snow [2].

The snow is the good news. "The West relies on the snowpack for maintaining a fresh water supply in dry seasons," the forecasters note, and the snowpack "needs to be filled in" [1]. An early-season load in the Sierra is the reservoir of next summer.

The rain is the risk, and the reason is timing rather than totals. Each storm may not bring much on its own, but soils have little time to dry between rounds, raising the chance of flooding, landslides and debris flows; flash-flood watches are posted along the coast, with mudslides possible around recent burn scars [1].

Adaptation here is unglamorous: cleared culverts, closed roads, chain controls, sandbags placed before the second storm rather than after the third. California has practised it for decades. The test this week is whether the practice holds.
