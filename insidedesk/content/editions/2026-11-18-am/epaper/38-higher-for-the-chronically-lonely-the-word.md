A study in Aging & Mental Health, summarised by Medical Xpress on Tuesday, draws a useful line through a word everybody uses loosely [1]. Researchers at the Université de Bordeaux used a French cohort of 3,777 adults aged 65 and over, followed from 1988 to 2018, and classified loneliness over eight years as never (48.3%), occasional (34.4%) or chronic (17.3%) [1].

Among 1,540 participants still eligible for the 20-year follow-up, dementia developed in 33.1% of the never-lonely, 34.5% of the occasionally lonely and 49.1% of the chronically lonely [1]. After adjustment, chronic loneliness carried a 38% higher risk than never feeling lonely; occasional loneliness showed no significant association [1].

"While most people experience loneliness at some point in their lives, it is when loneliness becomes chronic and persistent that it may increase the risk," said co-author Karine Pérès [1]. Another finding cuts against an easy proxy: 29.6% of the chronically lonely did not live alone [1].

The limits matter: loneliness was measured with a single question, and the design shows association, not cause [1]. But the message is calm rather than alarming. A lonely winter is not a diagnosis. Loneliness that lasts years is a signal worth acting on, for neighbours and GPs as much as for the person concerned.
