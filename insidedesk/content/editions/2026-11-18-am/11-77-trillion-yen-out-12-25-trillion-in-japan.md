Japan's October trade figures, published this morning, read like two different economies. Exports rose 20.5% on a year earlier to a record ¥11.77 trillion, led by semiconductor-related demand [1]. Shipments of chip-making equipment rose 49.7%, NDTV Profit reports [4]. Imports rose 21.6% to a record ¥12.25 trillion, driven by higher energy costs [1].

The gap is a deficit of ¥476.5 billion, about $3 billion, the sixth in a row and 58% larger than a year earlier [1][3]. AP attributes the import bill to oil prices that keep imports high [2]. Japan buys almost all of its crude abroad; when the Gulf's shipping lanes are disrupted, the invoice arrives in Tokyo in yen, and the yen is weak – the dollar bought 159.48 yen at about 03:30 CET, AFP's table shows [5].

There is a constructive reading. The export side is not a speech about AI; it is order books at equipment makers, shipped and invoiced. The import side is a commodity price, and commodity prices move both ways: Brent has lost about a fifth of its value this month (see Energy). If that holds, the deficit is the part of this report most likely to shrink on its own.

What it is not: proof that the chip boom is over or that it will last. It is one month of customs paperwork, which is precisely why it is worth reading.
