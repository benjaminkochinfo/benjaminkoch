**The podium.** On Thursday the sixteen state premiers, Social Democrats included, called on the federal government "to take financial measures as quickly as possible so that a cut in the electricity tax is possible for everyone". Their chair, Gordon Schnieder, added: "Das haben wir versprochen und das ist zu halten" — we promised that, and it must be kept [1]. (English quotes in this piece are the desk's translations from German.)

**The answer.** On Friday the deputy government spokesman, Steffen Meyer, said the cut is in the coalition agreement and "there is the clear goal of implementing it fully in the end". Then the clause: like every project, it stands "under a financing proviso" [1]. He named no date. Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil had said on Thursday, after the tax estimate, that anyone proposing a cut "should at the same time bring proposals for how to finance it" [1].

**The print.** The electricity tax for households is 2.05 cents per kilowatt hour; the EU minimum is 0.1 cent [2]. A cut to the minimum for all consumers would cost about €5.4 billion a year, by the finance ministry's estimate [2]. So far the cut applies only to manufacturing companies and to agriculture and forestry [2].

**What it would mean at home.** Including VAT, the cut would save about 2.32 cents per kilowatt hour, if suppliers pass it on in full [2]. That is roughly €58 a year for a two-person household using 2,500 kWh, €81 for three people at 3,500 kWh and €104 for a family of four at 4,500 kWh [2]. Desk arithmetic: at €5.4 billion a year, every year of delay costs the federal budget nothing and costs a family of four about €104.

**The gap.** The Länder demand and the federal answer both say yes to the goal. Only one side holds the ledger it would come out of. Chancellor Merz has pointed to higher revenue from stronger growth; the finance ministry does not think that is enough to cover the shortfall [1].

**Verdict.** A demand without a funding line is a podium. A cost estimate without a decision is a print waiting for a vote. The day the cut becomes real is the day someone publishes where the €5.4 billion comes from. Until then, plan with the bill you have. Nothing here is advice.
