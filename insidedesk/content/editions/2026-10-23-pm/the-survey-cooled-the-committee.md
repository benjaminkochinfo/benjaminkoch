The S&P Global flash composite PMI for the euro area fell to 51.9 in October from 53.1 in September, its lowest since June and below every forecast in a Reuters poll, which had expected 52.7 [1]. Above 50 still means growth. Services fell to 51.7 and manufacturing to 52.4 [3].

**Where it weakened.** France's composite fell to 48.4 from 51.1, back into contraction, against 50.5 expected [2]. Firms there tied a sharp fall in demand to political uncertainty, and business confidence hit its lowest since 2012 outside the pandemic [1]. Germany kept growing, but more slowly, at 52.8 from 53.8 [2]. Hiring across the currency area almost stopped [1].

**The price detail.** This is the line for the European Central Bank. Input costs rose faster for a second month, driven by energy, fuel and transport [1]. Yet firms raised their own selling prices more slowly than in September, which points to margins being squeezed rather than costs passed on [1]. "There is still little evidence of second-round effects, which will be reassuring for the ECB," said Harry Chambers of Capital Economics [1].

**The good line.** New export orders rose for a third consecutive month, after nearly four and a half years of declines [1]. Chris Williamson of S&P Global said the reading is consistent with growth of about 0.3% a quarter, "still among some of the best performances seen over the past three years" [3].

**The committee.** The ECB meets on 28–29 October with its deposit rate at 2.50%. A Reuters poll earlier this month expected a hold next week and a quarter-point rise in December, with inflation running nearly double the 2% target [1]. Traders scaled back bets on further rises after the surveys [2]. Across the Atlantic, a December Fed hike is no longer fully priced either [4].

**What it means.** An energy shock that raises costs but not prices is painful for firms and, for now, less dangerous for inflation. A central bank that waits for evidence before acting is doing its job, not dithering. Nothing here is advice.
