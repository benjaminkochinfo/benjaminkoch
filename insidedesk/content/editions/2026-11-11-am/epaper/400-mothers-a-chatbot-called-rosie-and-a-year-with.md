A chatbot was supposed to be the cheap answer to a hard problem: support for anxious, tired new mothers at 3 a.m. A University of Maryland trial of 400 pregnant and postpartum patients from racial minority backgrounds suggests it is not enough on its own, Behavioral Health Business reports [1].

One group got access to Rosie, a generative AI chatbot trained on evidence-based mental-health content and built to flag possible self-harm for real-time review by a clinical social worker [1]. The control group received a free children's book every month for a year and could contact study staff [1]. The researchers' conclusion: the chatbot "did not improve the primary outcomes of anxiety or depression at 12 months compared with active control" [1].

The details are instructive. Participants earned $0.50 per question to the chatbot, up to $45, to keep them engaged [1]. Heavier users reported more anxiety but better sleep, and the authors could not say which came first [1]. Subgroup signals, such as anxiety 4.1 times as prevalent among those caring for babies under seven months, are flagged by the authors themselves as needing cautious reading [1].

The best sentence is theirs: perinatal mental health is "shaped by structural stressors, caregiving burden, financial strain, social support, and access to clinical care" [1]. A chatbot can answer questions. It cannot do the night feed, pay the rent or book the appointment. The researchers suggest such tools work better as an add-on than as the treatment [1]. That is not a defeat for technology. It is a reminder of what the help actually is: people, time and a working clinic.
