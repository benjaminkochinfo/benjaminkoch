The Annex reads the method behind the headline. Today's: Uppsala University says premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD), the severe form of premenstrual syndrome, "does not follow any universal pattern" but falls into three subtypes [1]. The paper, led by Ella Schleimann-Jensen and Erika Comasco, appeared in Nature Communications on Tuesday [2].

**The design.** Women with PMDD and healthy controls rated their symptoms every day for two menstrual cycles on an online version of a standard scale, the Daily Record of Severity of Problems, with 21 items each scored from 1 ("not at all") to 6 ("extreme") [2]. The diagnosis was prospective: symptoms had to rise by more than 50% from the follicular to the luteal phase [2]. Participants came from the screening phase of two Uppsala drug trials, one of escitalopram and one of a progesterone-receptor modulator, recruited through newspaper, campus and social media adverts [2].

**The result.** Statistical modelling of the diaries found three groups: severe PMDD with early onset, about two weeks before menstruation and peaking about five days before; severe PMDD with late onset, rising about ten days before and peaking around the start of menstruation; and a moderate late-onset group peaking about four days before [1]. Over time, rising anxiety tended to be followed by more irritability, low mood and mood swings [1]. The paper calls anxiety "a central symptom" in how the episode unfolds [2].

**The footnotes that matter.** The press release says 90 women with PMDD and 40 controls [1]; the paper reports 44 controls and 134 participants in total [2]. Small, but it is the kind of thing a reader should check. The authors write that "no separate sample size calculation was performed" for this observational analysis and that it "was not separately preregistered" [2]. The dynamic model was limited to four core symptoms because adding more would risk "unreliable estimates given the sample size" [2]. And the strict 50% threshold, they note, "may limit generalizability to less severe PMDD presentations" [2].

**What the authors themselves say.** Comasco is plain that the study "cannot explain why these differences occur", and that more research is needed before the patterns can predict one person's course [1].

**For you.** If you, or someone you live with, recognise this, the most useful tool in the paper is the cheapest: a daily diary over two cycles. It is how the diagnosis was made here, and it gives a doctor something better than memory to work with.
