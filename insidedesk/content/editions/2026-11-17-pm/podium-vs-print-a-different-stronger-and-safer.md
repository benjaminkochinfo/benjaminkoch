**The podium.** On Tuesday morning Deutsche Bank responded to reporting by a consortium of journalists working from what CNBC described as more than 15 million leaked files [1][2]. The bank said the documents "date back several years", that the matters in focus go back "many years and in some cases as much as two decades", and that it had found no indication its current IT systems, account data or login credentials had been compromised [1]. Then came the ledger: "EUR 7bn of direct investments in our control environment between 2019-2025", credit ratings upgraded ten times in six years, and the sentence above: "a different, stronger and safer bank today" [1].

**The print.** The share price had its own reading. Deutsche Bank fell more than 6% in early Frankfurt trading [2]. At 15:40 CET it was still down 4.48% at €31.44, the weakest of the 40 DAX members, with 6,484,485 shares traded, more than any other member [3]. The reports, led by outlets including the Financial Times, Der Spiegel and Le Monde, cover alleged historic failures in risk management and financial-crime controls; the bank calls them largely historical [1][2].

**The gap.** The podium speaks in the past tense about the files and the present tense about the bank. The print does not dispute either tense. It prices the bit in between: the fines, reviews and management hours a story like this can still cost, whatever its vintage. CNBC, citing the FT, notes the bank has paid about €18.6 billion in settlements since 2012 [2]. Desk arithmetic: €7 billion over the seven years 2019 to 2025 is about €1 billion a year of control spending [1].

**For you.** If you hold the share, or a fund that does, the question is not whether the old files are embarrassing. It is whether they create new liabilities. That is answered by regulators and courts, slowly, not by a statement or a single afternoon. Nothing here is investment advice.
