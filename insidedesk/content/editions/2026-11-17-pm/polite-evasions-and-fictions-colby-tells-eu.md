Elbridge Colby, the US Under Secretary of War for Policy, joined EU defence ministers over lunch in Brussels on Tuesday for the council's session on transatlantic security [2]. His prepared remarks were unusually direct for a room of allies. "True allies, like true friends, are frank and direct when necessary. Polite evasions and fictions are not the marks of real partnership," he said [1].

The substance was a division of labour. The US is prioritising its own homeland and hemisphere and "deterrence by denial along the First Island Chain", Colby said; in Europe it wants allies "to take the lead in their conventional defense, with U.S. forces providing critical but more limited support" [1]. He praised NATO's commitment to 5% of GDP for defence and security, 3.5% of it on core military capabilities, and said Germany is moving to meet its commitments by 2029, six years early [1].

Then came the invoice clause. Washington is "more receptive" to EU-level defence efforts than past administrations, provided they are "non-exclusionary," he said; "we strongly oppose measures to exclude American firms from competition in European markets" [1]. He held up joint ventures and licensed co-production, citing Patriot missiles produced in Bavaria, and dismissed a "middle powers strategy" of building alternatives, using the F-35 as his example [1]. He also tied US capacity to deliver to Congress funding the President's defence budget [1].

The same council discussed security aspects of EU–China relations, focused on critical dependencies, and support for Ukraine's air defence with Ukraine's defence minister Yevhenii Khmara [2].

For you: strip out the praise and the speech is a procurement memo. The real questions are dull and decisive: who builds the missiles, where, and how fast. Europe's answer will be written in tender rules and production contracts, not in replies to speeches.
