The Annex reads the paper behind the headline. The headline says cannabis addiction goes with 57% higher odds of suicidal thoughts [1]. The paper, by researchers at the University of California, Merced, in the Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs, says that, with clauses worth reading [1].

**Clause one: the sample.** More than 415,000 American adults from the National Survey on Drug Use and Health, interviewed between 2014 and 2023 [1]. That is a decade in which recreational cannabis became legal in half of US states and products grew more potent [1].

**Clause two: the condition.** The study is about cannabis use disorder, difficulty controlling use even when it causes problems, not about everyone who uses cannabis [1]. The authors are explicit that occasional use is a different thing [1].

**Clause three: the result.** Adults with the disorder had 57% higher odds of reporting suicidal thoughts, and the link held after accounting for depression and psychological distress [1]. It appeared across age groups, sexes and ethnic backgrounds, and was higher among women and younger adults [1]. The surprise, in the lead author's words, was that the association "did not appear to strengthen over time" [1].

**Clause four: the small print.** This is an association from survey data [1]. It cannot say whether the disorder feeds the thoughts, whether underlying difficulties drive both, or whether something else explains the link [1]. Odds ratios are not percentages of people; a 57% rise in odds does not mean 57% of anyone.

**For you.** The senior author, Sidra Goldman-Mellor, puts it plainly: the raised likelihood is "not something that is widely understood" [1]. The practical ask is modest and institutional: when someone seeks help for cannabis use, ask directly about suicidal thoughts [1]. If that someone is you or a person close to you, the question is worth asking out loud, and worth asking of a professional.
