In October the Medical Services in Germany received 419,000 orders for care assessments, 69,000 more than a year earlier, according to figures their federal association gave the Augsburger Allgemeine [1]. Early numbers from individual states point to an even sharper rise in November [1]. Nobody announced a rush. The paperwork simply arrived.

The reason sits in the Bundestag. The coalition's care reform bill, debated at first reading on 5 November, combines partly higher contributions with cuts, and many changes are meant to apply from 1 January 2027 [1]. Among them: the relief amount of up to €131 a month for people in care grade 1 is to go, as is respite care, which family carers can claim when they themselves cannot provide care [1]. Recognition as needing care is likely to get harder overall [1].

The pressure behind the bill is plain arithmetic. In 2015 about 2.6 million people received benefits from statutory long-term care insurance; in 2025 it was more than six million [1]. The federal government propped up the system with loans of €0.5 billion in 2025 and €3.2 billion in 2026 [1].

For you, one sentence matters most. "Wer seinen Antrag also noch 2026 stellt, wird nach den bisherigen Kriterien bewertet", said Katrin Staffler, the federal government's commissioner for care: apply in 2026 and you are assessed under the old criteria, even if the visit comes in 2027 [1]. She has also asked for longer transition periods, objecting that a law passed in mid-December would leave families and home-care services two weeks to adjust [1].

The cost lands on the assessors. The Medical Service in Bavaria expects longer waits despite extra staff on standby, and says the rise goes "über die erwartbare Größenordnung hinaus", beyond what could be expected [1]. A reform meant to slow spending has, for now, sped up the queue. That is not a scandal; it is what happens when people read the timetable.
