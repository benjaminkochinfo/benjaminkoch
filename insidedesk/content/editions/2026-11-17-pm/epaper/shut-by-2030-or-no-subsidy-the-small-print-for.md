Germany pays for back-up power plants through tenders, and the rules of a tender are where policy actually lives. On Tuesday the economy ministry published an ordinance that fills in one of them [2]. It is dated 16 November, appears in the Federal Law Gazette as 2026 I No. 343a and applies from 18 November [1].

The question it answers is narrow and expensive: when an old coal unit is converted to burn gas, may the conversion costs count as if a new plant were being built, which is what a bid must show to clear the tenders' minimum investment thresholds [1]? The answer is yes only if the coal unit was already permanently shut on 22 July 2026, or is legally due to close before 1 January 2030 [1]. The same test applies to lignite and hard-coal units, large and small [1].

The reasoning, as the ministry put it, is that a conversion of a plant that would have stayed on the system beyond 2030 creates "no additional capacity" [3]. The first round had produced several awards for converted Leag lignite units, and criticism from environmental groups, municipal utilities and southern states [3]. Changing rules while a tender is running is legally contested, and losing bidders could sue [3].

For you, nothing changes on this winter's bill. What changes is what the levy and the budget will buy from the 2030s: capacity that is genuinely added, rather than old capacity with a new label.
