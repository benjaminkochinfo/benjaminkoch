By Tuesday afternoon Iran's navy had a count. Three oil tankers, it said, had caught fire after striking naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz since early Tuesday, the third in the eastern part of the strait [1]. The navy repeated that the strait is "closed to shipping" and told vessels to coordinate with Iran's armed forces, follow designated routes and keep their navigation systems switched on [1]. The Revolutionary Guards' navy has also called the US-backed southern route a "minefield of fire" [4].

Separately, a Panamanian-flagged gas tanker, the Tara Gas, was hit about seven nautical miles north of Khasab in Oman [2]. The accounts did not agree. The security monitor Vanguard and India's ports minister, Sarbananda Sonowal, said the crew of 25, 24 of them Indian, was safe; the British agency UKMTO reported a second strike, a fire on board and the crew "currently unaccounted for" [2]. Meanwhile a third American aircraft carrier, the USS Theodore Roosevelt, joined two others in the region [4].

Now look at the price. Brent crude was up 32 cents, or 0.4%, at $92.76 a barrel at 13:04 CET, and West Texas Intermediate rose 0.5% to $86.11 [3]. Over the month Brent has fallen 21% and WTI 18% [3]. Desk arithmetic: a 21% fall to $92.76 implies Brent started November somewhere near $117.

That is not indifference. It is a market reading a different document. The International Energy Agency said on Friday that the oil market will return to surplus in the fourth quarter as Middle East exports recover faster than demand, and the cost of shipping oil has dropped sharply as Gulf exporters push flows back towards pre-war levels [3]. Shipping data show more tankers making the passage, partly because of increased US security [4]. Each burning ship is a casualty and a claim. It is not, on its own, a change in how many barrels leave the Gulf.

The insurance ledger says the same thing in a drier voice. Gulf additional war-risk premiums have eased to 7–9% of hull value, with some cover written as low as 5%, even though there have been more than 110 claims with estimated payouts of around $2.5 billion, according to Lloyd's List figures reported by Insurance Business [5]. Before the war the rate was about 0.25%, Marsh's Marcus Baker said; as recently as mid-October brokers quoted 6–10% [5]. Desk arithmetic: on a tanker insured for $100 million, 8% is $8 million for one exposure, against $250,000 before the war.

Why cheaper while ships still burn? Capacity and competition. Beazley assembled a $1 billion marine war consortium at Lloyd's in April, a further Lloyd's facility followed in June, and some underwriters now return as much as half the premium to owners whose ships pass without incident [5]. Not everyone is relaxed: Marsh Re puts Middle East losses in the war, terrorism and political violence class above $2 billion against annual gross premiums of roughly $2.5 billion, which could make this the class's first loss-making year [5]. IUMI's Lars Lange argues war risk stays insurable only if it is priced well above peacetime levels [5].

The pressure points are therefore not where the speeches are. Tehran says the strait will not reopen until "the enemy accepts Iran's conditions," in the words of an army spokesperson [4]. Eurasia Group's Ian Bremmer expects Iran to keep escalating attacks on tankers and Washington to widen its blockade, and calls the assumption that economic pressure will force Tehran to accept better terms a "misread" [4]. Those are forecasts by others, and worth knowing. What actually moves your heating oil and petrol bill is duller: the export volumes the IEA counts, the OPEC+ quota meeting on 29 November [3], the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve, which fell to 276.4 million barrels last week, its lowest since August 1982 [3], and the premiums underwriters in London are willing to write.

For you: when the headline says "tanker on fire" and the price says "plus 0.4%", believe both. The first is a human and legal event for the crews and owners involved. The second is the sum of millions of contracts that care about the barrels still arriving. If the strait truly closes in practice, the export data and the insurance quotes will say so before any spokesperson does. Watch those, and you will not need to guess.
