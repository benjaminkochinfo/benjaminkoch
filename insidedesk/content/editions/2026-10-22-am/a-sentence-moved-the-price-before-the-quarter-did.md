On Wednesday, the most expensive sentence on Wall Street was not in an earnings release. After SpaceX posted its first profit as a listed company, Elon Musk said a merger of SpaceX and Tesla would be "a good idea" [1]. SpaceX closed 7.8% higher. Tesla, which had not yet reported a single number for the quarter, rose 3.4% [1]. A remark about a deal that does not exist moved a carmaker's shares more than many quarterly results move a share price.

Then the numbers arrived, and the mood went the other way. Tesla beat forecasts on both lines: 48 cents of adjusted earnings per share against 43 cents expected, and revenue of $28.05 billion against $27.88 billion [2]. Its shares first rose a little after the close and then slipped, trading about 2–3% lower in the evening [1][2]. Cboe noted that the fall came before the conference call had even started [3]. IBM also beat on earnings and revenue; its shares "initially popped" and were last down more than 3% [2]. Lam Research guided slightly above estimates and dropped more than 5% [2]. Earlier in the day, AT&T beat, raised its outlook and closed 6.5% lower [1].

**What the day was measuring.** None of this is irrational, and none of it is mysterious. A price is not a verdict on whether a company did well. It is a verdict on whether it did better than the story people had already paid for. Psychologists call that story a reference point: people judge an outcome as a gain or a loss relative to what they expected, not relative to zero. Wednesday showed the two ways a reference point is set. One is the numbers analysts publish before the release. The other is words: a founder's aside, a guidance range, a rival's plans.

By the time Tesla reported, the reference point had moved. A 3.4% rise built on a merger remark had already lifted the bar the quarter had to clear. The results beat the analysts' bar and still fell short of the new one. AT&T is the mirror image. Its quarter was solid by its own accounts, but the reference point was set by a competitor: SpaceX's plan to turn Starlink into a US mobile carrier [5]. AT&T's chief executive answered with plain arithmetic. He told analysts not to "over-rotate on what I would call one carrier of low-band spectrum" [5]. Arithmetic is slower than a headline. It usually wins in the end, but not on the day.

**Relief is measured against fear.** Meta offers the third lesson. Its revenue of $64.60 billion beat forecasts and the shares rose about 3% after hours [2][4]. One prominent analyst called its spending "better controlled than feared" [2]. Look at the release itself, though. Meta's 2026 capital spending range is now $135–145 billion, narrowed by raising the floor from $130 billion. Quarterly capital spending was $34.91 billion, and free cash flow was minus $2.12 billion [4]. The company is also creating a new class of non-voting shares, partly for "capital raising" [4]. The relief was real, but it was measured against the fear, not against the bill. "Better than feared" is a statement about the mood beforehand, and it says nothing about the cash.

**The insurance people buy before the news.** The calmest behaviour of the day was the least visible. Before Procter & Gamble reports on Thursday, options traders are pricing a move of about 3% either way, and put contracts at one strike are 1.5 times more active than normal, Cboe noted, "suggesting hedging may be in play" [3]. Honeywell had four times the usual number of puts at one strike for Friday's expiry [3]. People who know that the reference point can move without warning pay a small, known price beforehand to avoid a large, unknown one afterwards. That is not pessimism. It is good housekeeping.

**What you can take from it.** You do not need to trade anything to use this. When you see "shares fell despite strong results", there is a question to ask: what was the price measuring itself against? Usually the answer is a sentence: a guidance line, a chief executive's aside or a rival's announcement, and you can find it. When you hear "better than feared", ask what the fear was and then look at the number underneath. Meta's negative free cash flow was in its own press release [4]. AT&T's raised outlook was in the release that preceded a 6.5% fall [1][5].

Statements set expectations, but data settles them, and the settling takes longer than a trading day. The useful skill is not to react faster than other people. It is to read the release before you read the reaction. Nothing here is advice.
