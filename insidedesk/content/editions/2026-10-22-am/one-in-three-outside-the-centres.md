The Congolese health ministry's situation report for 19 October, published on 20 October, recorded 85 new confirmed cases of Bundibugyo Ebola, bringing the total since the outbreak began to 9,637, with 4,646 deaths [1]. That is the headline line. The more useful line is on page seven, under case management [1].

**The beds.** In North Kivu, 154 patients were admitted on 19 October and 95 left, ten of them cured [1]. That leaves 509 patients in hospital, 58 of them children under five [1]. The province's Ebola treatment and transit centres were 81.0% full, with 332 patients for 410 places; suspected-case beds were 86.5% full [1]. And 177 patients, or 34.8%, were being cared for in ordinary health facilities that are not Ebola centres [1]. The report lists that among its "défis", its challenges [1].

**The other provinces.** The picture elsewhere is the opposite. Ituri, which still accounts for 72.7% of all confirmed cases, had 431 patients and an occupancy rate of 42.5% [1]. Haut-Uélé had 29 patients for 169 beds (17.2%) and Tshopo 13 for 36 (36.1%) [1]. Bas-Uélé is the exception, with its three beds for confirmed patients all taken and 47 people in isolation while a proper centre at Buta's general hospital is still not ready [1]. Nationally, the tracker that compiles the bulletins counts 982 patients for 1,630 beds, or 60 per 100 [2].

**Why it matters.** Desk arithmetic: North Kivu took 59 more patients than it discharged in a single day. Each patient treated outside a dedicated centre is harder to isolate, and Ebola spreads through the bodily fluids that a ward has to contain. The province is also behind on the other side of the work: 79.7% of its 19,316 contacts were seen that day, below the 95% the response requires, and only 9 of 22 "rings" of disinfection and follow-up around new cases had been opened [1].

**The constructive reading.** Laboratories are keeping up: 514 samples were analysed on 19 October, 16.5% of them positive [1]. Across all provinces, 3,184 alerts came in and every one was checked and classified [1]. The surveillance machinery is doing its job, and the bottleneck has moved downstream, to beds, ambulances and staff in one province. That is a better problem than not knowing where the cases are. It is a problem solved by trucks, tents and rosters rather than by speeches, which is why the next bulletins' page seven is the one to read.

Nothing here is a reason for readers in Europe to change plans; the figures describe a response, not a risk to you.
