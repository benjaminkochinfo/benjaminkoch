Most studies of generosity ask people how generous they are. Mikael Elinder and Oscar Erixson used receipts instead. In a study published in The Economic Journal, they matched the military enlistment test scores of more than 1.2 million Swedish men, tested between 1969 and 1997, with tax records of charitable donations, which Swedish charities report automatically, with validated turnout in the 2009 European election and with the national vehicle register [1].

Across the population, one standard deviation higher on the test went with a 40% higher likelihood of giving to charity, 31% for voting and 14% for owning a car running on electricity, ethanol or gas [1]. Among 5,786 twin pairs, who share an upbringing, the links roughly halved but stayed: 26% for giving and 13% for voting [1]. Income did not explain much. Once education, income and family circumstances were accounted for, the green-car link was no longer statistically significant, while giving and voting held [1].

The limits are plain. The sample is mostly Swedish men born between 1951 and 1979, and helping a neighbour does not show up in any register [1]. The method is the point: the register tells you what people do, which is not always what they report.
