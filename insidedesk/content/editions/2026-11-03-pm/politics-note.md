**The podium.** On Monday, standing in front of the newly reopened Reflecting Pool, the president gave his closing message before today's midterms [1][4]. Among the reflecting-pool joints and the grass, he turned to the Gulf: "We control the Strait of Hormuz. We control it, and strongly control it." He added that, as the Wall Street Journal had reported, the United States "took out last week more oil than at any time in the history of the Strait" [1]. We have not been able to check that report, so we leave it to the Journal.

**The print.** The dull count is easier to check. By 16:24 Berlin time today, the tracker hormuz.now had logged five AIS-visible commercial crossings since midnight UTC: two inbound, three outbound, two of them tankers [2]. Its rolling seven-day average is 9.0 crossings a day. Its pre-crisis baseline is 84.8 [2]. Desk arithmetic: the week's traffic is running at about a tenth of normal. The counter warns that its day is still in progress, and ships with their transponders off are not in it. The official IMF PortWatch figure lags by days [2].

**The regulator.** The shipping world's own institution was blunter on Monday. Arsenio Dominguez, secretary-general of the International Maritime Organization, said there was "no confirmation that there are no mines in the region" and that the IMO was "not recommending anyone to go through the Strait of Hormuz" [3]. The designated lanes are not in operation. Ships that do cross use routes north through Iranian waters or south near Oman with US naval escorts, arrangements the IMO has not adopted [3]. He counted about 30 attacks on ships in and around the strait in October, and about 6,000 seafarers still aboard 350 to 400 ships in the Gulf [3].

**The gap.** Both statements can be partly true. Escorts are a form of control, and the president's own speech said a deal "could happen" [1]. But control that moves a tenth of the usual traffic is control of a corridor, not of a market. For you, the useful number is not the adjective. It is the crossing count, and the day the IMO changes its advice. Polls close from midnight Berlin time; this desk makes no forecast about them.
