The numbers from Florida's Atlantic coast read like a typo. Grant-Valkaria in Brevard County recorded 23.69 inches of rain, Malabar 23.44 and Palm Bay 19.75, according to preliminary National Weather Service reports compiled by WKMG in Orlando [1]. Desk conversion: 23.69 inches is about 602 millimetres. Some parts of the county saw a 1-in-100-year flood, the station's chief meteorologist wrote [1].

[[chart:brevard-rain-03pm]]

**It was a local storm, and that is the point.** The heaviest totals sit in a strip of southern Brevard. Melbourne had 13.11 inches and Kennedy Space Center 9.74; inland, Lake Harney in Seminole County had 8.44 and Eatonville in Orange County 7.43 [1]. The cause was unglamorous: an upper-level disturbance and persistent easterly winds feeding rounds of rain from Sunday [1]. One forecaster said the storm "exceeded everyone's expectations" [2].

**The machinery worked before the water went down.** At least 18 polling locations were moved across seven Florida counties, from Brevard to Palm Beach, NBC News reported, and Governor Ron DeSantis declared a state of emergency in seven Atlantic counties [2]. In Oklahoma a tornado with peak winds of about 100 mph made one polling place unsafe, and Utah and Colorado are letting voters cut off by floodwater return ballots electronically [2]. Nobody made a speech about resilience. Officials found other buildings.

**What the rain does to the vote is not knowable today.** One study of elections from 1948 to 2000 found turnout fell by about 0.8 percentage points for every inch of rain above a county's average, but experts told NBC the effect is shrinking as more people vote early or by post [2]. This desk will not guess at a result.

For you, the practical lesson is the one WKMG gave its viewers: floodwater is deeper and more dangerous than it looks, and you should never drive or walk through it [1].
