In August, prosecutors in Keelung indicted nine people, including staff at the local units of Nvidia and Super Micro, over 74 Nvidia B300 servers allegedly diverted to Chinese buyers for a profit of more than NT$600 million (US$18.5 million) [1]. None of them faces an export charge. The case rests on breach of trust and forged documents, because Taiwanese law had no offence that fitted [1].

That is the gap a bill from the ruling Democratic Progressive Party wants to close. Its amendment to the Foreign Trade Act, heard by parliament's economics committee on Monday, would ban exports of specified chips, key wafer materials, components and chipmaking equipment to mainland China without government approval [1]. Violators would face up to ten years in prison and fines of NT$5–10 million, and the same would apply to shipments routed through a third country [1].

The bill's fate is uncertain. The opposition Kuomintang and Taiwan People's Party together hold a majority, and neither has said where it stands [1].

The constructive point is about where enforcement belongs. A company's sales rules can slow smuggling; a criminal statute, with courts and a public record, is what makes it costly. Since 2024, servers have reached China via Thailand, Indonesia, Japan and Hong Kong [1]. Each of those routes ends at a customs form somebody signed.
