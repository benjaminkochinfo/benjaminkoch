A team led by André Mata at the University of Lisbon, with colleagues at Linköping University, asked a plain question: do we avoid political conversations because we assume the other side won't listen? [1] The work is in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology.

In surveys of 198 people in Portugal and 402 in Sweden, participants rated their own willingness to hear contrary views, then guessed how supporters of their least liked party would answer [1]. They rated themselves as more receptive than average and consistently underestimated their opponents, when set against those opponents' actual self-reports [1]. The lower the guess, the more frustrating and stressful a conversation was expected to be, and the less willing people were to have one [1].

In an experiment with 600 Swedes, describing an opponent as receptive made people more willing to talk politics with them, and even to chat about hobbies or buy their vintage furniture [1]. In a last study, 120 Portuguese students who saw the real survey answers after each guess revised their view of opponents upwards and became more willing to talk [1].

The authors note that the causation may also run the other way, and that multi-party Europe is not two-party America [1]. Still, on a polarised election night, the cheapest correction on offer is a fact: the other side, asked properly, says it is listening.
