Researchers at Columbia University asked 50,000 people in 92 countries ten questions about how they are doing: competence, optimism, self-esteem, engagement, vitality and more. The results, published in *Social Indicators Research*, point to young people as the group dragging global scores down, mainly through lower vitality, emotional stability and resilience [1].

There is a quieter finding that gets less attention. Relationships, meaning, competence, engagement and self-esteem were the highest-scoring dimensions [1]. People tend to rate their own lives better than the world around them, which the authors link to the familiar "optimism gap" [1].

The everyday factors behave as you would expect. Being in work, earning more and living with other people all went with better scores, and there was no point at which more resources stopped helping. Each extra level of education was linked to higher well-being, and hybrid workers scored better than fully remote ones [1].

The authors say country rankings should not be compared, and they note that the people who volunteered for an online survey may not be typical [1]. Lead author Kai Ruggeri wants "specific commitments" in place of what he calls "vague platitudes" [1].

The plain lesson is an old one. Payslips, classrooms and other people in the house are mental-health policy too, even when nobody puts them under that heading.
