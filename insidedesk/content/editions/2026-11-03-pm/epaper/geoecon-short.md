The US Treasury's sanctions office has issued General License 135, and the president has used a waiver under this year's Russia and Iran sanctions act [1]. Together they authorise some sale, delivery, offloading and import of Russian-origin diesel that the sanctions regulations would otherwise forbid [1]. With diesel dear, traders are asking whether the trade is open again.

A note from the law firm Seyfarth Shaw, published on Monday, is a useful corrective [1]. First, the licence "does not necessarily override" the Ending Importation of Russian Oil Act, a separate statute whose restrictions can only be lifted with notice to Congress and a chance for Congress to object [1]. Second, the EU, the UK and others keep their own restrictions, and a diesel cargo touches banks, insurers, shipowners and ports, each under its own regime. There is "little indication" that key allies plan to relax theirs [1]. Third, the move has met bipartisan opposition in Congress, so a contract signed under today's relief could have to be unwound tomorrow [1].

The firm's line is worth keeping: "The critical question is not simply whether a transaction is lawful today, but whether it can be completed if the regulatory environment changes tomorrow" [1]. A licence opens one door. The insurer, the bank and the port each hold a key to the others. Nothing here is legal or investment advice.
