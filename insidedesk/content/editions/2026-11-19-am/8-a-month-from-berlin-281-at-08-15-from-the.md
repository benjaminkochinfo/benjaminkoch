Wednesday was the day of verdicts on the coalition's electricity-tax cut, and they arrived in neat, quotable pairs. "Überfällig", said the left; "kleiner Hüpfer", said the Greens; "abenteuerlich", said the director of the IW institute about the way it is paid for [2]. Thursday morning brought the more useful thing: an actual sum. According to an example calculation by the comparison portal Verivox, reported by MDR, a family of three saves just under €8 a month from the cut, and someone living alone about €3 [1].

Desk arithmetic: €8 a month is roughly €96 a year for that family. It is real money, and it is also small next to the part of the bill that is set not in the Chancellery but on the exchange.

## The other author

For delivery on Thursday, the German-Luxembourg day-ahead price averages €224.83 per megawatt hour across the day, according to SMARD, the Federal Network Agency's data platform [3]. Inside that average the day has a shape you will recognise from your own kitchen. The cheapest quarter-hour came at 02:15, at €166.52; the dearest at 08:15, at €281.00, when the country is making coffee and starting machines [3]. The hour from 08:00 to 09:00 averaged €276.45, and every hour from 15:00 to 20:00 averaged above €250 [3].

Desk arithmetic: the 08:15 quarter-hour cost €114.48 per megawatt hour more than the 02:15 one. The first 19 delivery days of November average €239.01, against €169.48 for the whole of October, a rise of about 41%. Thursday sits below that November average; it is not a cheap day, merely a less expensive one than the record stretch around 10 November, when Tuesday averaged €351.48 [3].

Your supplier buys ahead and smooths these quarter-hours into a tariff, which is why the averages matter: next spring's tariff letter is being written now, in mornings like this one.

## What the critics actually asked for

Read past the adjectives and the reactions converge on one dull demand: permanence. The consumer federation's Ramona Pop called the cut an important first step that "must now be secured permanently as quickly as possible" [2]. The retailers' association HDE warned that a one-year cut would simply fizzle out [2]. Monika Schnitzer, chair of the German Council of Economic Experts, said the time limit created "no planning certainty" and few incentives for long-term investment [2]. The SPD's chief budget spokesman, Thorsten Rudolph, called the funding, a lower-than-expected bill for renewable-energy payments next year, "no permanent financing" [2].

The defence came from North Rhine-Westphalia's premier, Hendrik Wüst: Germany passes annual tax laws for a year at a time, "that is why they are called annual tax laws" [2]. Ifo's Clemens Fuest called the cut economically well founded, because the tax "hampers electrification" [2].

The contrarian point is that both camps are right about different clocks. A one-year cut is a fine invoice adjustment and a poor investment signal. Nobody installs a heat pump on the strength of a single tax year.

## The fuel behind the price

Gas-fired plants set the marginal electricity price across much of the region [6], and gas gave some help on Wednesday: the Dutch TTF benchmark closed at €81.11 per megawatt hour, down 5.03% on the day [5]. Oil was steady overnight. Brent traded at $92.12 a barrel at 03:37 CET, up 0.18%, after falling 1.3% on Wednesday; WTI was at $83.17, after a 3.2% drop to its lowest since 26 August [4]. Reuters attributes the slide to expectations of a supply surplus, with crude shipments through the Strait of Hormuz back to roughly pre-war levels, and to a larger-than-expected US stock build of 3.4 million barrels [4]. Iranian media reported on Wednesday that three tankers east of the strait were disabled by mines [4], a reminder that "pre-war volumes" and "peace" are not the same word.

For you, the reading is modest: the tax cut is small and certain, the wholesale price large and unpriced until it is. Nothing here is investment advice.
