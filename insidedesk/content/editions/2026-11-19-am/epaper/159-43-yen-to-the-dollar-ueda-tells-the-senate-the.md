Kazuo Ueda went to the Japanese Senate's financial affairs committee on Thursday to present the Bank of Japan's semiannual report on currency and monetary control [1]. The message, as summarised by FXStreet, was steady: underlying inflation roughly aligns with the 2% target, and the bank plans to keep raising rates because monetary conditions remain accommodative [2].

The sharper lines were about the currency. Ueda said a weak yen could cause an inflation overshoot by pushing up prices across many goods, and that exchange-rate swings may now matter more for inflation because companies pass on costs more actively [2]. He called it difficult to determine the response if weak domestic demand and a falling yen coexist [2]. Rising crude prices and a weak yen, he added, may dent household real incomes [2].

The yen stood at 159.43 per dollar in AFP's Asia snapshot, from 159.29 the day before [3]. Ueda also said the pace and timing of increases depend on how likely the bank's baseline projections are to materialise [2]: a central banker's way of saying no dates.

What is reassuring here is the procedure, not the promise. A governor explains himself to parliament twice a year, on the record, with a written report behind him [1]. If you hold yen savings or pay yen invoices, the currency is the variable he named; read his next statement for it. Nothing here is investment advice.
