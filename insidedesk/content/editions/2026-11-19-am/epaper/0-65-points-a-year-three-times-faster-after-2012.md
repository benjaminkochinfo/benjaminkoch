Intolerance of uncertainty, the tendency to struggle when important information is unknown or outcomes cannot be predicted, rose steadily among college students in the United States and Canada between 1992 and 2023, according to a meta-analysis in JAMA Psychiatry by researchers at Columbia and Boston University [1]. The team analysed 138 studies covering 35,296 participants who completed the standard 27- or 12-item scale [1].

Average scores rose by about 0.65 points a year over the whole period, and the rise ran about three times faster from 2012 to 2023 than from 1992 to 2011 [1]. "IU is not itself a health outcome or a root cause of disease," said senior author Katherine Keyes, but an intermediary process that contributes to anxiety [1].

The design answers one objection. Rising anxiety in surveys is often put down to young people knowing the vocabulary. Intolerance of uncertainty is a term few outside psychology use, which the authors say makes the trend "less vulnerable to that criticism" [1]. They list possible pressures, from smartphones and academic strain to loneliness and economic instability, without claiming which one did it [1].

The constructive line is theirs too: effective treatments that reduce intolerance of uncertainty already exist [1]. For you, the everyday version is unglamorous. Tolerating a plan with blanks in it, a reply that has not come yet, a week not fully mapped, is a skill. Skills can be practised, and the evidence says clinics know how to teach this one.
