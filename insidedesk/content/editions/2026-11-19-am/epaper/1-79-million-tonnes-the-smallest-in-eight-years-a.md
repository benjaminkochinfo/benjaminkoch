South Africa's 2026-27 wheat harvest is forecast at 1.79 million tonnes, the lowest in about eight years, The National reported on Wednesday [1]. The Crop Estimates Committee's third winter-crop estimate put production 1% below its September figure and 6% below the previous season; the national average yield, about 3.67 tonnes per hectare, is also an eight-year low [1].

The weather did much of the work. Wandile Sihlobo, chief economist at the Agricultural Business Chamber, attributed weaker yields partly to drier conditions in the Western Cape, which accounts for roughly two-thirds of the country's winter wheat plantings [1]. FNB's Dawie Maree pointed to drought in the Swartland in particular [1]. Harvesting is gathering pace: by 30 October farmers had delivered about 333,679 tonnes to commercial silos, with quality "relatively fair" so far [1].

The contrarian point is in the import ledger. South Africa already buys roughly half its wheat abroad, and Sihlobo expects imports of about 2 million tonnes this season [1]. Simphiwe Madikizela of Unisa's business school cautioned that a smaller harvest does not necessarily mean a supply crisis; the immediate risk is higher import costs and food-price pressure, not empty shelves [1]. Global supplies remain plentiful, according to Sihlobo, though logistics problems have left stocks piling up around the Black Sea [1].

For you, even far from Cape Town: a drought on one coast is increasingly a freight and currency question for someone else's bakery. The dull machinery of grain contracts and import tariffs is what keeps it from becoming more.
