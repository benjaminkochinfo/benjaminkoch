The US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security released the text of its remote-access rule on 16 November; it takes effect on publication on 19 November, law firm Sidley Austin writes [1]. It aims at data centres outside the United States that provide significant compute to companies headquartered in China, Macau or other arms-embargoed countries [1].

The threshold is specific. A licence is needed to send advanced GPUs to a data centre anywhere when the sender has reason to know it, alone or with "linked" sites, will provide such companies more than 12 million units of total processing performance, roughly the output of 300 of the most advanced commercial GPUs, Sidley says [1]. No licence exceptions are available beyond a narrow carve-out for commercial recommender systems [1]. Existing arrangements get a 90-day grace period [1]. Separately, most transfers to or within Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil now need a licence, with an exception for US-headquartered firms that report monthly [1].

Tencent's answer came on its results call. "Our cloud business today is primarily running inference," said chief strategy officer James Mitchell, adding he saw no impact on cloud revenue [2]. President Martin Lau called the measures "unfortunate" and said they would likely slow the company's next model in the near term [2].

Export control works by forms, thresholds and reporting, not by speeches. Watch the licence applications, not the adjectives.
