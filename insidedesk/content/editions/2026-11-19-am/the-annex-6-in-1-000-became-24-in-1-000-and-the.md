A University of Manchester-led study of more than 6.2 million children and young people in England, published in The Lancet Psychiatry, found that recorded anxiety and depression in primary care quadrupled between 2001 and 2019 [1]. Among 10- to 18-year-olds, first presentations to a family doctor rose from 6 in every 1,000 a year in 2001 to 24 in every 1,000 in 2019 [1]. The researchers linked anonymised GP records from the CPRD database with hospital admissions from 2001 to 2023 and tracked almost 380,000 young people after a first recorded symptom or diagnosis [1].

The useful part is the test. A popular explanation holds that the rise is mostly "overmedicalisation" of ordinary teenage distress, or lower thresholds for seeking help. If that were true, the authors argue, the risk of serious outcomes among those coming forward should have fallen [1]. It did not. Young people with anxiety or depression stayed consistently more likely than comparable peers to be admitted to hospital for self-harm, alcohol misuse or drug misuse, throughout the study [1].

Other lines in the ledger: those presenting were getting younger; increases were similar in richer and poorer neighbourhoods; recorded neurodevelopmental conditions such as autism and ADHD among them nearly tripled; and they were more than twice as likely to be referred to specialist services [1]. The share prescribed antidepressants halved between 2001 and 2005 and has been stable since, but courses for those who start run longer, with prescriptions per child up by around 50% [1].

"Children presenting to primary care today are not becoming less clinically vulnerable," said study author Dr Vicky Taxiarchi [1]. Tamsin Ford of Cambridge said there is "little evidence" that problems among those seeing their GP are milder now [1].

Now the annex, the part about what to do with it. Records show who came forward, not everyone who struggled, and a cohort cannot say why need rose. But it settles a planning question. A service sized for a passing fashion will be cut when the fashion fades; a service sized for a population that has grown fourfold needs staff, rooms and referral routes that work. If you are a parent, the finding is also a permission: a GP appointment for a child's persistent low mood or worry is not an overreaction. It is the front door the system now has to keep open.
