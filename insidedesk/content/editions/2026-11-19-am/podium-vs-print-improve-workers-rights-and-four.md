**The podium.** On Wednesday the federal cabinet adopted a package of labour-law changes that the coalition committee had agreed in principle in July [2]. Alexander Schweitzer, the labour minister, said the aim was "to keep jobs in Germany, make new hires possible and set the course for future-oriented jobs" (translated) [1][2]. He was keen to add a second half: "we are not only creating flexibility for companies, but also improving workers' rights" (translated) [1].

**The print.** The bill itself is more specific than either sentence. Staff hired by 31 December 2030 could be kept on fixed-term contracts without an objective reason for up to four years, up from two, and the contract could be extended six times within that period, also double today's limit [2]. Re-hiring on a fixed term with a former employer would be allowed, and the agreement could be made in text form rather than on paper [1][2]. For employees earning more than 1.75 times the pension contribution ceiling, about €180,000 a year according to tagesschau, a court could end the job against a severance payment without the employer giving reasons [1][2]. Bakeries and public libraries get more Sunday hours [1].

**The rights half.** It is in the text too. The bill tightens the rules against using the European company form, the SE, to avoid board-level co-determination [1]. The government says five of six large SEs have no parity co-determination, affecting 480,000 employees [2].

**The gap.** Both lines are true; they just weigh differently. The flexibility is counted in years and extensions, and tagesschau notes it will mostly land on career starters, who are often hired on fixed terms [2]. The protection is counted in a legal form most workers have never heard of. Read the bill, not the balance in the sentence.

**For you.** If you are about to sign a first contract in 2027, the clause to read is the end date and how many times it can move.
