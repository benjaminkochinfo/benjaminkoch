On Tuesday, three official voices described the Strait of Hormuz. Each was confident, and they did not agree with one another. The lesson is not that one of them is lying. It is that a statement and a count are different instruments, and only one of them is built to be checked.

**Tehran's version.** Foreign minister Abbas Araghchi told Iraqi television that indirect talks with the United States continue through Pakistan and Qatar, and that the strait will stay closed unless Washington accepts Iran's conditions [1]. Iran has offered a seven-day plan under which the waterway would fully reopen on the sixth day. Araghchi said Washington had accepted the conditions but wanted "additional provisions" [1]. "The negotiating table is still there," he said [1].

**Washington's version.** In an interview with Network18, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said that at any given time the traffic and energy moving through the strait are "80 to 100 per cent equal" to what they were before the conflict [2]. He also said US forces destroy the "overwhelming majority" of drones and missiles fired at ships each night, but that "every now and then" one gets through [2]. He said the onus is on Iran to stop, and that economic pressure will continue until it does [2].

**The Guards' version.** The same day, the Revolutionary Guards said their naval mines had "halted" two tankers at what they call an illegal crossing point in the southern part of the strait, and that a third tanker carrying more than two million barrels of crude had exploded and caught fire after hitting a mine [3]. AFP reported the claims from the Guards' own statements; on Tuesday there was no independent confirmation in that report [3].

**What has actually been counted.** A shut strait, a strait at 80 to 100% and a strait mined at its southern exit cannot all be true at once. The most recent independent measure in the record is less dramatic than any of them. The UK Maritime Trade Operations centre, a Royal Navy office that logs ships and incidents, counted 254 transits in the week to 14 October, 125 outbound and 129 inbound [4]. The Guardian put that at roughly 70% below pre-conflict levels [4]. UKMTO also recorded 12 attacks in the seven days to 16 October [4]. The shiptracker Kpler counted at least 18 vessels reportedly attacked over two weeks [5].

So the strait is neither shut nor normal. It is open, thin and dangerous. That is a boring answer, and it is the only one with a method behind it.

**What the price did with all this.** The oil market heard all three versions on one day and reacted as you would expect. Brent touched $111.99, a five-month high, then settled at $108.79, down 76 cents on the day [5]. The swing was more than $6 within the session [6]. An unconfirmed report that a deal was close knocked prices down as much as 3.3%, and a diplomat involved in the talks then said no agreement was near [6]. By early Wednesday in Asia, Brent was back around $109.52 [6]. In other words, the price moved on the speeches and came back to rest on the risk. Analysts at ING called it a market "pricing in a significant risk premium" [5].

**Why the dull count matters more.** Each statement on Tuesday served its speaker. Tehran needs the strait to look closed so that reopening it is worth something in the talks. Washington needs it to look open so that pressure on Iran looks cheap. The Guards need their mines to look effective. None of this makes the statements worthless. They tell you what each side wants to trade. But none of them tells you how many ships went through yesterday.

The transit count does. It is published by an office with no seat at the table. It comes out at a regular interval, and if it moves you can see by how much. The same is true of the insurance quotes shipowners pay and the queues of tankers at anchor. These are the invoices of the conflict, and they change more slowly and more honestly than the statements do.

**The constructive reading.** There is a real negotiation, with real intermediaries and a written plan with a numbered day for reopening [1]. Intermediaries, a written plan and a date are the dull scaffolding that agreements are built on. A deal, if it comes, will show up first in the weekly count, then in the price. If it does not come, the count will show that too. Either way, you will not need to take anyone's word for it.

Until then, the useful habit is to treat every confident sentence about Hormuz as a bid in a negotiation, and the weekly count of ships as the receipt.
