Marius Vollberg and Jörg Gross of the University of Zurich gave participants a choice between 7 points for themselves or 5, knowing only a range for what another person would get: 1 to 3 points or 3 to 5 [1]. Then they asked them to guess the other person's actual payout, and paid them for accuracy [1].

People who took the 7 tended to report the two options as more similar for the other person than they were. People who took the 5 tended to inflate the good their choice did [1]. The distortions appeared even when the guesses were made before the choice, and even when the choice was forced, which points to preferences rather than after-the-fact excuses [1].

The finding with consequences came last. When participants were shown a previous player's estimates first, only the estimate that made selfishness look harmless changed what they did: they became more selfish [1]. The generous distortion did not spread [1]. The authors note limits: abstract tasks, mainly US participants, and large differences between individuals; fewer than 10% produced the full selfish pattern [1].

The paper was published on 1 October; it appears here because its point sharpens the edition's other stories about convenient numbers. When the consequences of a choice are vague, ask for the range, not someone's reading of it.
