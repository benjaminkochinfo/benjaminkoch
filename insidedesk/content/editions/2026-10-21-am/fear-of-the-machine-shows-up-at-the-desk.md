A study led by researchers at Washington State University followed more than 600 workers across US industries, including finance and construction, over six years [1]. It was published in the journal Occupational Health Science [1].

**What it measured.** Not ordinary job insecurity, the worry of losing one job at one firm, but what the lead author, psychology professor Tahira Probst, calls occupation insecurity: the fear that technology will transform or erase a whole line of work [1]. A bartender who loses a job can find another bar. A medical coder whose occupation is automated cannot simply move to another employer [1].

**What it found.** Workers who worried that automation would make their occupation obsolete were more likely to justify behaviour they would normally call inappropriate, such as turning up late or taking a colleague's lunch [1].

**What the author does not claim.** The study was not designed to estimate how many jobs AI will replace [1]. Probst also told Spokane Public Radio that "a single study doesn't prove anything" and asked for replication in places with different labour protections and AI rules [1]. That caution is part of the result.

**The constructive point.** Her main takeaway is about management: "how organizations roll out these AI tools matters enormously" [1]. If employees are left to make sense of the change alone, the fear does its own damage, whether or not it is justified [1]. A clear plan for the rollout, written down and explained to staff, is routine office work. It may also be the best protection against the fear doing harm.
