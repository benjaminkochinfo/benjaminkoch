One in four students responding to a University of Bath survey said they had used generative AI for mental-health support in the previous six months, according to a peer-reviewed study in JMIR Mental Health published on Monday [1]. Among students with very low wellbeing, more than a third had done so [1]. Students used the tools for advice when distressed, to understand their mental health and as a personal therapist or coach [1].

Read the method first. 2,020 students took part, 9.1% of those invited, at a single university, and the researchers say it may not represent students across the UK [1]. A separate US survey of 67,477 students at 107 colleges found 7% had received counselling or emotional support from an AI chatbot in the past year, the first time it asked, Inside Higher Ed reported [2].

"The reality is that people are already using AI for their mental health, whether or not universities and healthcare services are ready for it," said lead author Jeffrey Lambert [1]. Bath has published guidance on benefits, risks and when to seek further help [1].

That is the calm response: not a ban, not a celebration, but a leaflet that says what the tool cannot do and where the human help is. A risk with instructions is smaller than a risk without them.
