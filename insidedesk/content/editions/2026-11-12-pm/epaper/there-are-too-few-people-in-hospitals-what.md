"Something's wrong with the data. There are too few people in hospitals," a colleague told the Danish epidemiologist Caroline Klint Johannesen while they analysed health records for last winter, she recalls in a Nature news report published on Thursday [1]. Nothing was wrong. On 1 October 2025, Denmark began vaccinating pregnant women against respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) nationwide; the mother's antibodies cross the placenta and protect the baby for roughly its first six months [1]. High-risk newborns whose mothers were not vaccinated, including premature babies, received monoclonal antibody injections instead [1]. Infant hospital admissions "plummeted", her study in The Lancet Regional Health – Europe found [1].

The scale of what is being prevented is large. The World Health Organization estimates that RSV puts roughly 3.6 million children under five in hospital each year and kills about 100,000, 97% of them in low- and middle-income countries [1]. In older adults the risk is underappreciated; the US CDC estimates between around 10,000 and 25,000 deaths in each of the past two winters [1].

Since European regulators approved the long-acting antibody nirsevimab in 2022, a second antibody and three vaccines for adults have followed, one suitable in pregnancy, and dozens of countries now run programmes [1]. From "no mechanism to prevent RSV to an embarrassment of riches", the US paediatrician Buddy Creech says [1]. The remaining work is unglamorous: appointments, cold chains, reminders, and money for the countries that carry most of the deaths.
