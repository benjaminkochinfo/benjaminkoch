The Bundestag on Thursday passed, by a large majority, a law meant to improve support for children from daycare age onwards [1]. Its core is a set of binding and largely uniform tests to spot language weaknesses and other developmental gaps early [1]. Until now, the Länder have handled this very differently; the federal government can provide money, but carrying out the tests is the Länder's job [1].

The debate split along a familiar seam. Coalition speakers said the law would give children from disadvantaged families more chances. The Left and the Greens said it delivers more diagnosis but too little money for staff and care. The AfD backed it in principle but doubted it would work, given shortages of skilled workers and overstretched municipalities [1].

The sharper objection comes from the Länder. Saxony's education minister, Clemens (CDU), said the Bundesrat therefore wants to call the mediation committee: if the federal government decides daycare should meet higher standards, "then it must also pay for the educators needed" [1].

That is the constructive reading of the row. A test is only as good as the person who acts on the result, and that person is on a payroll somewhere. The law has settled what should be measured; the mediation committee, if it is called, will be where someone settles who signs the salary slips. For parents, nothing changes at the nursery door this week.
