Munich Re, the world's largest reinsurer, reported on Thursday that natural catastrophes cost it €514 million in the third quarter, after retrocession and before tax [1]. In the same quarter last year it booked a release of €47 million, meaning old provisions turned out larger than needed [1]. So the summer of 2026 was not cheap for the people who insure the insurers. And yet the company called its major-loss expenditure "below average expectations" [1].

**The ratio that tells you.** All major losses, natural and man-made, came to €646 million, against €118 million a year earlier [1]. That was 14.9% of net insurance revenue in property-casualty reinsurance, below the 18% the company budgets for in an average quarter [1]. The combined ratio, the share of each premium euro spent on claims and costs, was 80.0%, against an unusually low 62.7% a year earlier [1]. Munich Re now expects the full-year figure in that business to be below 80% and has raised its 2026 profit target from €6.3bn to €6.6bn [1].

**The costliest event was not the weather.** The largest single natural disaster of the quarter for Munich Re was the El Choco earthquake in Colombia, with nominal losses of about €0.3bn [1]. That is worth remembering on a desk called Climate & Weather: the reinsurer's nature bill is a portfolio of storms, floods, fires and the earth moving under a city, and a single event can outweigh a season of weather.

**Where the growth came from.** The quarter's fastest growth was not in catastrophe cover but in life and health reinsurance, where revenue rose to €3,769m from €2,868m; the company says it is expanding in "less volatile segments" [1]. Its solvency ratio stood at 301%, against a Solvency II target of more than 200% [1].

Desk arithmetic: €514m in natural-catastrophe losses against a €47m release a year earlier is a swing of €561m in one line of one quarter, and the company still raised its annual target. That is what pricing, reserves and a diversified book are for: the dull machinery that turns a bad week somewhere in the world into a budgeted number in Munich. Nothing here is investment advice.
