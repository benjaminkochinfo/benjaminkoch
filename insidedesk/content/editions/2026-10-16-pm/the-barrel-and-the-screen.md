There are two oil prices this week, and they are not having the same week.

The first lives on the screen. Brent futures for December delivery settled on Thursday at $105.92 a barrel, fourteen cents lower on the day, and were trading at $105.14 late on Friday morning, European time [4][3]. That is the number on the evening news, the one that moves in cents and gets a verb: eased, edged, steadied.

The second lives in the tank. The U.S. Energy Information Administration's Europe Brent spot price, a physical barrel loaded at the North Sea, was $126.55 on Wednesday, the latest day published [1]. Different instruments, a day apart, so do not subtract them to the cent. But a gap of roughly twenty dollars between the barrel you can load now and the barrel you can load in December is not a rounding error. It is the market telling you, in cash, what the present costs compared with the future it hopes for.

[[chart:brent-wti-spot]]

**What the gap is made of.** The present is expensive because the corridor is narrow. About 12.6 million barrels a day moved through the Strait of Hormuz on 13 October, according to Kpler data cited by BBC Verify, down from about 15 million at the end of September and against a pre-war baseline of roughly 17 million [5]. Attack reports to the UK's maritime trade agency hit a weekly record of twelve between 5 and 11 October [5]. A December contract can price a negotiated reopening. A refinery that needs crude next week cannot run on a negotiation.

**Why WTI is the tell.** On the same Wednesday, EIA's WTI spot, the American inland barrel at Cushing, was $101.91: $24.64 below Brent spot [1][2]. From July 2025 to the last trading day of February that gap averaged $4.29, on the desk's arithmetic from the same EIA series; it peaked at $37.62 on 2 October. This is geography arriving as a price. A barrel in Oklahoma is a long way from a minefield. A barrel priced off the North Sea competes for the same European and Asian buyers who lost Gulf supply. This week WTI futures rose 5.6% while Brent rose 0.4%, helped by a large U.S. crude stock draw after hurricane-related production shut-ins [3]. The American barrel has its own weather.

**Who pays the screen, and who pays the tank.** Funds and hedgers trade the screen. Refiners, airlines, hauliers and anyone heating a house with oil pay something closer to the tank, with a lag. That is why the morning headline can say "oil eases" while your diesel invoice does not. European diesel refining margins fell almost 2% on Friday, to $78.67 a barrel, after the International Energy Agency agreed to release more diesel. In September they were as high as $95 [3]. Lower is not low.

**The constructive read, and it is allowed.** The screen is not lying; it is forecasting. A much cheaper December says the market expects the corridor to widen, through escorts, deals or plain adaptation. Brent spot has already come down from its 2026 peak of $138.21 on 7 April [1]. Emergency stocks are being released, and the IEA says total releases will exceed the 400 million barrels pledged in March [6]. The honest position is to hold both prices in your head at once: the future is priced as calmer, the present is not, and you live in the present first.

What the desk will not do is pretend the screen is the barrel. When the two prices converge it will be because tankers are moving, or because demand has been destroyed. Those are very different Tuesdays. The spot assessment, not the headline verb, will tell you which one arrived.
