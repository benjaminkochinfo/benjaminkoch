On Thursday the World Health Organization asked countries to speed up action on lead. Its estimate: exposure contributes to more than 3.5 million deaths a year worldwide and affects hundreds of millions of children [1]. There is no known safe level, and even relatively low exposure can cause irreversible harm [1].

This is the opposite of a breaking story. The sources are ordinary and known: paint, cookware, cosmetics, spices, informal recycling and industrial contamination [1]. WHO's Richard Brown said children absorb lead from their environment or food at "four or five times" the rate of adults [2]. Yet fewer than half of countries had confirmed legally binding controls on lead paint in WHO's latest published assessment [1].

So the gap is not knowledge. It is enforcement. WHO's member states have asked it for a Global Action Plan on Lead Mitigation, to be presented to the World Health Assembly in 2027, with a technical package for countries the same year [1]. "We know where many exposures come from and we know how to prevent them," said Bruce Gordon, WHO's acting director for environment and health [1].

The same day brought the fast kind of health news. Kenya confirmed a second case of Ebola, a relative of the first patient, who died on 5 October; in the eastern DR Congo the outbreak has reached 65 health zones across seven provinces since mid-May [2]. Fast emergencies get attention. Slow ones get budgets, if someone reads the annex. Checking old paint, imported cookware and spice sources is household information, not medical advice; a doctor and a blood test are the binding numbers.
