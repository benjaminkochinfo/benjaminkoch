**The podium, in its own words.** On Thursday the IEA's Governing Board said about 330 million barrels had been released under the March collective action, that roughly 100 million barrels remain, and that total releases will exceed the original 400 million because some members have given more than they pledged [1]. It promised over 20 million barrels of diesel "as soon as possible" [1]. Nothing in that statement is false.

**The print.** Kpler data cited by BBC Verify put Hormuz flows at about 12.6 million barrels a day on 13 October, against a pre-war baseline of roughly 17 million [2]. That is a hole of about 4.4 million barrels, every day.

[[chart:hormuz-gap]]

**Same units, please.** G7 leaders set a four-month deadline on 2 October for releasing the outstanding 100 million barrels, according to S&P Global's Platts [3]. Spread over four months, about 122 days, that is roughly 0.8 million barrels a day: under a fifth of today's gap. Released faster, it helps more for less long. Either way it is a bridge, not a pipeline.

**The cushion is real.** On 7 October the IEA said member governments still hold around 1.1 billion barrels of public emergency stocks, including over 200 million barrels of diesel [4]. Divide that by today's gap and you get about eight months, on the desk's arithmetic, before subtracting the barrels no government would ever leave itself without. Months, not weeks. That is the constructive half of the story, and it is true.

**Verdict.** The podium is accurate and insufficient, which is the normal condition of podiums. Releases buy time for the corridor to reopen; they do not reopen it. The market noticed, politely: European diesel margins slipped almost 2% on Friday, and one analyst quoted by Reuters said the release is unlikely to ease diesel tightness [5].
