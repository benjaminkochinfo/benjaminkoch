Health systems decide who "needs" care by scores and guidelines. People decide by something else. A mixed-methods study in BMC Health Services Research followed 456 people whose depression questionnaire (PHQ-9) score was at least 5, meaning at least mild symptoms, with telephone interviews at the start and 12 months later [1]. Those who did not perceive a need for help were highly unlikely to seek professional care [1].

The researchers then interviewed 23 participants chosen to explain the patterns, and found that "subjective need" is not a yes-or-no switch. They describe six types of it, shaped by barriers, earlier treatment experiences, social support and coping strategies [1]. The study does not test a new care pathway, and its findings are limited to the people studied [1].

The authors suggest looking at subjective and guideline-defined need side by side, rather than treating the guideline as the only measure [1]. The lesson is modest and useful. An unused appointment is not always a failure of the system, and a filled one is not always a success. If you want people to use care, start where they are: what they feel they need, what got in the way last time, who stands beside them. A guideline is a map. The person decides whether to walk.
