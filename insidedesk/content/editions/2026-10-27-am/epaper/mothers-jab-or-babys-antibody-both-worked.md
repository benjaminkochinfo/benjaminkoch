RSV season runs from autumn to spring, and an infant is more likely to be hospitalised for RSV in the first year of life than for any other cause [1]. Parents now have two ways to guard against it: a vaccine (RSVpreF) for the mother late in pregnancy, which passes antibodies to the baby, or nirsevimab, a monoclonal antibody given to the newborn [1].

Which is better? A Kaiser Permanente Northern California study of 17,533 infants born over the 2023–2025 seasons, presented at IDWeek and published in JAMA Network Open, found neither significantly more effective [1]. Among 11,294 infants whose mothers were vaccinated, 106 (0.94%) were infected and 7 (0.06%) hospitalised in their first six months; among 3,831 infants given nirsevimab, 40 (1.04%) were infected and 5 (0.13%) hospitalised [1]. The researchers matched infants born in the same week and with similar mothers to make the comparison fair [1]. The study was funded by the US National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases and the research division itself [1].

The practical reading: if you are expecting a baby this winter, the right option is the one you actually get. A vaccine given too close to birth may not have time to protect the baby, and then the antibody shot covers the gap [1]. Two good tools and a sensible rule for when to use which: that is what quiet progress looks like.
