Psychiatry still lacks its X-ray. Diagnosis rests largely on what patients report, which is why a candidate biomarker is news, and why it needs reading slowly. A team from the Max Planck Institute of Psychiatry in Munich and the University of Melbourne analysed functional MRI and symptom data from 434 participants in the North American Prodrome Longitudinal Study: 356 people at high clinical risk of psychosis and 78 healthy controls, measured at five points over eight months [1]. The results are in Nature Mental Health [1].

Declining connectivity within the hippocampus went with worsening negative symptoms, such as lack of motivation, as well as worsening depressive symptoms and psychosocial functioning [1]. A fall in connectivity during the first two months predicted an increase in negative symptoms in the months that followed, in the high-risk group but not in the controls [1]. There was no measurable link with hallucinations or delusions, or with cognitive functioning [1].

The lead author, Lukas Röll, supplies his own brake: "Our results were statistically robust, but the effects are small," he says, adding that predicting a person's course from this measure alone "may not be the way forward" [1]. The more interesting use is therapeutic. Negative symptoms are much harder to treat than hallucinations, and the team suggests future trials could test whether non-invasive brain stimulation or exercise slows the decline [1]. A small, honest effect that points to a testable trial is worth more than a big claim that points nowhere.
