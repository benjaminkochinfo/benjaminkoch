Storms in late October and early November sent torrents through normally dry creeks across the US Southwest, flooding communities and forcing hundreds of evacuations [1]. They also did what last winter's thin snowpack did not. Lake Powell, the second-largest reservoir in the United States, has risen more than 20 feet (6.1 metres) from its mid-September low, dwarfing its gains from this year's snowmelt, according to hydrologist Martin Hoerling of the University of Colorado [1].

Keep both halves of the sentence. The lake is still just over a quarter full, and 15 of its 17 boat launches were unusable on Friday [1]. Hoerling estimates inflows could total over 1.2 million acre-feet [1]. Locally the relief is plainer: Pagosa Springs in Colorado lifted water restrictions on more than 10,000 people after about 7 inches of October rain, and Flagstaff's Upper Lake Mary took in more than 3 billion gallons since early October [1].

The institutions are not moving. The Bureau of Reclamation is unlikely to change releases from Lake Powell until it knows when snowpack has peaked, typically around April, and a spokesperson said "considerable uncertainty remains" [1]. Under the federal plan, California, Nevada and Arizona will cut use by 1.25 million acre-feet a year for the next two years; Nevada and Arizona are challenging it in court [1].

That patience is the right instinct. One wet autumn in a megadrought is a deposit, not a new salary. As Hoerling put it: "We need to figure out how to be resilient in a drier future" [1].
