Japan's current-account surplus, the broadest measure of what the country earns from the rest of the world, fell to ¥2,372.3 billion in September from ¥4,375.8 billion a year earlier, NDTV Profit reports; markets had expected ¥3,354.4 billion [1].

Desk arithmetic: the September surplus is about 54% of last year's, a fall of roughly 46%, and about ¥982 billion short of the forecast.

The yen hardly flinched. The dollar rose to 158.06 yen early on Tuesday from 157.90 [2]. Currencies respond to many things at once, and this morning the louder signal came from the Bank of Japan's summary of its October meeting, in which one board member said the next rate rise should come "when it is possible without becoming overly cautious"; Bloomberg reads that as a good chance of a December move [3].

Why it matters to you even if you never hold yen: a country that earns less abroad and whose households are spending less at home is one where the central bank's next step is genuinely hard. That is a reason to watch the institution's own records, published on schedule, rather than any confident prediction about what it will do. Nothing here is investment advice.
