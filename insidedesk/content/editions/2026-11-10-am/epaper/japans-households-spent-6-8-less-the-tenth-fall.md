While Tokyo's stock market rose almost 3% on Tuesday morning, Japan's kitchens told a different story. Household spending fell 6.8% on the year in September, against a 3.1% decline expected by economists, NDTV Profit reports [1]. It was the tenth monthly fall in a row and the steepest since September 2020, as inflation and higher borrowing costs weighed on household budgets [1].

The same morning, the Bank of Japan published the summary of its late-October meeting. One of the nine board members said the bank "should decide on the next policy interest rate hike when it is possible without becoming overly cautious", and Bloomberg reads the record as a good chance of a December move [2]. Put the two releases side by side and you have the central bank's dilemma in one morning: prices still rising, and the households who pay them already cutting back.

There is nothing contradictory in an index at 60,000 and a family buying less. Stock markets price company profits, many of them earned abroad; household surveys count rice, rent and electricity at home. Both are true at once.

For readers elsewhere the lesson travels well. When you see a record on the stock screen, look for the household spending figure from the same country. It is duller, slower and more honest about how most people are actually doing.
