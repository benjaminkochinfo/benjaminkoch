Doctors at the Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center treated 18 patients whose melanoma could be removed surgically with something every pharmacy stocks: a standard flu vaccine [1]. Patients first had the jab in the arm; a couple of days later, and again 12 days after that, the same vaccine was injected directly into the tumour, for most patients at about three times the usual dose [1]. No serious side effects emerged [1].

Two weeks later surgeons removed the tumours. Most samples were packed with immune cells, and blood tests suggested the immune system had switched on [1]. By surgery, seven tumours showed signs of shrinking and three patients had no cancer cells left [1]. "A pretty striking pattern emerged," said Dr Ian Garbarine, who presented the findings on Friday at a cancer immunotherapy meeting in Phoenix [1].

The idea is not that the flu vaccine teaches the body to recognise cancer. It disturbs the tumour's quiet, drawing in immune cells that then pick up pieces of the dying tissue [1]. The team is now testing it alongside checkpoint inhibitors in patients whose melanoma has spread [1].

Now the brakes. The trial was small and had no comparison group, so it cannot show the approach works [1]. Marcin Kortylewski of City of Hope, not involved, liked the idea but noted that similar treatments have looked promising early and then failed, and that it only works on tumours a needle can reach: "This is early-stage" [1]. Conference results are preliminary until peer-reviewed [1]. A cheap, off-the-shelf idea deserves a proper randomised trial. That is the good news here, and also the whole of it.
