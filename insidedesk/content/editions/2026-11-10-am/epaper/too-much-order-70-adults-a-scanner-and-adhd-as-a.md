The idea is called brain criticality: the brain may work best near a tipping point between activity that is too disorganised and activity that is too synchronised [1]. Hamed Haque of the University of Helsinki and colleagues tested whether ADHD symptoms reflect a shift towards the synchronised side, in a study published in Translational Psychiatry [1].

They recorded 70 adults with magnetoencephalography, 36 neurotypical and 34 with an ADHD diagnosis, at rest and during two attention tasks; those with ADHD paused medication for 24 hours [1]. The ADHD group responded more often when they should have held back, but performed similarly in most other respects [1]. In the recordings, the ADHD group showed stronger long-range temporal correlations, a marker of closeness to the tipping point, in alpha rhythms during one task and gamma rhythms during the other, with moderate effect sizes [1]. Across everyone, stronger gamma correlations went with higher symptom scores; in the beta range the relationship was curved, peaking at moderate symptoms [1].

The caveats come from the paper and PsyPost: the study is correlational, the sample modest, people with other conditions were excluded, the criticality reading is indirect, and an earlier resting-state study found no difference [1]. There was no single brain pattern that separated everyone with ADHD from everyone without [1].

For you, that last line is the useful one. It fits the picture of ADHD as a continuum across the population rather than a switch, which the authors argue in their title [1]. A continuum calls for support measured by need, not by label.
