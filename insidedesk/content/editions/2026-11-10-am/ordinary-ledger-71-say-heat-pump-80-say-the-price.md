If you are wondering what to put in the cellar when the old boiler gives up, the people who advise on it have a clear favourite. In a nationwide survey of 2,551 energy experts in 2026, the ZEW in Mannheim found they had recommended a heat pump in an average of 71% of cases in 2025 [1]. Among heating installers, heat pumps made up 57% of the systems they actually installed [1]. In a separate experiment with realistic advice scenarios, the heat pump was recommended in about two-thirds of cases [1].

The interesting part is what the experts say stops people. Not the machine: only 4% name poor product quality as an obstacle. Instead, 80% cite high investment costs, 74% frequent changes to the legal framework and 72% customers' lack of money [1]. Co-author Kathrine von Graevenitz says customers care above all about investment and long-run total costs and enough heating output (desk translation) [1].

Your house decides more than your intentions. A lower energy need of 70 rather than 120 kilowatt-hours per square metre raised the probability of a heat-pump recommendation by 24.5 percentage points; a high need of 200 kilowatt-hours lowered it by 32.7 points; underfloor heating instead of radiators raised it by 19.8 points [1]. The age and income of the household and the year the building went up also mattered [1]. And advisers did not always agree: in comparable situations, they sometimes reached different recommendations, co-author Bettina Chlond notes [1].

On prices, more than 90% of the experts expect natural gas to be dearer over the next five years, while more than half expect heat-pump electricity prices to stay the same or fall [1]. That is their expectation, not a guarantee, and it is worth hearing precisely because these are the people who see the invoices.

The ordinary lesson: before you ask "heat pump or not", ask what your house uses per square metre, and get a second opinion if the first one arrives too quickly. Stable rules would help everyone; a second quote helps you today.
