The World Meteorological Organization published its State of the Climate Update 2026 on Monday, timed for the COP31 talks in Türkiye [1]. Strip away the adjectives and it is a ledger. The global average surface temperature from January to September was about 1.47°C above the pre-industrial average [1]. The past 12 years have each been among the warmest on record, and the past four top the list; 2026 is on track to be one of the four warmest [1].

The driver for the coming months is El Niño, building since August and expected to be the strongest on record by December [1]. WMO Secretary-General Celeste Saulo put the physics plainly: "El Niño doesn't add new heat to the planet. It releases heat from the ocean, which is the warmest ever measured" [1]. 2026 is on track to be the tenth year in a row of record ocean warmth, and sea level is the highest on record and accelerating [1].

The update also counts what this year cost. Roughly 146.5 million people in India were exposed to an extreme heatwave in April, with temperatures of 47°C [1]. Provisional estimates put excess mortality in Europe this summer above 30,000, about 16,000 of them in the 22–28 June heatwave alone [1]. A glacier collapse in the Himalaya in late August caused a flash flood that killed more than 1,300 people in Nepal [1].

Three lines in the report's highlights deserve more attention than they will get. For the first time, the World Health Organization has classified El Niño's expected health impacts as a health emergency [1]. Water stored on land has declined globally since 2014; in 2025, 42% of the assessed area was below normal [1]. And El Niño is already raising risks for agriculture where farming depends on rain and food insecurity is high [1].

UN Secretary-General António Guterres called it "a new age of climate extremes" [1]. You may prefer the WMO's drier phrase: El Niño as a "risk multiplier" [1]. Multipliers are for planners. The useful response to a report like this is not alarm but preparation: heat plans in hospitals, water budgets, crop insurance and early warnings, the dull machinery that turns a forecast into fewer funerals.
