Most people discharged from an emergency department after a traumatic event never make it to a mental health appointment; earlier studies put the share who attend an outpatient visit at roughly half [1]. Henry Ford Health in Detroit is testing a different door: sleep.

Its RESTED trial enrols patients within 72 hours of a traumatic event, after emergency treatment and discharge [1]. Depending on symptoms, they get one of four telehealth video interventions: cognitive behavioural therapy for nightmares, CBT for insomnia, or sleep education with or without nightmare education [1]. Initial findings, described in a commentary in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, show more than 76% of those enrolled went on to begin treatment [1]. All patients surveyed said video made sessions "very easy" to attend; more than 71% said in-person sessions would have been difficult; nearly 86% would prefer video for future care [1].

The point is not the screen; it is the entry point. Nightmares after trauma are linked to a higher risk of post-traumatic stress disorder and suicidal thoughts, says Dr David Moore, an investigator on the trial [1]. "Patients are willing to engage in care, especially when we remove some of the traditional barriers," he says [1].

The caveat: these are preliminary figures from an ongoing trial, and the comparison with "roughly half" comes from earlier studies, not a control group [1]. Whether early sleep treatment lowers PTSD risk is the team's next question, not yet its answer [1]. If you or someone close to you is sleeping badly after an accident or assault, the bad nights are worth mentioning to a GP; they are a symptom, not a weakness.
