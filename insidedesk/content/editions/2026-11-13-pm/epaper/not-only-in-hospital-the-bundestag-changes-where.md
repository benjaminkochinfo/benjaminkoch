The Bundestag on Friday widened the rules on compulsory medical treatment, a measure allowed only in tightly limited exceptional cases [1]. Until now, a court-appointed guardian could consent to it only if it took place in a hospital. Under the amended law it may also happen elsewhere, if transport to a clinic or a stay there would threaten the person with a significant health impairment [1].

The change concerns people who have a legal guardian because a disability or mental illness means they cannot manage their own affairs [1]. It implements a decision of the Federal Constitutional Court, which asked for new rules two years ago [1].

The case behind it is specific. According to the Federal Court of Justice, a woman with paranoid schizophrenia living in a supported-housing group was regularly treated against her will in a nearby hospital [1]. Her guardian asked for the medication to be given on the ward of her residence instead, arguing that getting her to the clinic sometimes required restraining her, which regularly re-traumatised her [1].

This is what good law-making often looks like: not a grand reform but a narrow fix, ordered by a court, written around one person's hardship and meant to reduce it. The hard part now moves to the places that will apply it, the guardianship courts, doctors and care homes, and to how carefully they document each exception.
