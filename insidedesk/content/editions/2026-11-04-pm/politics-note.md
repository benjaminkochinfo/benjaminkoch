**The podium.** The morning after his party lost both chambers, Donald Trump told CBS News that Democrats are "going to want to work with me. Otherwise I'd just veto everything and they're not going to want that" [1]. Separately he told Fox News that "a part of it was Iran", meaning the war and the petrol prices that followed it [4]. The veto line is the one to check, because it rests on a number.

**The print.** The Constitution prints that number. A bill the president returns unsigned becomes law only if, after reconsideration, it is "approved by two thirds" of each House [2]. CBS News projects at least 54 Democratic senators in January, with Alaska and Mississippi still uncalled [1]. In the House, Democrats had a 228–139 majority on Wednesday morning, with 68 races still uncalled [3]. Desk arithmetic: with all 100 senators voting, an override needs 67, so Democrats would need 13 Republican votes; with all 435 House seats filled and voting, it needs 290, which is 62 more than the 228 Democratic seats called so far.

**The gap.** On the arithmetic, the podium is right: a 54-seat Senate cannot override a veto alone. But the same article of the Constitution also prints the other half of the bargain. "No Money shall be drawn from the Treasury, but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law," it says in Section 9 [2]. Spending laws start in Congress, and from January Congress means Democrats. A president who vetoes everything also vetoes the budgets his own departments run on. The veto stops things; it does not pay anybody.

**What it means for you.** Both sides now hold a brake, and neither holds the whole steering wheel. That is not gridlock by design so much as the machinery asking for signatures from two places at once. The useful thing to watch is not the next speech but the first appropriations bill: who drafts it, how many Republicans vote for it, and whether it comes back signed. Speeches say "everything". Bills come one at a time, with page numbers.
