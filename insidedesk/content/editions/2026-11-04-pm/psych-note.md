**The document.** "Measuring Partisanship and Representation in Online Congressional Communications", by Michael Kistner of the University of Houston and five co-authors, in the *American Political Science Review* [1]. It covers members of the US Congress from 2009 to 2022: about 4.7 million posts on Twitter, 2.4 million on Facebook and 184,000 official email newsletters [1]. Desk arithmetic: roughly 7.3 million messages in all. On the day after a midterm, it is worth reading what the campaign looked like when nobody was campaigning.

**The line.** On Twitter, negative partisan attacks received 60% more likes, 80% more retweets and 79% more replies than an average post by the same politician [1]. On Facebook, they drew 86% more shares and 93% more comments [1]. Messages about bipartisan work or services for the local district did worse than the typical post, and so did claiming credit for something government had delivered [1].

**The lines around it.** Attacks made up about five to ten per cent of messages in the early years and nearly twenty per cent by the end of 2022 [1]. Language-based partisanship rose across all three channels from 2011 [1]. Measured this way, Democratic rhetoric showed no statistically significant trend over the decade, while Republican rhetoric moved strongly towards the extremes [1]. The sharpest rise in Democratic attack messages came after the 2016 election; the Republican spike followed 2020 [1]. Messages about funding for the district or helping constituents did not keep pace [1].

**The caveats.** The data stop in 2022, before the rise of newer, more partisan platforms [1]. Messages were classified by language models trained on a hand-coded subset; the authors checked the scores against roll-call votes, fundraising and bill sponsorship, and they lined up well [1]. Engagement measures what a polarised online audience rewards, not what the electorate wants.

**The constructive reading.** The researchers point to separate surveys in which most voters say they prefer representatives who focus on legislative work over partisan bickering [1]. They warn of a "social media feedback mirage", in which politicians mistake likes and shares for support [1]. That is a fixable error. The dull jobs that score badly online, such as answering a constituent's letter, securing a grant, or co-signing a bill with the other side, are still what the voters who were counted this week say they want. A politician who checks the casework queue before the retweet count is reading the better metric.
