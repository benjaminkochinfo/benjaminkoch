Election nights are narrated in blocs: how men voted, how women voted. A paper by Claire Gothreau of Lebanon Valley College and Amanda Friesen of the University of Western Ontario, in *Politics and the Life Sciences*, suggests that the bloc hides most of the story [1]. They asked people to rate, on separate continuous scales, how masculine and how feminine they feel [1].

In a 2022 survey of 1,017 US adults, binary gender was no longer a significant predictor of political participation once those self-ratings were included; people who rated themselves higher on either scale were more likely to sign petitions, protest or donate [1]. A 2020 survey of 1,043 adults replicated that [1]. On policy, the meaning of "masculine" depended on who said it: among men, higher masculinity went with stronger support for gun rights; among women, higher masculinity went with lower support for the death penalty [1]. A third survey of 1,200 people found the same split for authoritarian attitudes, which rose with masculinity among men and fell with it among women [1].

The caveats are real. All of it is correlational and self-reported, and liberal respondents may simply feel freer to describe themselves outside the usual categories [1]. The authors call for studies that follow people over time [1].

The useful point for anyone reading exit polls this week: the line between two groups is often less informative than the variation inside each. "Men moved right" is a headline. The men who did and did not are the story.
