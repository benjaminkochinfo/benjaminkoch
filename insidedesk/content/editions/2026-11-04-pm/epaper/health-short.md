The drugs that kill cancer cells do not ask whose cells they touch. A nationwide survey of more than 1,000 nurses in German oncology wards and specialist practices, published in the journal *Pflegewissenschaft*, finds that the people handing those drugs out are often less protected than the rules require [1].

About a quarter had not done any of the mandatory yearly refresher courses on handling cytostatics, which can cause cancer, mutations and harm to fertility [1]. More than 40% said they did not know their own workplace's safety guidelines, and only about 60% used the special gloves meant for these drugs [1]. In none of the clinics where respondents worked were all recommended measures fully in place [1].

Many reported skin contact during infusions, because closed transfer systems were not used enough or infusion sets were faulty; wipe tests to detect contamination were often missing [1]. Several nurses described rashes, headaches, dizziness, hair loss and changes in taste after shifts, symptoms that can point to exposure [1].

The researchers are not asking for new science. They want stricter legal duties for clinics and practices, proper equipment, regular training and consistent use of TRGS 525, Germany's existing technical rule for hazardous substances in healthcare [1]. That is the dull part, and it works: a rule only protects the hands that actually wear the glove.
