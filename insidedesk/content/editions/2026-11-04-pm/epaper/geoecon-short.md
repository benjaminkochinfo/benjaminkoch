On Monday Iran's Persian Gulf Strait Authority added 114 vessels to its "non-compliant" list, bringing it to 240, The Maritime Executive reported, as summarised by Forwarder News [1]. The list started at 45 ships in late August and stood at 77 by mid-September [1]. Iran says listed ships face "fines, detention, or confiscation" unless they clear passage with it and use the northern channel near its coast [1].

The risk firm Orbital counts 118 crude tankers among the 240, plus LPG and LNG carriers, a few bulk carriers and now four containerships [1]. Seventy-two entries are linked to Gulf state companies such as ADNOC, Nakilat, QatarEnergy and Bahri [1]. The new clause that matters most: any vessel that cooperates with a listed ship, "via STS, transshipment, etc.", will itself be added [1].

That reaches the dull workarounds that keep goods moving. Container lines already land much Upper Gulf cargo at Fujairah or Khor Fakkan and send it on by bonded land bridge and feeders, which Hapag-Lloyd says take about five days and "do not operate on a fixed weekly schedule" [1]. A feeder that touched a listed ship can now be a listed ship.

Does the list stop ships? The evidence is mixed: in September, Windward found listed ships kept trading, mostly with their transponders switched off [1]. Ships are still being struck, though, and Iran's parliament speaker warned on Wednesday that "our NO comes with a Nightmare" [2]. Forwarder News's advice to shippers is plain and unglamorous: check every vessel and feeder on a Gulf booking against the list, and write detention into the contract. Nothing here is investment advice.
