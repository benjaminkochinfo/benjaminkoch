Researchers at UCL and Imperial College London measured 233 blood metabolites in more than 6,000 British civil servants from the Whitehall II study, then followed their health for up to 20 years [1]. An ultra-processed diet left a recognisable pattern: less omega-3 and "good" HDL cholesterol, more of the triglycerides that clog arteries [1].

That pattern accounted for 51% of the diet's link with later cardiovascular disease and 43% of its link with type 2 diabetes, even after allowing for sugar, fat and salt [1]. The highest third of ultra-processed eaters had a 17% higher heart-disease risk than the lowest third [1].

It is observational, so it cannot prove cause, the authors say [1]. But it arrives while the UK's nutrition advisers are weighing whether processing itself belongs in the guidance [1]. Evidence before rules: the right order.
