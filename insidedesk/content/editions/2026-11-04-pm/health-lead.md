On Wednesday the British Heart Foundation opened a new data platform, its CVD Observatory, and the first figure it put on the table was a record [1]. In 2014/15, UK hospitals recorded 173,744 visits for heart failure. In 2024/25 they recorded 264,417, a rise of 52% [1]. Over the same decade, hospital visits of every kind rose by 20% [1]. England's rise was the steepest of the four nations, at 58% [1].

[[chart:uk-heart-failure-visits-04pm]]

Desk arithmetic: that is 90,673 more visits a year than a decade ago, or about 724 a day in 2024/25. The BHF says heart failure patients stay in hospital for around ten days, about twice the average stay [1]. Desk arithmetic again, and only roughly, since "around ten days" is an average: 264,417 visits at ten days each comes to about 2.6 million bed days a year, and the decade's growth alone adds roughly 900,000.

It would be easy to read this as a story about hearts getting worse. Read the charity's own explanation first. The rise, it says, reflects an ageing and growing population, "more people surviving heart attacks", and stark health inequalities [1]. The middle item matters. A country that saves more people from heart attacks will have more people living with damaged hearts afterwards. Some of this line is the bill for a success, and you should not want it gone at the price of the success.

**The number that is not in the headline.** Further down the release is the figure that changes the argument. Research suggests that up to 80% of heart failure diagnoses in England are made in hospital, even though 40% of those patients had symptoms that should have triggered an earlier diagnosis [1]. That is the real story. The condition affects more than a million people in the UK [1]. Most of them are not found at a desk with a stethoscope and a referral form. They are found on a ward, already breathless and swollen.

The BHF's chief scientific and medical officer, Professor Bryan Williams, put it plainly: "Most hospital visits for heart failure could be prevented" [1]. Too often, he told the Mirror, someone is only diagnosed "when they are already seriously unwell" [2].

The patient story the charity published is a case study in how that happens, not in negligence. Szandra Tar, 35, from Grangemouth, was fit and played volleyball [1]. Her symptoms began with shoulder aches at the end of 2024. By early 2025 she was out of breath after a short walk. A GP call in June 2025 ended with over-exercising or anxiety as the explanation [1]. By July she could not climb a flight of stairs, her face had swollen, and the swelling was put down to the heat or antibiotics [1]. It was only when a doctor listened to her chest and heard a fast resting heart rate that she was sent to hospital, where she spent 11 days on a cardiology unit [1]. She now has an implanted defibrillator, lifelong medication and a genetic diagnosis that her children are being tested for [1].

Nobody in that story was a villain. Each explanation was plausible on its own. What was missing was a routine that makes the less likely explanation get checked: breathless plus swollen plus worse, so listen to the chest and check the heart. The BHF's own warning signs are simple enough to print on a fridge magnet: breathlessness, chest pain and swelling from fluid retention, and urgent advice if symptoms are "new, persistent or getting worse" [2].

**What the response looks like.** Notice what is being proposed, because it is pleasingly dull. The charity wants earlier diagnosis and better management "in the community to keep people out of hospital" [1]. The Department of Health and Social Care points to its Cardiovascular Disease Modern Service Framework, meant to "identify and treat people earlier" [2]. The government's ten-year plan aims to cut early deaths from heart disease and stroke by a quarter, and the Mirror reports its first three years focus on managing blood pressure, cholesterol and diabetes outside hospital [2]. None of that will make a speech go viral. All of it is the kind of work that shows up, years later, as a line bending.

And the observatory itself is part of the answer. "When data is fragmented, it is harder to see the full picture," the charity argues; trends get missed and inequalities stay hidden [1]. A register that counts the same thing the same way every year is not glamorous. It is how you find out whether the framework works, or whether it is a nice document.

The BHF says heart failure diagnoses could double by 2040 [1]. That is its projection, not ours, and it is the reason the 80% matters more than the 52%. You cannot make the population younger, and you would not want fewer heart-attack survivors. You can move the moment of diagnosis from the ward to the surgery. If you, or someone you look after, has breathlessness that is new or getting worse, swollen ankles or a belly that will not fit last week's trousers, do not wait for it to explain itself. Ask for the chest to be listened to.
