German producer prices of industrial products were 8.1% higher in October 2026 than a year earlier, Destatis reported on Friday morning [1]. It is the steepest annual rise since February 2023, when the figure was 13.9%, and prices rose 1.4% from September alone [1].

[[chart:de-ppi-oct-20nov]]

**Energy writes most of it.** Energy was 18.7% dearer than a year ago and 3.8% dearer than in September [1]. Because of the war in Iran and the Middle East, mineral oil products cost 51.9% more than in October 2025; light heating oil cost 97.2% more, naphtha 76.7% and fuels 38.4% [1]. Natural gas in distribution was up 21.9% and electricity 12.6%, while district heating was unchanged [1].

**The rest is warmer than comfortable.** Without energy, producer prices rose 3.6% on the year and 0.3% on the month [1]. Intermediate goods, the parts and materials one firm sells another, rose 6.8%, driven by metals (+12.9%) and especially copper and copper semi-finished products (+29.4%) [1]. Basic chemicals were 12.5% dearer, fertiliser 11.3% and animal feed 12.4% [1]. Capital goods rose 2.4% and durable consumer goods 2.3%, while non-durable consumer goods were 1.3% cheaper than a year ago [1].

**Why this is the useful number today.** A producer-price index is the invoice before your invoice: what bakeries pay for flour, farmers for fertiliser, builders for reinforcing steel (+11.9%) [1]. It does not reach shop shelves at once, or in full, but it is what the next round of price lists is negotiated from. The committees know it. Reuters reported on Friday that markets now price a quarter-point rate rise from the Federal Reserve in December, and the euro was trading near its lowest since April 2025 at about $1.097 [2].

**For you.** The 1.3% fall in non-durable consumer goods is the reminder that the chain is long and uneven. If a supplier letter cites "higher costs" this winter, ask which ones: energy, materials or wages. The answer tells you whether it is likely to last.
