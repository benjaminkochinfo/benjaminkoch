Heavy smog pushed several cities in Pakistan's Punjab into hazardous air on Friday, Dawn reported [1]. Lahore recorded an hourly air quality index reading of 465 between 7am and 8am local time, while Gujranwala and Multan registered 661 and 641 [1]. A separate ranking of Pakistani cities put Lahore at 426, with Sargodha at 289 and Sialkot at 232 [1]. Residents were advised to minimise outdoor activity, keep windows closed, wear masks outside and use air purifiers indoors [1].

**The machinery is running.** The Punjab Environment Protection and Climate Change Department says its teams carried out 84,370 inspections of brick kilns between 1 January and 18 November, demolished 1,429, sealed 968 and registered 1,248 police cases, imposing fines of Rs178 million [1]. In the smog season from 1 September alone there were 29,999 visits and 519 kilns demolished [1].

**The scale is larger.** Pakistan's average fine-particle (PM2.5) concentration was 67.3 micrograms per cubic metre in 2025, nearly 14 times the World Health Organization's annual guideline of 5, according to the 2025 World Air Quality Report cited by Dawn [1].

**Why we carry it.** Smog is a climate-adjacent story about the same thing every climate story is about: what burns, where, and who counts it. Punjab's numbers are a reminder that institutions can be busy and the air can still be hazardous, and that both facts belong in the same sentence.
