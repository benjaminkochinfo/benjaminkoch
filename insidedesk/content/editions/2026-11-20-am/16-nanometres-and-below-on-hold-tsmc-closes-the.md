New rules from the US Commerce Department took effect on Thursday and removed a compliance route that had let some Chinese chip designers use advanced manufacturing without individual export licences [1]. Two people familiar with the matter told Reuters that TSMC has instructed that affected orders on processes of 16 nanometres and below be halted unless the customer obtains a licence [1].

Chinese designers will generally be limited to older 28 nm and 40 nm processes, and only if their chips use no high-bandwidth memory and need no advanced packaging, according to TSMC guidance seen by the two sources [1]. Most leading AI chips are made on 7 nm or smaller [1]. TSMC said it "is aware of and will comply with the new Bureau of Industry and Security regulations as applicable" and does not comment on market rumours [1].

The mechanics matter more than the headline. Until now TSMC could check a Chinese design's computing power and decide whether it fell outside the controls; under the new rules, affected customers' advanced chips are presumed to be covered [1]. The guidance also asks for closer scrutiny of customers' locations, parent companies, major shareholders, packaging providers and shipping routes, including orders placed through subsidiaries in Taiwan, the US, Europe, Japan and South-East Asia [1].

The market drew its own line on Friday: a semiconductor sub-index tracked by RTHK fell 3.9%, and RTHK cited TSMC's tighter limits as one reason sentiment towards chipmakers was hurt [2]. The US list of approved designers includes Nvidia, AMD, Apple, MediaTek and Realtek, and no mainland Chinese firm [1].

For you, this is a reminder of where technology policy actually lives: not in summit communiqués but in order forms, customer checks and the number in front of "nanometres".
