**The ledger, before breakfast.**
- Super E10, national average of open stations reporting to the federal price register at 05:11 this morning: €1.968 a litre [1].
- Diesel: €2.216 a litre. Super E5: €2.024 [1].
- Heating oil, national average per litre on Sunday: €1.69 at esyoil, €1.75 at Tecson [2].
- Brent crude in Asian trading: $98.06 a barrel, down 0.1% [3].
- Desk arithmetic: a 50-litre tank of E10 at this morning's average costs about €98.40; the same tank of diesel about €110.80.

Start with the pump, because it is where most households meet the oil market. The national average is one number; the spread around it is the more useful one. In Saarland and Thuringia the average E10 litre costs €1.920; in Schleswig-Holstein it costs €2.035 [1]. By brand, the averages run from €1.929 at HEM to €2.028 at Agip Eni [1]. Desk arithmetic: on a 50-litre tank, the gap between those two regional averages is about €5.75. That is not a market signal. It is a reason to look at a price app before you look at the fuel gauge.

Heating oil tells a slower story. The comparison portals have it almost flat across the past week, between €1.69 and €1.75 a litre, and Berliner Morgenpost notes that at the end of October last year the litre cost around 80 cents less [2]. The oil analyst Steffen Bukold, writing for esyoil, says traders consider last week's sell-off overdone and that the situation in the Strait of Hormuz has not changed [2]. You do not need a view on that. You need to know how full your tank is and how long a cold month takes to empty it.

Then the general price level. Germany's inflation rate fell to 2.8% in October on the statistics office's first estimate, from 3.3% in September, helped by the cut in fuel tax that took effect at the start of the month [4]. Energy was still 12.2% dearer than a year earlier; food rose only 0.4%; services 2.5% [4]. The fuel-tax cut is due to run until the end of the year, and the Bundesbank expects inflation to pick up noticeably when it expires [4]. That is not this desk's forecast; it is the central bank's, and it is the sort of thing worth writing in the margin of January.

**The week's dates.**

*Tuesday.* The US Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the consumer price index for October at 08:30 Eastern time, 14:30 in Berlin [6]. It is an American number, but it moves the dollar, and the dollar moves what you pay for oil. Bloomberg reports traders assigning an almost 80% probability to a US rate rise by year-end [3].

*Thursday.* The Bundestag's budget committee begins its final session on the 2027 budget [5]. This is where the household details sit. ZDF lists them: a proposed plastic tax worth about €670 million in 2027, which Union parliamentary leader Thorsten Frei says the coalition "will not do" (desk translation); a sugar tax worth €690 million, whose proceeds Frei says would go to the health fund to relieve statutory health insurance; and SPD wishes to stop planned savings on housing benefit and the abolition of the immediate children's supplement, together about €770 million [5]. A cut in electricity tax for everyone, which the Union has asked for at about €5 billion a year, is, according to coalition assessments quoted by ZDF, not expected to be added [5]. If you were waiting for a cheaper electricity bill from Berlin this winter, that is the sentence to read.

*From today to 20 November.* COP31 runs in Antalya, Türkiye [7]. Climate conferences do not change your tariff. Over years, the rules they agree change what power stations, insulation and boilers cost. Watch the texts, not the speeches.

**What to do with all this.** Very little, and that is the good news. Heating oil is a couple of cents a litre below where it started last week [2]. The big decisions of the week happen in committee rooms, on statistical release schedules and in negotiating halls, which is exactly where they should happen: in public, on paper, with dates attached. Nothing here is investment advice. It is a calendar, and a calendar is the most underrated financial tool in the house.
