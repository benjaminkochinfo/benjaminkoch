After a quarrel, the reply can matter as much as the wrong. That is the finding of three preregistered studies by Brianna Davison, now at the University of Calgary, and colleagues, published in The Journal of Social Psychology [1].

The team surveyed 242 adults in 121 couples, then 538 adults in relationships of at least six months, asking each to recall an unresolved hurt and how their partner had responded [1]. A "nonapology" here is not mere silence but an active defence: excuses, denial, blame, lashing out [1]. In both surveys, attachment anxiety, a strong wish for closeness paired with fear of rejection, was linked to more grudges, withdrawal and withheld forgiveness only when the partner was seen as highly nonapologetic [1]. In the second study that link was more than twice as strong among high-anxiety participants [1].

A third study with 334 students tested imagined replies [1]. A hostile text raised destructive reactions in everyone [1]. But when the partner did not respond at all, more anxious participants reacted more destructively than less anxious ones; the authors suggest silence leaves room for the worst reading [1].

The caveats are honest: scores were low on average, 1.79 and 2.05 out of 7, effects were modest, and conflicts were recalled by one partner only [1]. The practical point is small and sturdy. If you cannot yet say sorry, say something.
