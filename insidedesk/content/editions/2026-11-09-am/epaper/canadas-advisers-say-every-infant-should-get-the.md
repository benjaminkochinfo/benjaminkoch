Canada's National Advisory Committee on Immunization (NACI) now recommends that all infants be vaccinated against hepatitis B [1]. The dose can be given within 24 hours of birth or at the first routine childhood appointment at two months, when babies already get their tetanus, diphtheria, whooping cough and polio shots [1].

Until now, the country has had a patchwork. British Columbia, Alberta, Quebec, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island and all three territories already vaccinate in infancy. Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Ontario, Nova Scotia and Newfoundland and Labrador wait until grade 6 or 7 [1]. That matters because hepatitis B is far more likely to become chronic when caught young, especially before the first birthday, and untreated chronic infection can lead to cirrhosis or liver cancer [1].

Prevalence in Canada is low, estimated at below 2% overall, and higher in some groups, including people born where the virus is endemic [1]. Pregnant women are screened, but providers point to gaps in care [1]. The committee says protection early in life "is expected to promote health equity", and admits that "it will take time" for provinces to switch, so older children will keep being offered the jab during the transition [1].

The contrast is noted in the same report: a US federal advisory panel has voted to drop its long-standing recommendation of universal vaccination at birth [1]. Two neighbours, one virus, two answers. The Canadian one comes with a consultation of the provinces and a transition plan, which is how a recommendation becomes a programme.
