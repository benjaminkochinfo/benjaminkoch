World Diabetes Day fell on Saturday, and the most useful figure published around it was not about a new drug. The International Diabetes Federation's Atlas estimates that 589 million adults aged 20 to 79 were living with diabetes in 2024, and that about 43 per cent of them, or 252 million, did not know it [1]. Nearly nine in ten of those undiagnosed live in low- and middle-income countries [1].

The target is clear and dull, which is a compliment. WHO member states have agreed that at least 80 per cent of people with diabetes should be diagnosed by 2030 [2]. The IDF puts today's share at 57 per cent [1]. Its preliminary analysis says about 134 million additional diagnoses would be needed, roughly 89,000 every day from this World Diabetes Day to the end of 2030 [1]. That is not a research problem. It is an appointments problem.

The IDF's new survey shows where the queue forms. It asked 2,411 adults aged 25 to 64 in Brazil, India, Kenya, Saudi Arabia, Spain and the United States, all with known risk factors and no diagnosis [1]. 84 per cent agreed a risk check was worthwhile; only 15 per cent thought they were likely to develop type 2 diabetes [1]. 67 per cent wrongly believed pre-diabetes usually comes with noticeable symptoms [1]. Fear of the result was the most cited barrier at 28 per cent, cost at 26 per cent, getting an appointment at 20 per cent [1]. The IDF itself warns these samples are not nationally representative [1].

Across the Americas, PAHO counts 112 million adults with diabetes, about 13 per cent of the adult population, and 43 million people aged 30 and older with diabetes who receive no treatment [2]. "We need to strengthen primary health care to detect the disease as early as possible," said PAHO director Jarbas Barbosa [2]. Note the order in his sentence: detection, then care. A diagnosis without follow-up is a letter nobody answers.

The bill is already being paid. The IDF estimates diabetes-related health spending exceeded US$1 trillion worldwide in 2024 [1]. Complications such as kidney failure, heart disease and vision loss are, in the IDF's framing, what late diagnosis buys [1].

What would help is mostly unglamorous. In the survey, 46 per cent said they would welcome walk-in checks at pharmacies or supermarkets, 38 per cent evening or weekend appointments, and about one in four a personal invitation explaining how to get checked [1]. If you have a risk factor, you need not wait for an invitation: a check is the cheapest piece of medicine on offer. "We cannot close the diagnosis gap if the people most in need of testing don't know their own risk factors," said IDF president Peter Schwarz [1].
