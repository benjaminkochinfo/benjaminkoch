Researchers at the University of Limerick randomised 55 young women, average age about 22, who screened for analogue generalised anxiety disorder, a level of worry that meets questionnaire thresholds rather than a clinical diagnosis [1]. For eight weeks, after two weeks of familiarisation, one group lifted at 70 to 80 per cent of their one-repetition maximum, two sets of 8 to 12 repetitions of eight exercises, following WHO and American College of Sports Medicine guidelines [1]. The other did a "sham" version at 20 per cent [1]. The study appears in the Journal of Affective Disorders, publication dated 15 November [1].

The headline finding is a non-finding. There was no significant difference between the groups over time (p = 0.806) [1]. Both, however, saw large reductions in depressive symptoms, still present at one-month follow-up [1]. The within-group effect was larger in the heavier-lifting arm (standardised mean difference 1.53) than in the light arm (1.04), which the authors read as a hint of greater benefit, while saying the difference was "unconfirmed" [1].

Read it for what it is: a small trial, a sham that was itself exercise, and no group that did nothing. That design cannot tell you how much of the improvement came from lifting, from attention, or from time passing. It can tell you that a structured, supervised routine, even a light one, coincided with lower symptoms in young women who worry a great deal [1].

If you have been put off strength training because you think it has to be heavy to count, this paper is mild encouragement to start light.
