Most of Friday's attention went to what Joachim Nagel said about interest rates. The longer half of his Munich speech was about something duller and, for your income, possibly bigger: the cost of selling across Europe's own borders [1].

The outside pressure first. By the Bundesbank's estimates, the average US tariff rate has risen from 2.3% in 2024 to almost 12%, a level last seen in the early 1940s [1]. The United States and China together take roughly one in every seven euros of Germany's goods exports [1].

Then the inside. Nagel cited an IMF study estimating the cost of trading across EU borders as equivalent to tariffs of around 44% on goods and 110% on services [1]. "Those are barriers we impose on ourselves," he said. "And they are ours to remove" [1]. He listed the plumbing: nine member states still miss the 10% electricity-interconnection target set for 2020; three non-European providers hold more than 70% of Europe's cloud market; Europe hosts an estimated 5% of the world's AI computing capacity against almost 80% in the US [1].

There is some progress. The securitisation reform reached political agreement last month, the first under the Savings and Investments Union, and once all trade deals are in force preferential agreements could cover more than half of the EU's external trade, up from around 46% at the end of 2025 [1].

None of this needs a summit. It needs regulators, grid operators and lawyers to finish paperwork. That is unglamorous work, and it is the cheapest growth on offer. Nothing here is investment advice.
