The Annex reads the paper behind the headline. This week's paper is a randomised trial from the Norwegian Institute of Public Health, published in Internet Interventions and led by Marit Knapstad and Otto Robert Smith [1]. The headline says online therapy cuts therapist time. It does. The paper says something more careful as well.

**Clause one: the setting.** 390 adults seeking help for anxiety or mild to moderate depression at six primary care services were randomised to guided internet-based cognitive behavioural therapy or to usual treatment at Prompt Mental Health Care, Norway's service modelled on England's NHS Talking Therapies [1]. In the usual-care group, 78 per cent received individual face-to-face therapy [1]. The trial ran from October 2021 to December 2023 [1]. This is routine care, not a university clinic with hand-picked volunteers, which makes it more useful and messier.

**Clause two: the result.** Both groups improved markedly over six months [1]. Recovery was 56.1 per cent online and 58.9 per cent in usual care [1]. That looks close. But the researchers had set a strict threshold for showing online therapy was not meaningfully worse, and it did not meet that standard for depression or anxiety scores [1]. Only about half of participants completed the six-month questionnaire, which cut the study's statistical power [1]. "Not shown to be non-inferior" is not the same as "shown to be worse". It means the trial could not rule it out.

**Clause three: the clock.** Online therapy used about 46 per cent less therapist time per client, roughly 144 minutes against 266 [1]. Desk arithmetic: 122 minutes saved per client, so the therapist hours that serve one usual-care client would serve about 1.8 online clients. For a waiting list, that ratio is the story.

**Clause four: the small print.** Early dropout was higher online, 10.4 per cent against 6.1 per cent [1]. Therapists reported using fewer techniques such as exposure exercises online, which the authors think may have limited benefits for some anxiety conditions [1]. Among eligible people who declined to join, 53 per cent said they did not want internet treatment, and fewer than one in five participants named it as their preferred option [1]. Wellbeing, social anxiety, sleep and physical activity were judged no worse online [1].

**For you.** The authors conclude that guided online therapy should sit alongside traditional options, not replace them, and estimate it could suit at least half of primary care clients [1]. If you are offered it, it is a real treatment with a real therapist behind it. If you are offered only it and you have strong anxiety, it is fair to ask about exposure work, or for the face-to-face route. The trial gives you the words for that conversation.
