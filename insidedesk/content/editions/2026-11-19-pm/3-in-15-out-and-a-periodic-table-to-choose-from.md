Google released Gemini 4 Neon on Wednesday, and the number to keep is not a benchmark but a tariff: $3 per million input tokens, $0.15 per million cached input tokens, and $15 per million output tokens, with a context window of one million tokens [1]. It is rolling out in the Gemini API, in Gemini for business, and to paying Pro and Ultra subscribers in the Gemini app and the Antigravity coding tool [1].

Google's own claims are generous, as launch claims are. Neon scores 61 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at its highest thinking level, third overall, and ranks first on the same firm's Coding Agent Index at 58.6, Google says [1]. At its default setting it costs, by Google's account, 32% less per task than Carbon, the model it calls its most capable [1][2]. The Register lays out the awkward family tree: on that index Neon's 61 beats Carbon's 60, Hydrogen's 54 and Argon's 50, while Carbon and Argon are priced at $4 and $20 per million input and output tokens, non-discounted from next month [2].

Desk arithmetic: at list prices, one million tokens in and one million out costs $18 on Neon and $24 on Carbon or Argon, a quarter less, before caching.

## The menu problem

The contrarian point is The Register's. Google's chemical names do not suggest a hierarchy, and the company appears content with that [2]. Antigravity's Orchestrator can swap models mid-task, for example letting Carbon write a plan and handing the work to the thriftier Hydrogen [1][2]. Model pinning exists, The Register notes, "for those who know what they want" [2]. Google's blog section "Where Neon sits in the Gemini 4 family" says Carbon handles the hardest long-horizon projects, Hydrogen fast everyday work, and Neon "balances depth and efficiency" [1].

If you build on these models, the useful work is unglamorous: pin the model, log which one actually ran, and reconcile the token bill each month against what you would have paid on a single tier. Routing is a convenience. An invoice is the evidence.
