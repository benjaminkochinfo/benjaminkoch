England's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence has recommended two capsule sponge tests, Cytosponge and EndoSign, for detecting Barrett's oesophagus and oesophageal cancer in primary care and community settings, Pulse reported on Thursday [1]. The patient swallows a small capsule on a string; it dissolves in the stomach, leaving a sponge that expands over about seven minutes and is then pulled back up, collecting cells from the lining of the gullet on the way [1].

The target group is deliberately narrow. The tests are an option for people with persistent gastro-oesophageal reflux and several risk factors who do not need an urgent suspected-cancer referral [1]. If the result suggests cancer or Barrett's oesophagus, the next step is still an endoscopy [1]. The sponge does not replace the camera; it decides who should wait for it.

That is where the money and the time sit. The tests cost around £100 each, need no sedation, and can be given by one trained health professional in a GP practice or a community diagnostic centre [1]. NICE's committee said they will help reduce high demand on NHS endoscopy services, and noted that in some areas people with Barrett's oesophagus are not being checked as often as guidance recommends; the sponge can also serve as a monitoring tool for them [1]. An NHS study of EndoSign covered 2,000 high-risk patients in GP surgeries and diagnostic hubs in the East of England, Lancashire and South Cumbria, and Wessex [1]. Studies show 95 in every 100 people manage to swallow the capsule, after one or more attempts [1].

"Oesophageal cancer is very difficult to treat when it is diagnosed at a later stage, so identifying people sooner is really important," said Professor Jonathan Benger, NICE's chief executive [1]. These are the first recommendations from NICE's National HealthTech Access Programme [1].

For you, the point is modest and practical. If you have lived with heartburn for years and swallow antacids like sweets, the useful conversation is with your GP, not with a search engine. Health systems rarely fail for lack of brilliant machines; they fail at the queue in front of them. A £100 sponge that sorts the queue is the dull kind of progress that lasts. The guidance is still a draft, open for consultation until 7 December 2026 [1].
