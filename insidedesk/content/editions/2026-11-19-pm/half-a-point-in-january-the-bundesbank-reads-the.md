The most useful sentence in German money today was not spoken on a podium. It sat in the Bundesbank's monthly report, published on Thursday morning, and it had a date in it. Until the end of the year, the central bank wrote, inflation could hover around its current level, because the fuel discount, the Tankrabatt, is still holding prices down [1]. At the start of the year that discount runs out and several price-raising state measures take effect. "Overall, this could raise the inflation rate by half a percentage point," the Bundesbank said [1].

The current level is 2.8%, the October rate [1]. Desk arithmetic: half a point on top of 2.8% would put the rate around 3.3%, if nothing else moved. Plenty else will move, and the Bundesbank lists it with the dull honesty of an inventory clerk. Gas tariffs are likely to rise noticeably [1]. Higher energy and input costs should gradually pass into the prices of other goods and some services [1]. Food could carry a delayed effect from the summer's heat and drought, and the recent rise in producer prices for crops is a first sign [1]. For petrol and heating oil, crude prices matter, but so does refining capacity in the Middle East and in Russia [1]. The conclusion: "Mainly because of the military conflicts in the Middle East, the upside risks to the inflation outlook continue to dominate" [1].

## The good half of the report

This is not a gloomy report, and it should not be read as one. The Bundesbank expects the economy to grow more strongly in the fourth quarter, after 0.2% in the summer quarter [1][2]. State spending on defence and infrastructure is still pushing, industry has more domestic orders and world trade is expanding, so output and exports should pick up [1]. Low water on key waterways held growth back over the summer, and the bank expects the catch-up to run into the first quarter of 2027 [1]. Private consumption, it says, will stay subdued: strong energy price rises weigh on it despite the discount [1].

In other words, the economy is getting its paperwork back in order. Your household budget is the part still waiting for its January invoice.

## What Berlin has put against it

The coalition has already put two measures on the table: a fuel price cap from 1 January and a cut in electricity tax for 2027, to the European minimum for all consumers, initially for one year [1]. Both are policy, not price. A cap limits a number on a sign; it does not change what a refinery charges for a barrel. A tax cut lowers a line on your electricity bill; it does not touch the gas tariff the Bundesbank expects to rise [1]. The final budget vote is due in the week of 24 to 27 November, and that is where the wording will be fixed.

## The savings side of the ledger

Here is the constructive part, and it is quietly happening in the comparison tables. On Thursday at 09:41 CET, Instabank raised its overnight-account rate to 2.60% from 2.50%; on Wednesday BankB went to 2.66% from 2.60%, and earlier this week Moank and Distingo also moved up [3]. Verivox, which checks about 800 banks daily, says the market "is in motion because many banks are raising their rates", with the best offer at up to 4.25%, mostly as a time-limited deal for new customers [3].

Desk arithmetic: a regular rate of 2.60% is below October's 2.8% inflation, and further below the 3.3% the Bundesbank's half-point would imply. An introductory 4.25% clears both, for as long as the promotion runs. The difference between the two rates on €10,000 is €165 a year before tax. Read the end date of any offer the way you read the Tankrabatt's.

## Why the dollar still matters in Dortmund

Abroad, the day was calmer than Wednesday. The dollar index ended a seven-session rally, and by 15:37 CET the euro had risen 0.2% to $1.1002, back above the $1.10 line it lost this week [4][5]. US jobless claims rose 1,000 to 203,000, and the Philadelphia Fed's factory survey fell to a six-month low of 6.3, while its prices-paid index climbed to 58.2 [6]. Walmart's chief executive John Furner said diesel and transport costs "do work their way into cost of goods" [7]. That is the same chain the Bundesbank describes, told by an American retailer.

## For you

Check three dates this week: when the fuel discount ends (at the turn of the year), when any introductory savings rate ends, and when your gas supplier's next tariff letter is due. None of them is a forecast. All of them are on paper. Nothing here is investment advice.
