A study in the New Zealand Medical Journal, by researchers at the University of Auckland, linked answers to the New Zealand Health Survey from 2011/12 to 2018/19 (91,272 respondents in total) with hospital and prescription records in the national Integrated Data Infrastructure [1]. Distress was measured with the Kessler K10 scale, with 12 or more out of 40 counted as high; service use meant publicly funded hospital stays for mood and anxiety disorders, or antidepressants and anxiolytics dispensed [1].

The headline is a mismatch. After adjustment, there was no meaningful difference in the risk of high psychological distress between Asian New Zealanders and those of sole European ethnicity [1]. Yet every Asian sub-group was less likely to use mental-health services, with adjusted relative risks between 0.32 and 0.59 [1]. Among people who did report high distress, 22.22% of Asian participants had used services, against 48.93% of sole Europeans [1].

Splitting the "Asian" category changes the picture again. Indian participants had the highest share with high distress and the most deprivation; New Zealand-born Indian participants had 2.41 times the risk of high distress of those born overseas [1]. Chinese men were less than half as likely as Chinese women to use services (aRR 0.47), a gap not seen among Indian participants [1]. Across every sub-group, people who had been unable to see a GP or fill a prescription because of cost or transport were 2.24 to 4.53 times as likely to report high distress [1].

The authors are frank about limits. Their "service use" misses GP visits, private care, talking therapies and culturally based help, so some care is uncounted, and they could not measure length of residence [1]. But the gap remains after adjusting for age, sex, deprivation, birthplace and unmet need, which points, they argue, to structural and cultural barriers [1].

Now the annex, the part about what to do with it. The policy lesson travels well beyond New Zealand: a single ethnic box on a form can average a well-served group with a badly served one and report that all is fine. The practical lever the study points to is the most boring one in health care, a GP appointment someone can afford and reach. If you work in a clinic, a school or an HR department, the finding is a prompt: low demand is not the same as low need. Sometimes it only means the door is harder to find.
