The Folketing on Thursday consented to the agreement between Greenland, Denmark and the United States that amends and supplements the 1951 accord on the defence of Greenland [1]. The US ambassador, Ken Howery, put the vote at 109-0 [2]. Greenland's parliament, the Inatsisartut, had approved it on 3 November; the government concluded the deal on 22 September in New York, and can now notify Washington that both parliaments have consented, which completes ratification [1].

According to the Guardian, the deal gives the US underwater access to Greenland and specific permission to build a missile-defence system, recognises Greenland as an equal partner, and stipulates that Greenland would remain in Nato if it became fully independent [2].

"This is a positive development, as it removes all uncertainty surrounding Greenland," foreign minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen said [2]. He also warned that trust with the US had been damaged and that "there is a significant task of rebuilding ahead", and said the US had yet to inform Denmark of any plans for new bases [2].

The contrarian reading is the calm one. A year that began with talk of invasion ends with a treaty text, two parliamentary votes and a notification procedure. Treaties do not guarantee good behaviour. They do give a small country something it can point to, clause by clause, when the mood in a bigger one changes again.
