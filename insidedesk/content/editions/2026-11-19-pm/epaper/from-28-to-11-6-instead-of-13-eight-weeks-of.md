Researchers at Ferdowsi University of Mashhad in Iran randomly assigned 66 women with major depressive disorder, all taking the antidepressant citalopram, either to usual care or to an added eight-week course of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy: weekly two-hour group sessions plus daily home meditation [1]. The study is published in the journal Mindfulness [1].

Both groups started at an average of around 28 on the Beck Depression Inventory-II, in the moderate-to-severe range [1]. After eight weeks the medication-only group averaged about 13; the mindfulness group 11.6, a difference the authors' statistics called robust [1]. Resting EEG recordings showed lower theta and higher low-beta activity in areas likely corresponding to the left insula, and more upper-alpha activity across parts of the right hemisphere [1].

Read the caveats before the brain map. The EEG used 19 scalp electrodes, so the locations are probabilistic estimates, as the authors say [1]. All participants were women, nobody was blinded to their group, and the scores were self-reported, with no long-term follow-up yet [1].

What survives is modest and useful. The pill did most of the work in both arms; structured group practice added a little more. If you or someone close to you is on an antidepressant and still struggling, the evidence supports asking about therapy alongside it, not instead of it.
