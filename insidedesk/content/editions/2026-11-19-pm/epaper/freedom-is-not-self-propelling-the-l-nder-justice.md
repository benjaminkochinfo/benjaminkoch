The justice ministers of Germany's Länder met in Hamburg's city hall on Thursday for their autumn conference, chaired by Hamburg's justice senator Anna Gallina (Greens) [1]. One of the main themes: how to protect the judiciary against extremists [1].

"Attacks on the independent judiciary are a real danger to our democracy," said Lower Saxony's justice minister Kathrin Wahlmann (SPD) [1]. North Rhine-Westphalia's Benjamin Limbach (Greens) added that freedom is "not self-propelling" and stands or falls with a judiciary that decides independently [1].

The concrete part is a proposal from Hamburg, Lower Saxony and North Rhine-Westphalia: a joint working group of the federal government and the Länder to make the courts more robust against "authoritarian-populist and extremist forces", looking in particular at how judges and prosecutors are recruited, appointed and promoted [1]. "Overall, there is still a lot of work ahead of us," Gallina said [1]. The agenda also covers child protection online, the fight against corruption, reform of the animal-protection law and support for victims [1].

The unglamorous word in all this is "promotion". Courts are rarely captured by a speech; they are shaped over years by personnel files, selection panels and the rules for who rises. A working group that reads those rules before anyone needs them is the right kind of dull.
