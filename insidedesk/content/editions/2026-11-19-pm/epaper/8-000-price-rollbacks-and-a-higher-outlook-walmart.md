Walmart reported fiscal third-quarter revenue of $188.84 billion, against $187.27 billion expected by analysts surveyed by LSEG, and adjusted earnings of 65 cents a share, one cent above forecast [1]. It now expects net sales to grow 4.9% to 5.2% this year, up from 4% to 5%, and adjusted earnings per share of $2.84 to $2.89 [1]. Revenue rose 5.2%; net income fell to $4.95 billion from $6.14 billion a year earlier [1].

The growth sat in the unglamorous machinery. Global e-commerce sales rose 17%, driven by pickup and delivery from stores; US advertising sales rose 37%; membership fee income rose 17% [1]. Walmart said it had rolled back prices on nearly 8,000 products [1]. Comparable US sales excluding fuel rose 2.9%, in line with expectations [1].

Chief executive John Furner told CNBC that diesel and transport increases "do work their way into cost of goods", and that tariff refunds had helped the company offset them [1]. Finance chief John David Rainey said the fourth quarter was "off to a pretty good start" [1].

The quarter is a reminder that a retailer's margin is mostly logistics. A lorry's fuel bill reaches the shelf before any speech about prices does. Nothing here is investment advice.
