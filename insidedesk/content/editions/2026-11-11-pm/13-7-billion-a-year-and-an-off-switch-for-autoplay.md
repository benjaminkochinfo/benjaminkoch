The European Commission on Wednesday proposed the Digital Fairness Act, its answer to the small frictions of online life that add up to real money [1]. Its own case is plain: around 60% of Europeans buy online, and online shoppers are over 60% more likely to run into problems than those buying in a shop [1]. Consumer commissioner Michael McGrath called it, together with a reform of cross-border enforcement, "the most significant reform of EU consumer protection rules in more than 20 years" [2].

**What is in it.** A ban on "dark patterns" such as fake claims that a product is almost sold out; prices shown in real time, with the highest possible price, in virtual ticket queues; the option to turn off addictive features such as endless scrolling and autoplay; disclosure rules for influencers; limits on personalised ads and prices aimed at minors; and subscriptions that are easier to cancel, with reminders before free trials turn into paid ones [1][2]. Children would be protected from loot boxes and pay-to-progress mechanics in games, and you would be able to reach a human rather than only a chatbot in customer service [2].

**The invoice on both sides.** The Commission's impact assessment puts the annual benefit to consumers, including time lost, at €13.7 billion, against recurring costs to businesses of €678 million a year [2]. Desk arithmetic: about €20 of benefit for every €1 of cost, on the Commission's own numbers. In large cross-border cases, the enforcement reform would allow fines of up to 6% of global annual turnover, and non-EU companies would need a legal representative in the EU [2].

**The objections are worth hearing.** Bitkom, the German digital association, said the act "pursues the right goals" but would add obligations and red tape without decisively improving protection; its point is that existing rules should be enforced more consistently, including against sellers from outside the EU (our translation) [3]. The digital rights group EDRi argues the opposite worry: that the rules on addictive design still rely too much on you finding the off switch [2]. Both are, at heart, arguments about enforcement and defaults. That is where a law succeeds or fails, long after the press conference.

**For you.** Nothing changes yet; this is a proposal, and it must pass Parliament and the member states [2]. In the meantime the cheapest protection is already yours: put the end date of every free trial in your calendar the day you start it.
