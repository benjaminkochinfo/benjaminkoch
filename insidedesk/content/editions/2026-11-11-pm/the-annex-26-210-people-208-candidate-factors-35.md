The Annex reads the method behind the headline. Today's: researchers at the University of Hong Kong's School of Public Health say they have identified 35 factors linked to psychological resilience, the ability to keep or recover mental health in the face of adversity, in data from 26,210 residents aged 15 and over [1]. The paper is in Nature Mental Health [1].

**The design.** Most resilience studies test one favourite factor at a time, usually in Western samples [1]. This one used the FAMILY Cohort, a population-based prospective cohort, and an "environment-wide association study": it screened all 208 candidate predictors it had, across personal, family, social and lifestyle domains, rather than a pre-selected few [1]. The team says statistical corrections and internal validation were used to cut the risk of false positives and selective reporting [1]. That is the part worth admiring: when you test 208 things, some will look important by luck, and the correction is the institution saying so in advance.

**The result.** The 35 survivors fall into seven domains, from health and life satisfaction to close relationships, financial stability (for example, having medical insurance) and health behaviours such as meeting WHO physical-activity levels [1]. One detail is quietly useful: interactive weekend activities with family, such as exercising, sharing meals or chatting, went with higher resilience over time; passive family time showed no significant link [1]. Links differed by group: family harmony and medical insurance mattered more for women, physical activity and religious involvement more for men, and most associations were stronger in people over 60 than in those aged 15 to 24 [1].

**The footnote that matters.** The lead author, Dr Michael Ni Yuxuan, says it plainly: "this observational study does not establish causality" [1]. People who volunteer may be resilient because they volunteer, or volunteer because they are resilient. The study cannot tell which.

**For you.** The list reads like common sense, and that is not a criticism. If you want one thing from it, take the difference between doing something with your family and merely being in the same room. It costs nothing to test on yourself this weekend.
