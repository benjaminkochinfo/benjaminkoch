Millions of people in Spain joined the country's first general strike in 14 years on Wednesday, a 24-hour stoppage backed by the largest unions and tenants' associations under the slogan "Salaries. Housing. Prices" [1]. Unions reported 90% participation in the car industry and ports and 82% in waste collection, but said turnout was uneven across the country [1]. In Catalonia, CCOO and UGT estimated an average of 84% [2]; union figures reported by EFE put Extremadura and Castilla-La Mancha at about 41% [3]. The employers' federation CEOE saw "low participation" in most sectors and pointed to the grid: after 08:00, electricity demand was down about 10%, less than the drops of more than 14% in earlier strikes [3]. By midday, officials said 27 people had been arrested over minor incidents [1].

The grievance has numbers behind it. Rents have risen by 80% in a decade and home prices by 63%, as more flats became tourist lets, short-term rentals and investments [1]. Tenants' unions want limits on evictions of vulnerable people, rent freezes and a crackdown on short-term lets [1].

The politics are tight. Spain votes on 29 November in a snap election called by Pedro Sánchez after right-wing parties blocked his emergency housing legislation; polls point to a PP–Vox coalition [1]. Sánchez praised the strike; Madrid's regional president Isabel Díaz Ayuso said strikers were "holding citizens hostage" [1].

For you: a strike is loud for a day. Rents are paid every month. Whoever governs after 29 November will be judged on permits, completions and lease terms, the dull machinery that decides what a flat costs.
