Ask about 8,000 people born in England, Scotland and Wales in one week of 1970 what matters most, and they answer health: 9.3 on a scale of 1 to 10, for men and women and across income groups [1]. Friendships scored 8.9, marriage or partnership 8.7, independence 8.6; a fulfilling job 7.9, home ownership 7.8, children 7.5, and money came last at 6.1 [1]. The figures come from a new briefing by the UCL Centre for Longitudinal Studies, using 1970 British Cohort Study data collected in 2021–2024 [1].

The gap between value and condition is the story. A quarter (26%) rate their health as "poor" or "fair", and one in five (20%) report symptoms of psychological distress [1]. Women were more likely than men to report distress (26% against 15%), and people in the lowest-income households four times as likely as the most affluent to have a high number of symptoms (37% against 9%) [1]. Compared with baby boomers entering their fifties in 2008, Generation X is less likely to report excellent or very good health (41% against 50%) [1].

There is a constructive line in the data too. A third (33%) have seen a doctor about their mental health, and women in this generation were more likely than boomer women at the same age to have done so (42% against 32%) [1]. "Some may be struggling with the aspect of life most important to them," says co-author Professor George Ploubidis [1]. Asking for help is not a symptom. It is the system being used as intended.
