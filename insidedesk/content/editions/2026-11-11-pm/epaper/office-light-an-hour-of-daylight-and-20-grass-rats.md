Occupational health usually means ladders, solvents and noise. A study from Michigan State University and UC Berkeley, published in the Journal of Biological Rhythms, asks about something quieter: the light you work under [1].

The researchers used Nile grass rats, which, unlike almost all laboratory rodents, are active by day, as people are [1]. In one experiment, eight males cycled through four lighting conditions for two weeks; in another, 20 males lived under either daylight-equivalent or fluorescent office lighting for six weeks [1]. Animals under office light showed the weakest daily rhythms, and a single hour of daylight-equivalent light at midday partly restored them [1]. Four of the ten fluorescent-lit animals developed diabetic-range blood glucose, against one of ten in the daylight group, and their sperm motility was less than half that of the daylight group, though sperm counts were unchanged [1].

The numbers are small and the subjects are rodents, and the team says as much: it calls the findings promising early evidence and asks for more work to see whether the effects occur in humans [1]. "Light is not just something that helps us see," says co-author Lily Yan [1].

The useful thing for an employer or a building manager is that this is a question with cheap answers to test. For you, a midday walk outside is not a prescription. It is a habit that costs a lunch break and nothing else.
