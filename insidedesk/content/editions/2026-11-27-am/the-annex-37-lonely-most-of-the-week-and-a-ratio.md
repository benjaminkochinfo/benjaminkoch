**What happened.** A study published on 23 November in the Bundesgesundheitsblatt draws on CRONOS-3, a sub-survey of the European Social Survey run between 2024 and 2026 in 11 countries, with 7,968 respondents in the analysis [1]. Its authors, Insa Backhaus-Hoven and Terje Andreas Eikemo, looked at who reports poor mental health, and what travels with it [1].

**The numbers.** About 6% of all respondents reported poor or very poor mental health, but 12.5% of adults aged 18 to 34 against 4.7% of older adults [1]. Then the striking finding: 37% of respondents had felt lonely most of the time during the previous week [1]. Among the young, 52.2% felt at least moderately lonely, against 32% of older adults [1]. In the regression, loneliness carried a prevalence ratio of 5.85 for poor subjective mental health, lack of social support 2.34 and lack of childhood friendship 2.54 [1].

**What a ratio is not.** A prevalence ratio of 5.85 means poor mental health was reported almost six times as often among the lonely, after adjusting for age, gender, education and country [1]. It does not prove loneliness causes it; the authors stress that all measures are self-reported and that a single item is not a diagnosis [1].

**Why it matters.** The study also found younger adults reported more unmet need for mental health care [1]. The authors argue that access to care matters but must come with efforts to reduce loneliness and strengthen social ties [1]. That is less glamorous than an app and more like urban planning: clubs, libraries, sports halls, the bus that still runs on a Sunday.

**What to take from it.** If you are one of the 37%, you are not unusual; you are in the largest minority in the room. The cheapest public-health measure in Europe may still be the invitation that is actually sent.
