Nvidia has told manufacturing partners such as Dell and Supermicro not to ship servers containing its chips to buyers in Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia, the Financial Times reported, citing three people familiar with the matter [1]. It is unclear whether the halt is temporary [1]. Nvidia declined to comment [1].

The reason is a rule, not a slump. The US Commerce Department introduced measures last week to stop Chinese companies renting access to advanced chips through cloud providers in the region [1]. Cloud providers have 90 days to obtain a licence by proving their end users do not include restricted entities [1]. "There's this new rule but no guidance on execution," one cloud executive told the FT. "It's causing sheer chaos in the region" [1].

The sums are large. Nvidia booked $104 billion of revenue through its Singapore subsidiary in 2025, a 72% increase in a year, though that unit also records some Taiwan and China revenue [1]. TechCrunch, citing the Wall Street Journal, notes an Institute for Progress estimate that remote data centres like these supply about 24% of China's AI computing power [2]. Bankers have stopped about $500 million of credit to one Singapore cloud project tied to ByteDance, according to the FT [1].

The lesson is plain. For years, export controls lived in legal text, and the loophole lived in leases. Now compliance has moved to the loading dock, where it is measured in pallets that do not leave. The data-centre boom in Malaysia and Indonesia was partly built on Chinese demand [1]. A licence regime with a deadline and no guidance is how a policy becomes a balance-sheet problem for people who never met a policymaker.
