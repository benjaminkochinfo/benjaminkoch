A NORC survey of 1,009 US adults, taken in April and published this month, measured social disconnection three ways at once [1]. 39% were at risk of social isolation, 45% were not broadly socially integrated and 36% were lonely [1]. Together, 70% met the threshold on at least one measure, 38% on at least two and 12% on all three [1].

The measures correlate only modestly; isolation and loneliness at r=0.21, integration and loneliness at r=0.11 [1]. In plain words: the person with few contacts, the person who joins nothing and the person who feels lonely are often different people.

Age cuts the other way from the stereotype. Adults over 60 were the least lonely, at 24%; 18- to 29-year-olds the most, at 48%, and 15% of them met all three thresholds [1]. Lower income and being unmarried also raised every risk [1].

The margin of error is 4 percentage points [1]. The practical point survives it: one programme will not fix three problems. A lunch club, a volunteer rota and a counsellor serve different people, and a town needs all three.
