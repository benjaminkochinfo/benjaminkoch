The coldest night of the British autumn so far was Thursday's, and it is about to lose that title. The Met Office recorded -8.4°C at Tulloch Bridge in the Scottish Highlands overnight, with sub-zero readings in Northern Ireland, while the south stayed much milder [1]. Friday night is forecast to be colder still: around -8°C to -9°C in one or two Highland glens, and frost spreading south under clear skies [1]. Where snow is lying in rural Scotland, BBC Weather says it could reach -10°C [3].

The cold came on a northerly wind carrying Arctic air, after the season's first snow warning on Tuesday, when more than an inch fell in parts of northern England [1][3]. The UK Health Security Agency has a yellow cold health alert in force for the north-west, the north-east and Yorkshire and the Humber until 9am on Monday; yellow means a moderate likelihood that the weather increases demand on health services and the risk to vulnerable people [1].

The weekend then splits the country in two. Saturday starts frosty almost everywhere, -1°C to -3°C even in some towns, then turns sunny and crisp [2]. But an Atlantic front reaches Cornwall on Saturday evening and runs into cold, dry air held in place by high pressure to the north [2]. The Met Office's explanation is the useful part: the clash slows the front and strengthens it, so the rain gets heavier while barely moving [2]. Some 30–50 mm could fall in the wettest places such as Dartmoor on Sunday, more than 75 mm in a reasonable worst case, while the north stays dry and frosty at 7°C to 9°C [2].

There were already 12 flood alerts in England on Friday after the wet spell [1]. And the weather showed up in the shop figures too: clothing and footwear stores reported 1.5% growth in sales volumes in the latest official retail data, which the Guardian links to more typical autumn weather after a warm September [1]. Cold is not only a forecast; it is a coat bought, a boiler switched on, and a ward that fills up.
