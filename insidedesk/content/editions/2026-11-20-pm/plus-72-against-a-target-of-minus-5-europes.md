The European Union set itself five antibiotic targets for 2030, all measured against 2019. This week, for European Antibiotic Awareness Day, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control published where the bloc stands, and the scorecard is lopsided: one target met, one close, three far off [1].

The good line first. Bloodstream infections with meticillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, MRSA, are down 19% since 2019, against a target of minus 15% [1]. And the share of human antibiotic use coming from the World Health Organization's first-line "Access" group rose from 61.1% to 62.5%, close to the 65% goal [1]. ECDC adds a caveat even to the success: MRSA infections have risen in the past two years, which "now threatens" that progress [1].

Then the hard numbers. Total antibiotic consumption in humans was only 5% lower in 2025 than in 2019, against a target of minus 20% [1]. Bloodstream infections with third-generation cephalosporin-resistant E. coli rose 16.4%, where the target is minus 10% [1]. And carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae, a bacterium resistant to the antibiotics hospitals keep in reserve for severe infections, accounted for a 72% increase in bloodstream infections since 2019, against a target of minus 5% [1]. ECDC links it to high mortality and to spread inside healthcare facilities [1].

The remedies ECDC lists are not glamorous: hand hygiene, protective equipment, isolating patients in time, cleaning, and behind that enough infection-control staff, laboratory diagnostics and isolation rooms [1]. Its 2026 campaign targets parents, because 90% of human antibiotic use happens in the community and outpatient sector, not in hospitals, and children are often exposed: colds, coughs and sore throats are mostly viral [1].

"We are still behind with several of the EU's 2030 targets," said ECDC director Pamela Rendi-Wagner, pointing to faster genomic surveillance as the new tool for tracing how resistant clones spread [1]. The arithmetic of the scorecard is simple: the target that moved was the one hospitals could attack with routines. The ones that did not move depend on millions of prescriptions written one at a time.
