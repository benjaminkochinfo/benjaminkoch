On a day when chip shares fell hard again on both sides of the Atlantic, the most informative document in technology was not a price. It was a spreadsheet of export licences, released on Thursday afternoon in Washington by the chairman of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, Brian Mast, under his powers in the Export Control Reform Act [1]. Mast called it "the most detailed public disclosure in the history of the Export Control Reform Act" [1]. It deserves to be read before the tape, because it answers a question the tape cannot: how much of the China story is paper, and how much is silicon.

**The numbers in the file.** Between December 2025 and May 2026, companies applied to ship more than 3.4 million AI chips to China, including Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X [3]. The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security approved more than 879,000 of them [1][2]. Four of the buyers sit on the Pentagon's list of Chinese military companies: Tencent with 150,000 chips, Alibaba with 75,000, Baidu with 43,000 and BYD with 4,000; together they account for more than 30% of the approvals [3]. Mast also said two firms named in Nvidia's applications, Maginfra and New Matrix, were suspected "smuggling fronts", and called them "fake companies" [2]. DIGITIMES reported on Friday that ByteDance and Alibaba sought approval to send more than 1.1 million Nvidia AI chips to data centres in Malaysia, Indonesia and Thailand [6].

**What the file does not say.** An approval is not a shipment. How many of the licensed chips reached China is not known; a Commerce official told the committee in July there had been "very few" shipments [3]. Nvidia said it sold $2.4 billion of licensed Hopper chips, the family that includes the H200, to China in its latest quarter [3], and that China and Hong Kong customers made up about 9.5% of all revenue [2]. Its spokesperson said approvals were "subject to strict conditions and limited volumes" and that "despite receiving some licenses, we have since stopped shipping H200s to China" [2]. AMD said it "complies with applicable U.S. export control laws and regulations" [2].

**The contrarian reading.** Bernstein's Stacy Rasgon, asked by CNBC, did the arithmetic out loud: 879,000 of 3.4 million is "about one out of every four", "not that many", and in the context of the market "a rounding error. It's tiny" [2]. That is the part the sell-off skips. The file describes a queue in which three in four requests did not get through, with sales further limited because Beijing did not approve large-scale imports, according to the analyst Patrick Moorhead [2]. The scandal is real; the volumes are modest. Both things can be true, and only one of them is in the share price.

**The tape, for the record.** Nvidia traded at $137.50 at 11:11 New York time (17:11 CET), down 5.8% on the day and about 14% on the week, on course for a seventh straight weekly loss, its longest such run since 2008 [4]. At roughly $3.3 trillion it is now worth less than Alphabet at about $3.4 trillion [4]. AMD was down 9.1%, Broadcom 8.7% and the Philadelphia semiconductor index 6.6% at that point [4]. In Europe, ANSA's 17:09 CET snapshot had ASML down 9.56%, Aixtron 9.35%, STMicroelectronics 9.3% and Soitec 11.29%, with the cable maker Prysmian, which supplies server and data-centre gear, down 6.29% [5]. That last one is the industrial side of the story: the people who sell copper and switchgear to data centres are being repriced alongside the people who sell the chips.

**Where this leaves the calm reader.** The licence file is now public, the hearings are dated, and the rules changed this week: on Monday Commerce issued an interim final rule closing the loophole that let Chinese companies rent Nvidia computing power from data centres in third countries [2]. That is a lot of paperwork, which is good news of a kind: it can be checked. What it cannot yet tell you is whether next quarter's invoices from Malaysia or Thailand will look different. Those numbers arrive with earnings, not with Friday afternoons.
