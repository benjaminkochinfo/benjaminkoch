Since Friday, 20 November, Germany's consumer-credit law covers payments that until now barely counted as credit [1]. It implements the new EU Consumer Credit Directive and applies to all consumer loans signed from today; older contracts generally stay under the old rules [1]. Newly covered are loans under €200, many "buy now, pay later" options at online checkouts, loans running less than three months and zero-percent financing for furniture or electronics [1].

**The checkout changes first.** Lenders must now check whether you are likely to repay, using income, spending and your financial circumstances; a credit-bureau score alone is not enough, and social-media data and sensitive data such as health may not be used [1]. Online this runs in real time in the background: if the check says you cannot afford the instalments, the pay-later option is simply not shown, and you pay now, by card or in advance [2]. BaFin's own examples of what now counts include concert tickets for €180 split into interest-free instalments, and clothes paid by a payment service with the debit pushed back ten weeks [2]. From today, pay-later credits also feed into credit scores, because bureaus receive the data through the checks [2].

The classic invoice purchase usually stays outside, as long as the shop itself grants free deferral: up to 50 days for small and mid-sized firms, up to 14 days for large online retailers [1][2].

**Three new protections are worth knowing.** If an automated check rejects you, you can demand an understandable explanation and a review by a person [1]. A loan is usually void if its annual percentage rate exceeds the usual market rate for comparable loans by 100% or by 12 percentage points, whichever comes first: at a 7% market rate the line is 14%, at 15% it is 27% [1]. And a loan can now be concluded without a signature, but a pre-ticked box is not enough [1].

**For you.** Debt counsellors rate buy-now-pay-later as the most problematic credit product, according to a survey by the federation of consumer organisations [1]. The Verbraucherzentralen's advice is dull and effective: write down every running instalment, including small ones with different providers, before adding a new one [1]. Not advice, just arithmetic.
