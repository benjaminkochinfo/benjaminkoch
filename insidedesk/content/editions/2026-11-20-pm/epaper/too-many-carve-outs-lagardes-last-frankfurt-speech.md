Christine Lagarde spoke at the Frankfurt European Banking Congress for the eighth and, she said, last time as ECB president on Friday [1]. The sharpest line was about a deal most savers have never heard of. "The Council has just agreed its position on strengthening the supervision of capital markets, which is welcome," she said. "But the position weakens the European supervisor's ability to take effective decisions and includes too many carve-outs" [1].

Her argument is about slow losses. "Each year without it, more of our savings are invested abroad and more of our growing firms look elsewhere for funding. Yet no single year's loss is large enough to overcome the resistance to change," she said [1]. Europe has spent more than a decade agreeing on the need for a capital markets union without overcoming vested interests, Econostream summarised [2].

Bundesbank president Joachim Nagel, on the same stage, made a similar point without naming the Council. National responsibility for financial-market regulation might seem more comfortable but is not the best way to optimise the system, he said, adding: "often I have the feeling that there is still a lot of dogmatism when you talk about the savings and investment union" [3].

Two central bankers asking governments to give up supervisory powers is not new. What is new is the timing: the Council has fixed its position, so the carve-outs Lagarde objects to are now the starting point of the talks with Parliament, not a draft. For a saver the stakes are indirect but real: deeper European markets would mean more ways to invest at home, and more European firms worth investing in. Nothing here is investment advice.
