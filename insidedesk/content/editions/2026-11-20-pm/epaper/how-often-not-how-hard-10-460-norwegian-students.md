Researchers led by Michael Grasdalsmoen of Western Norway University of Applied Sciences followed 10,460 full-time students aged 18 to 35, as published in PLOS One [1]. At the start, the students described how often, how hard and how long they exercised; a year later they completed a self-administered diagnostic assessment for common mental disorders [1].

Students who reported less physical activity were more likely to meet criteria for anxiety or depression a year later [1]. But the dimensions did not weigh the same. Frequency was the most consistent factor, more reliably linked to later mental health than intensity or time spent [1]. There were sex differences: among women, lower intensity went with higher risk; among men, fewer total weekly hours did [1]. Overall, the likelihood of common disorders fell as weekly exercise rose, up to about 10 hours a week [1]. The findings fit the WHO recommendation of at least 150 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity a week [1].

The authors stress the limit: an observational study cannot show that exercise prevented the disorders [1]. Students who were already struggling may simply have moved less.

Still, the practical reading is friendly to busy people. A short walk four times a week is easier to keep than a punishing session once, and on this evidence regularity is the part that counts.
