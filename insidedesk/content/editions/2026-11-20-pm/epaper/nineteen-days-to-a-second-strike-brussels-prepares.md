The European Commission is considering changes to its trade Enforcement Regulation so that Brussels can respond faster to serious economic threats, as it develops a new instrument aimed mainly at China, POLITICO reported this week, citing three people familiar with the talks [1]. The work follows a Franco-German request in October for a "second strike" tool [1]. It would be part of a broader trade-defence package, with the formal proposal currently scheduled for 9 December [1].

The design is about who pulls the trigger. A qualified majority of EU countries would be needed to block a decision to use the new tool, a far lower hurdle than for the Anti-Coercion Instrument, which needs a qualified majority to be triggered and has never been used [1]. The backdrop is the EU's €1-billion-a-day trade deficit with China [1]. Bernd Lange, who chairs Parliament's trade committee, cautioned that using the Enforcement Regulation without a WTO dispute behind it could be "a bit tricky" [1]. A weapon that is easier to fire is also easier to be accused of firing.
