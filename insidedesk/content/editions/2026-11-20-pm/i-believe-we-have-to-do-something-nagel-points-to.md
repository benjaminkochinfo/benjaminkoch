Joachim Nagel did not leave much room for doubt. Asked about the European Central Bank's December meeting at the Frankfurt European Banking Congress on Friday, the Bundesbank president said: "I believe we have to do something" [1][2]. Reuters reported that he repeated his recent message that tightening was likely, with euro-zone inflation running at twice the ECB's 2% target [2].

The numbers he gave are blunt. Inflation is about 3% in Germany and 4% across the currency union, both too high, Nagel said, according to Econostream [1]. It could rise further at the beginning of next year, though he pointed to an offsetting effect from an energy-tax decision [1]. The ECB has already raised rates twice, bringing the relevant rate to 2.50% [1]. A further move, he said, would leave policy "slightly in the restricted territory" [1].

Then the reassurance. Tightening "will not undermine" the economic forecast, Nagel argued, and returning inflation to 2% is a precondition for growth; medium-term inflation expectations remain well anchored [1][2]. He put German growth at roughly 1.3% this year and next, somewhat lower in 2028, and recalled that the IMF's autumn meetings had, unusually, upgraded Germany's outlook [1].

Here is the contrarian reading. A rate rise that costs no growth would be a rare thing, and Nagel himself described four years of a "roller coaster" and could not exclude uncertainty rising to "even higher elevated levels" [1]. The December meeting brings new projections, including a first forecast for 2029 [1]. If those keep 1.3% while rates rise, the forecast is doing the persuading.

For you: variable-rate loans and overdrafts follow the ECB with a lag; fixed-term deposits tend to price in expected moves early. Check what your bank pays now, not what it advertises after December. Nothing here is investment advice.
