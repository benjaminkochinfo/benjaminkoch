**The podium.** In her eighth and last keynote at the Frankfurt European Banking Congress as ECB president, Christine Lagarde turned from money to machines [1]. Where a challenge needs a European answer, she said, Europe should strengthen the institutions that can give it; in technology, that means European public-private partnerships, "with public money, including from the EU budget, taking the first losses and drawing in private capital" [1]. Lagarde steps down in February, CNBC noted [2].

**The print.** Her own speech carries the bill. Citing a September study by the KIRA Center, she said one recent estimate puts the investment needed to lift Europe's share of the world's AI computing capacity from around 5% to 15% by 2030 at around €1.3 trillion [1]. She was frank about why that money is not flowing: "as long as we can buy computing power from foreign providers and frontier AI models remain available to us, the case for paying that price is hard to make" [1].

**The gap.** The two lines describe different animals. A first-loss tranche is a cushion: public money takes the first hit so private investors will put up the rest. It works when the project is roughly sound and the risk is the unknown. €1.3 trillion over about four years to triple a market share is not a cushion problem; it is a demand problem. Europe's buyers can still rent compute from abroad, and Lagarde's own words say that is why they do [1]. A guarantee changes who loses if a data centre stands empty. It does not fill it. CNBC adds the political friction: data-centre projects are becoming more controversial, complex and costly, and opposition to them weighed with voters in the US midterms [2].

**For you.** None of this reaches a household bill soon. But "EU budget taking the first losses" is a phrase to remember when the next long-term budget is negotiated: it is a liability, and it is shared.
