At its GTC conference in Berlin on Wednesday, Nvidia announced the DSX Pod AI Factory: seven of its Vera Rubin AI racks packed into a unit roughly the size of a standard shipping container, weighing about 28,000 kilograms, and designed to travel by truck on normal roads [1]. The cabling and liquid cooling are assembled at the factory [1].

**What is actually new.** Not the chips. The change is where the work is done. "The only thing that has to happen when it gets to the site is it's placed in its location, connected to the rest of the other containers, and validated through final burning and testing," Ian Buck, Nvidia's vice president of hyperscale computing, told reporters [1]. Nvidia says this cuts the time for a new AI data centre to start serving tokens from six months to 14 days [1]. That figure is the company's own and is untested until the first pods arrive.

**Why Europe gets a mention.** Nvidia said the pod is for customers worldwide and could help with deployment problems faced by European customers [1]. It did not spell out which. What the design does is move the cabling and cooling work from the site into a factory hall, where it can be done in series and checked before the unit leaves [1].

**What it does not change.** A pod still has to be placed, connected to the other containers and tested on site [1], and the site still needs its power connection; that does not come on the truck. Nvidia gave no price; analysts estimate a single Vera Rubin rack at more than $7 million [1]. Deliveries are expected from early 2027 through manufacturing partners including Foxconn, Gigabyte, Lenovo, HPE, Quanta and Pegatron [1].

**The direction of travel.** Nvidia has moved from selling single graphics chips to servers, then racks, and now containers of racks [1]. Jensen Huang said recently that what Nvidia sells into one gigawatt of data-centre capacity has gone from about $18 billion to "$40-plus billion per gigawatt" [1]. The investor question this week, as Meta and Tesla report, is whether buyers keep spending at that rate [2]. The engineering question is simpler and more useful: how much of a building can be made in a factory.
