Researchers from the University of Münster and Charité in Berlin followed 334 psychiatric inpatients who stayed at least four weeks, with questionnaires on admission, roughly every two weeks and just before discharge [1]. The work appeared in the Journal of Affective Disorders [1].

As depression scores fell, the patients described their personalities differently: neuroticism went down, conscientiousness and extraversion went up [1]. Those with higher average neuroticism improved more slowly on 14 of the 21 symptoms measured, from sadness to insomnia [1]. Higher extraversion went with faster relief on seven, including work difficulty, indecisiveness and pessimism [1].

The limits are stated plainly. The data are observational, treatments varied, and the personality test was a brief 15-item version with limited precision [1]. Above all, a depressed person tends to judge themselves harshly, so the shift may simply be a return to the patient's usual self as the mood lifts [1].

That caveat is the useful part. A trait score taken on a bad week is a reading of the week as much as of the person. Clinicians who note it, and plan for a slower course where needed, are using the finding as intended.
