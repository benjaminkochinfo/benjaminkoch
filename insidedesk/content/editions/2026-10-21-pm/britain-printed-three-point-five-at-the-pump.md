The Office for National Statistics said on Wednesday that consumer prices rose 3.5% in the 12 months to September, up from 3.1% in August and the highest rate since October 2025 [1]. Investing.com called it more than expected, and London's FTSE 100 lagged the continent in the morning [3].

**Where it came from.** Transport made the largest upward contribution, "particularly motor fuels" [1]. Petrol averaged 169.5 pence a litre in September, up 8.2p in a month and the highest since August 2022; diesel averaged 192.5p, up 10.7p, the highest since July 2022 [1]. Motor fuel prices were 29.8% higher than a year earlier [1]. Heating oil (kerosene) rose 22.7% in the month [1].

**Where it did not.** Core inflation, which leaves out energy, food, alcohol and tobacco, rose from 2.6% to 2.8% [1]. Food inflation stayed at 1.3%, the lowest since 2021 [1]. Services rose from 3.4% to 3.7%, partly on air fares, which fell less in September than a year earlier [1]. Desk arithmetic: the gap between headline and core is 0.7 percentage points.

**The same picture in Frankfurt's data.** The euro area showed the split more sharply. Headline HICP was 3.8% in September; core was 2.5% [2]. Desk arithmetic: a gap of 1.3 points. The ECB's deposit rate is also 2.5%, after rises in June and September [2]. Lagarde told the IMF's steering committee last week that wages have "so far shown no material response to the energy shock" [2].

**What to take from it.** A fuel shock shows up first in the headline and only later, if at all, in the core. The chart shows the deposit rate now level with core inflation, and the headline line well above both. That is the position the Governing Council takes into its meeting on 29 October. Britain's print adds a second witness, not a new verdict. Nothing here is advice; it is a reading of published numbers.
