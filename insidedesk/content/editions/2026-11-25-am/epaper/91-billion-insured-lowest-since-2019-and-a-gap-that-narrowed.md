Global insured losses from natural catastrophes were about $91 billion in the first nine months of 2026, the lowest for the period since 2019 and well below the five-year average of $135 billion, according to Aon's third-quarter recap, as summarised by actuary.info [1]. Economic losses were about $204 billion, and the protection gap, the share not insured, was 55%, against a long-term average of 71% [1].

The shape matters more than the total. Aon counted 23 events with insured losses above $1 billion, against an average of 14, and none above $10 billion [1]. Europe's season-long drought was the costliest insured event at more than $8.5 billion; in France, clay shrink-swell subsidence produced a record $5 billion insured loss, and the government introduced a new subsidence risk map on 1 July covering about 55% of the country [1].

That is the figure to carry into the 1 January renewals. A quiet year for giant losses can soften reinsurance prices; a year of many mid-sized ones keeps the cost with primary insurers and, in the end, with policyholders. Drought that cracks a house is slow, unspectacular and expensive. It now has its own map.
