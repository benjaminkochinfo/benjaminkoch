The Annex reads the method behind the headline. Today's: a brief report by Hidehito Honda, Hirokazu Kumazaki and Kazuhiro Ueda, published on 12 November in Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, on how people with and without autism spectrum disorder (ASD) turn probability words such as "likely" or "doubtful" into numbers, and into decisions [1].

**The design.** Participants read a hypothetical scenario: a friend is considering an expensive migraine treatment, and a doctor describes its chance of success either as a number ("a 30% chance") or in words that lean positive ("it is possible") or negative ("it is unlikely to succeed") [2]. They said how strongly they would recommend it, and separately translated each verbal phrase into a number [1][2]. A hierarchical Bayesian model, followed by cluster analysis, sorted people by how much the wording's direction and their own numerical translation drove their choice [1].

**The headcount, read closely.** The university release says 48 people with ASD and 90 without [2]. The abstract is more precise: Experiment 1 had 48 participants with ASD and 41 without "in the final analyses", and Experiment 2 an independent sample of 49 without ASD [1]. Desk arithmetic: 41 + 49 = 90, so the totals agree, but the direct comparison inside one experiment is 48 against 41. That is a small study, and the journal labels it a brief report [1].

**The result.** Three profiles [1][2]. One, mostly people with ASD, was swayed more by positive or negative framing and less by the numbers they had inferred. A second, mostly people without ASD, leaned on the inferred numbers. A third, mixed, weighed both [2]. Participants with ASD also gave more similar numbers to different phrases [1]. The authors are careful: participants varied in age and intellectual ability, so the differences cannot be attributed to ASD alone, and what places someone in a cluster is still unknown [2].

**For you.** The finding that travels is not about autism: "even subtle differences in wording can influence people's decisions," says Ueda [2]. When a clinician tells you something is "unlikely", it is fair, and not rude, to ask what that means in numbers, and to say back what you understood.
