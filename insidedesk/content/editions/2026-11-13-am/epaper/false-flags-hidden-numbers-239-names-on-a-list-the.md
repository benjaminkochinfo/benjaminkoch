In a crisis, the rulebook is the first thing to look old-fashioned and the last thing you want to lose. International Maritime Organization Secretary-General Arsenio Dominguez urged member states on 11 November to act on violations of maritime rules around the Strait of Hormuz [1]. He cited reports of substandard vessels, ships concealing their IMO identification numbers, fraudulent registrations and false flags, and ships transiting without meeting basic requirements such as the SOLAS safety convention [1].

"IMO's technical and operational regulations are there to protect seafarers, ships and the marine environment," he said. "They must be respected and enforced effectively" [1]. He called on flag, port and coastal states to take measures within their jurisdictions, and on owners to avoid trade that exposes crews to unacceptable risks [1].

The setting is tense. Iran's Persian Gulf Strait Authority has reportedly expanded its blacklist to 239 vessels [1]. On 10 November, Gulf Cooperation Council defence ministers agreed to set up a forward headquarters for the bloc's Unified Military Command in Kuwait [1]. Iran's military claims four tankers were struck by explosions in the strait, a figure no maritime authority has confirmed [2]. Traffic remains a trickle: three transits on 8 November against a pre-crisis baseline of 85 a day, according to IMF PortWatch data cited by Straits [2].

For you: unglamorous paperwork, such as registries, flags and identification numbers, is what lets an insurer, a port and a crew know who is on the water. Enforcing it is not a ceasefire, but it is a floor.
