The dollar broke back above 160 yen this week for the first time since the start of September, on bets that the Federal Reserve will keep raising rates into the new year, AFP reports [2]. By Friday lunchtime in Tokyo it had eased back: AFP had it at 159.89 yen and Bloomberg at 159.91 [2][3].

The reason it eased is a ministry, not a market. Japan's chief currency official, Atsushi Mimura, said the recent moves looked "too fast", and Japan spent ¥15.4 trillion ($96.4 billion) on intervention this summer, according to a Bloomberg report cited by CNBC TV18 [1]. "Markets are jittery about potential FX intervention whenever we approach the 160 area," Win Thin of Bank of Nassau 1982 told Bloomberg [3].

Behind the drama sits arithmetic about interest. US two-year yields rose 14 basis points on Thursday, and Japan's ten-year yield, though rising, stood at 2.870% on Friday [1][3]. Money flows towards the higher rate; a weak yen is the price of that gap.

The euro was little changed at $1.1060 [3]. For you, the practical points are modest. If you are planning a trip to Japan or paying a Japanese supplier, the yen is near its weakest against the dollar since September, but an official intervention can move it abruptly, so it is worth not betting the budget on a single day's rate. And if you hold yen savings, remember they are a bet on two central banks, not one. Nothing here is investment advice.
