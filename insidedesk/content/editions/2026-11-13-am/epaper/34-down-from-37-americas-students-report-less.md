The 2025–2026 Healthy Minds Study, based on responses from 67,477 students at 107 US colleges and universities, found 34% screened positive for moderate or severe depression, down from 37% in the previous report [1]. Moderate or severe anxiety fell from 32% to 30%, suicidal ideation from 11% to 10% [1]. Positive mental health, measured through relationships, self-esteem, purpose and optimism, rose from 36% to 43% [1].

"The data are truly encouraging," says co-principal investigator Justin Heinze, before adding that "clearly there is still a lot of need" [1]. A quarter of students reported non-suicidal self-injury in the past year, 28% screened at risk for an eating disorder, and two-thirds said emotional difficulties had hurt their academic work in the previous four weeks [1].

Care reaches many: 61% of those screening positive for depression or anxiety received counselling, medication or both [1]. The barriers are mundane: time (23%), money (19%), not knowing where to go (12%) [1]. And 7% received counselling or support from an AI chatbot such as ChatGPT, which the team says raises questions about safety and effectiveness [1].

The companion staff survey is the quieter story: of 5,557 faculty and staff, 48% had a one-to-one conversation with a student about mental health, only about half felt confident recognising distress, and 28% reported high or very high burnout [1]. Only 30% said their institution prioritised employee mental health, against 61% for students [1]. A support system is people, and people need support too.
