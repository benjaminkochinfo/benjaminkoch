When a strait closes, the trade does not stop wanting to move; it looks for a longer road. For Saudi crude that road is the East-West pipeline, which ends at the Red Sea port of Yanbu. AFP describes it as increasingly important because it lets the world's largest crude exporter reach customers while Iran blockades the Strait of Hormuz [2]. On Thursday, the detour itself became the headline.

Yemen's Houthis said they had attacked a pumping station on the line, an oil facility in Yanbu and an Aramco building in Riyadh [1]. Their spokesman said one drone "targeted Pumping Station No. 7", and a ballistic missile the Yanbu facility [2]. The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted and destroyed two drones, one in the Riyadh region and one in the Eastern Province [1]. Nobody has confirmed damage at the pumping station or at Yanbu [2]. These are one side's claims, and should be read as such.

The market did not wait for confirmation. Brent settled at $95.82 a barrel, up $3.06 or 3.3%; WTI at $88.74, up $2.21 [1]. By 03:44 CET on Friday, Straits showed Brent at $95.86 [3]. Context matters, though: both benchmarks were still down about 2.5% on the week, and OPEC+ data showed crude output rose in October, driven by higher Saudi production [1]. The physical picture in the strait is unchanged and grim: IMF PortWatch recorded three transits on 8 November against a pre-crisis baseline of 85 a day [3].

The useful distinction is between a price and a flow. Thursday priced a claim; a confirmed halt at Yanbu or on the line would be a flow problem, and a larger one, because the pipeline is the main route around Hormuz [2]. "Investors are growing weary from hearing the same song on peace prospects," Timothy Snyder of Matador Economics told Reuters [1].

For you: a $3 move in one day is not yet a heating-oil or pump-price story; a sustained outage would be [2]. The figures to watch are loadings at Yanbu, not the volume of the statements. Nothing here is investment advice.
