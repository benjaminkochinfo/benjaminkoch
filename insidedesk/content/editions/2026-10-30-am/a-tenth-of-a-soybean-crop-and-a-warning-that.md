A larger field is not a larger harvest. That is the plain point of a study by the credit bureau Serasa Experian, reported by Valor International on Thursday: unstable El Niño rainfall, especially in Brazil's Central-West, could cut the country's 2026/27 soybean output by 9.7%, to 163.6 million tonnes [1]. "A larger planted area does not necessarily mean a better crop," said Daniel de Oliveira of Serasa, even though planting is up 1.8%, to 49.9 million hectares [1].

**Where it would land.** Mato Grosso and Goiás would account for 80% of the potential losses, while Rio Grande do Sul in the south is the state most likely to see its harvest grow [1]. About 40% of the soybean area has been planted [1]. Serasa puts the lost gross production value at about R$33 billion, and stresses that this is an indication, not a forecast of actual losses, because prices would move too [1]. Valor reports that NOAA now sees a 100% chance of El Niño reaching "very strong" by the year-end [1].

**The longer view.** FEWS NET, the Famine Early Warning Systems Network, has published the first of three El Niño special reports, and its tables are sobering and steadying at once [2]. In the seven strong and very strong El Niño events since 1980, South Africa's maize crop averaged 27.7% below its normal trend and Australia's wheat 15.3% below, while world output of wheat, maize and rice together came in 1.3% above normal [2].

**The timing.** The report's most practical finding is about when. The worst hunger after an El Niño tends to arrive in the following lean season, not during the rains [2]. For Southern Africa, FEWS NET expects the most severe needs in November 2027 to March 2028, well after the rainfall shock, and notes that the region enters this season after a cereal harvest nearly 10% above its five-year average [2]. USDA projects global 2026/27 production of the three staple grains 2.7% below last year, but still the second-largest crop on record [2].

**The calm point.** This is what early warning is for. The rain is forecast months ahead, the harvest is counted, the stocks are known, and the hardest months are marked on a calendar more than a year away. That is time for governments, aid agencies and grain traders to do dull, decisive things: buy, store, budget. The danger is not the forecast; it is reading it and waiting.
