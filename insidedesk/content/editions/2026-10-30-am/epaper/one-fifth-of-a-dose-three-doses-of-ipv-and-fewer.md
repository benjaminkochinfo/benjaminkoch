WHO's Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization, SAGE, met from 5 to 8 October, and on Thursday Kate O'Brien, WHO's immunisation director, set out what it concluded [1]. Read closely, it is a budget document as much as a medical one: "financial pressures are making it harder for country immunization programmes to close these gaps," she writes [1].

**Yellow fever.** Fractional doses, one fifth of a standard dose, may continue to be used in outbreaks when supply is short, and are now also recommended in certain preventive campaigns where supply, money or programme limits would otherwise delay vaccination [1]. For routine and catch-up vaccination of young children, the standard dose remains the recommendation [1].

**Diphtheria.** SAGE noted rising outbreaks and said response resources should focus on finding cases, antitoxin treatment, and antibiotics and vaccination for cases and close contacts [1]. Mass vaccination campaigns have "a limited role" in stopping transmission; the lasting fix is strong routine and booster vaccination [1].

**Polio.** With wild poliovirus still circulating in Afghanistan and Pakistan, SAGE said countries giving fewer than three doses of inactivated polio vaccine in routine immunisation should move to three without delay [1].

**Mpox.** SAGE found critical gaps in the evidence that prevent a recommendation for preventive vaccination of children, and asked for more evidence on pregnant women and people with severe immunocompromise [1].

**The calm point.** Not every answer is "more". Sometimes it is a smaller dose, given sooner; sometimes it is a routine clinic that never closes, rather than a campaign that comes and goes. That is the dull machinery of public health, and it is the part that works.
