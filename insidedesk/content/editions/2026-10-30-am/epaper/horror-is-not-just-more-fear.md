Tomorrow is Halloween, when fear is a costume. A study published in Cognition and Emotion argues that real-world horror is a different emotion altogether [1].

Pamela Marie Taylor of Akita International University ran two experiments, each with 60 native English speakers in the United States recruited online [1]. Participants read four short news headlines about a person being harmed. The headlines varied in two ways: moderate injury against extreme, permanent harm, and abstract wording ("a person was murdered") against concrete, sensory detail [1].

In the second study, extreme harm raised horror ratings by 1.01 points on a five-point scale and fear by only 0.39; concrete wording raised horror by 0.62 points and fear by 0.14 [1]. Both features made the event seem more abnormal, and that sense of abnormality explained much of the shift towards horror [1]. Fear, Taylor argues, comes from uncertainty about danger that might happen; horror from too much concrete information about harm that already has, and that does not fit our picture of a safe world [1].

"A person's emotional reaction to a news event is influenced by the language with which it is told," Taylor said [1]. She is careful not to call vivid headlines manipulative, and notes the reverse: abstract language "can normalize" harm [1].

The limits are real. The samples were small and 70% female, the concrete headlines were also longer, the abnormality analyses were exploratory, and everything was self-reported at one moment [1].

The practical use is modest and good. When a headline leaves you shaken, ask what happened, and then ask how it was told. Both are facts; only one is the event.
