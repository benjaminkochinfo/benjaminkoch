Friday morning in Asia looked like a rout if you read only the share prices. South Korea's KOSPI slumped 5%, which takes its October fall past 17%, Reuters reports, and Japan's Nikkei fell 3% [1]. China's blue-chip CSI300 was heading for its largest monthly fall in a decade [1]. Nasdaq 100 futures were down 1.4% [1]. Read the other half of the screen, though, and the story changes. Bonds were surging towards their biggest monthly gain in nearly three years [1].

That is the useful finding of the week. The money that left the AI trade did not disappear into panic. It went somewhere orderly.

**What the shares were punishing.** Not weak sales. Alphabet fell 12.6% on Wall Street and slipped further after hours, wiping out about $500 billion in market value, after heavy AI spending turned its cash flow negative [1]. After the US close, Intel dropped 23% in extended trading and Amazon about 7%, both for the same reason: cash going out faster than it comes in [1]. Intel's own quarter was not a disaster. Revenue rose 21% to $16.51 billion, in line with estimates, and adjusted profit of 52 cents a share beat the 40 cents expected [3]. But it forecast fourth-quarter revenue of $15–16 billion, below the $17.07 billion analysts expected, and said it does not expect positive cash flow this year [3]. "It's increasingly questioning whether shareholders will earn an adequate return on the enormous capital being deployed," Pepperstone's Chris Weston said of the market [1].

That is a sober question, not a hysterical one. In Tokyo the selling was sharp but narrow: chip-equipment makers Advantest and Tokyo Electron fell 9.1% and 5.6%, Ibiden 15.2% and Lasertec 13.8% [2]. The broader Topix lost a milder 1.3%, and across the Nikkei's 225 members 123 fell while 100 rose [2]. Konami jumped 15.3% on its results [2]. Over the week, the Nikkei was still up 0.2% at that point [2]. Desk arithmetic: almost half the index rose on a day the headline fell 3%. That is a market sorting balance sheets, not throwing everything out.

**Where the money went.** The cleanest receipt is the US Treasury's own daily table. At Thursday's close the 10-year par yield stood at 4.98% and the two-year at 4.61% [4]. On 30 September they were 5.29% and 4.88% [4]. In Tokyo trade on Friday, Reuters reports, the 10-year fell more than 7 basis points further to 4.91%, a fall of some 37 basis points through October and the largest one-month drop since December 2023 [1]. The two-year fell more than 8 basis points to 4.57%, and Fed funds futures rallied as traders dialled back some expectations of US rate rises [1].

Desk arithmetic, on the Treasury's official closes: from 30 September to 29 October the 10-year fell 31 basis points and the two-year 27 [4]. The 10-year's peak this autumn was 5.31% on 5 October [4]. It has now handed back most of what it gained in September.

Some of this is the Federal Reserve's doing, not the chipmakers'. Wednesday's hold, with no firm signal of when the next rise might come, spurred the move into bonds further, Reuters notes [1]. Haven currencies firmed too: the yen was up about 0.2% at 157.47 per dollar, and the Swiss franc was near a one-month high at 0.8213 [1]. Oil helped in its own way. Falling crude on optimism over Middle East peace talks supported Japanese shares, Reuters reports [2].

**Why this is the calm version.** Falling yields mean cheaper borrowing for governments, firms and, with a lag, mortgage holders. A bond rally that comes from investors demanding proof of returns, rather than from a broken bank or a frozen market, is the financial system doing its unglamorous job: re-pricing risk and sending capital where the receipts look sturdier. Nobody had to be rescued for that to happen. Earnings season is supposed to be when slides meet ledgers. This week the ledgers won, and markets noticed.

**What comes next is scheduled, not guessed.** The Bank of Japan held at 1.25% by 8–1 this morning, said underlying inflation is now broadly consistent with its 2% target, and Governor Ueda speaks at 07:30 CET [5]. Principal Asset Management's Howe Chung Wan told Reuters the key is how the BoJ describes "the recent global bond market volatility" [1]. Europe then gets its own receipts: euro-area growth at 11:00 CET and German inflation at 14:00 CET [6]. For you, the point is simple. The loudest number of the week was a share-price fall. The most useful one may be a yield that went down. Nothing here is investment advice.
