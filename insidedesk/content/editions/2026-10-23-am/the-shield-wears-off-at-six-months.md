A baby is born with its mother's antibodies, and for decades that borrowed protection was one reason the first measles shot waits until 12 to 15 months in the United States [1]. A study presented this week at IDWeek in Washington suggests the loan runs out much sooner [1].

**The numbers.** C. Buddy Creech of Vanderbilt and colleagues used leftover samples from 92 mother-infant pairs [1]. At birth, 96.7% of infants had detectable neutralising antibodies against the vaccine strain; at two months, 89.1%; at six months, 8.7% [1]. Against two common wild strains, B3 and D8, the six-month figures were 19.6% and 17.4% [1]. Antibody concentrations against the vaccine strain fell from 1,173 to 65 mIU/mL over the six months [1].

**Why now.** The schedule was set when many mothers had immunity from infection, which passes more antibody to the baby [1]. Most mothers today are vaccinated and pass on less [1]. That matters more in a year like this one: the CDC counted 4,410 confirmed US cases by 15 October, and Pennsylvania alone 1,310 cases and five deaths in August and September [1]. "If measles elimination is not going to be our norm anymore, then we're going to have to think about what the earliest reasonable time to vaccinate is," Creech said [1].

**The lab bench.** Health officials in California, Oregon, Washington and Hawaii have asked CDC director Erica Schwartz to validate a rapid, high-volume measles test; a request from May was denied after two months, citing "other competing priorities" [2]. Labs now rely on individual PCR runs, while platforms bought in the Covid years can process up to 500 tests in eight hours [2]. An HHS spokesperson said the agency was "prioritizing emerging technologies" [2]. A vaccine given within 72 hours of exposure can prevent infection, which is why speed of diagnosis is not a technicality [2].

**The constructive reading.** Both stories point to fixable machinery. A schedule can be revised on evidence; infants from six months can already be vaccinated when travelling or in outbreak areas [1]. A validated protocol, once published, can be run by any lab [2]. Nothing here is medical advice; the schedule is a question for your paediatrician.
