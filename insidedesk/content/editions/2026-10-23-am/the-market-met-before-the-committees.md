Next week three committees sit down. The Federal Reserve meets, the European Central Bank is expected to hold its deposit rate at 2.5%, and the Bank of Japan decides on Friday [4]. On Thursday, before any of them had opened a briefing book, the bond market held its own meeting, and it voted for less.

The trigger was an invoice, not a speech. Meta reported negative free cash flow of $2.12 billion for the third quarter and raised the bottom of its full-year capital spending range to $135 billion, from $130 billion; the price of insuring its five-year debt reached an all-time high, CNBC reported [3]. Reuters called it the company's first cash outflow in more than a decade [1]. The Nasdaq Composite fell 2.6%, its worst day since June, and the S&P 500 1.2%, its worst since July [2].

**The bond vote.** Money went where it usually goes when shares fall: into US Treasuries. The 10-year yield fell more than 19 basis points overnight, the biggest drop since March 2023, before steadying at 4.9591% on Friday, back below 5% [1]. The 2-year yield fell nearly 19 basis points to 4.6566% [1]. The 2-year is the committee's yield, the one that moves with expectations for the Fed, and it moved as much as the long one.

That is where the committee arithmetic changed. Markets "sharply pared back" expectations of further Fed tightening, stripping out almost a full quarter-point hike for next year, Reuters reported; a December rise is no longer fully priced, with the implied probability down to 85% [1]. ING's analysts put it plainly: "The risk-off environment takes pressure off the Fed to hike next week" [4].

**The currency vote.** The yen strengthened to 156.28 per dollar, its strongest in more than a month, and was set for a 1% gain on the week [4]. The dollar index was at 101.52, near its lowest since the start of the month, and the Swiss franc held near its firmest in a month at 0.8199 per dollar [4]. OCBC's analysts, quoted by Reuters, sorted the havens by fear: AI jitters favour the yen, oil fears favour the Canadian dollar and the Norwegian crown, and French fiscal stress favours the franc [4].

**The exception.** Not every ten-year bond was a refuge. France's benchmark yield rose another 6 basis points overnight to 5.0601%, its highest since 2002, while Japan's fell 9 basis points to 2.790% and Australia's 10 basis points to 5.164% [1].

Desk arithmetic: on Friday morning's figures, France paid about 10 basis points more than the United States to borrow for ten years. The euro, at $1.122, was held back by the same worry [4]. A safe haven is not a nationality. It is a budget that markets believe will pass.

**Tokyo's homework.** Japan handed its committee a fresh number on Friday. Core consumer prices, excluding fresh food, rose 2.1% in September from a year earlier, up from 1.7% in August; the measure that also strips out energy, which the BOJ watches closely, rose 2.4% [5]. Most economists polled by Reuters expect the Bank of Japan to hold next Friday and raise rates by a quarter point in December [4]. That is a poll, not a decision, and the desk records it as one.

**What this is not.** It is not a verdict on the economy. Brent settled at $113.17 on Thursday, its highest since May [2], and expensive oil does not make committees relax. ING expects the 10-year yield to "snap back above (5%)" eventually, citing inflation, the deficit and the AI boom [1]. A single session can move a probability; it cannot write a minute.

Here is the constructive part, and it is a dull one. "US Treasury bonds are — gasp — working as your hedge again," wrote Nomura's Charlie McElligott [1]. The word that matters is "again". On Thursday shares and bonds parted company, and the mechanism that is supposed to cushion a household's pension when shares slide did roughly what it says on the tin. Lower long yields also lifted the shares of homebuilders and regional banks, CNBC reported [2], which is how a bond rally eventually reaches a mortgage quote.

Next week the committees will read the same screens you have read here, plus the data you have not: wages, prices, order books. They do not need to agree with Thursday's vote. They only need to explain theirs. Nothing here is advice.
