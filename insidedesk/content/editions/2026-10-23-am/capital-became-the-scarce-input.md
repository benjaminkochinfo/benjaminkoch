Thursday's sell-off had a clear invoice behind it. Meta reported negative free cash flow of $2.12 billion for the third quarter and raised the floor of its full-year capital spending range to $135 billion; its shares fell almost 7% and the spread on its five-year credit default swaps reached an all-time high [2]. Revenue still grew 26%, to $64.60 billion, ahead of forecasts [2]. Investors were not doubting that Meta sells advertising. They were doubting how long it can spend faster than it earns.

The doubt spread down the supply chain. The US technology sector fell 3%; Nvidia lost 4.4%, Alphabet 3.2% and Advanced Micro Devices 8.1% [1]. Tesla fell 3.1% after cutting its 2026 capital spending plans [3]. The Nasdaq Composite fell 2.6%, its worst day since June, and the S&P 500 1.2%, its worst since July [1]. The Dow Jones Industrial Average, heavier in banks and industrials, closed marginally higher [1].

The most useful sentence of the day came from a seller of the shovels. "There's a scarcity of capital. The demand [for] the capital is much greater than the availability of the capital," Nvidia's Jensen Huang told CNBC, speaking about the smaller cloud companies of the AI build-out [1]. Ed Yardeni called the AI trade a "bubble" but added: "Taking some air out of the AI bubble is a good thing. I don't think it's going to burst and create problems for the economy" [1]. That is a view, not a forecast the desk endorses; it is a fair description of what a correction is for.

Asia took the baton on Friday. Japan's Nikkei fell 1.3% and was down 5% on the week; South Korea's KOSPI fell 1.7%, on course for a 4.3% weekly loss; an index of mainland Chinese blue chips hit its lowest since July 2025 [3]. Taiwan's market was closed for a typhoon [3]. US stock futures, by contrast, were roughly flat on Thursday evening [1].

The calm reading is in the weekly numbers. The S&P 500 was still heading for a slim gain on the week and the Dow for a 1.7% rise [1]. A market that sells the biggest spenders and keeps the rest is not panicking. It is doing the dull job it exists for: asking who pays for the build-out, and at what price. Nothing here is advice.
