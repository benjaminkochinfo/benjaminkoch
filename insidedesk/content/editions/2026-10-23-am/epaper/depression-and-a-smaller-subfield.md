The hippocampus is not one thing. A study in Translational Psychiatry, led by Danielle Luu of the University of Southern California, measured three of its subfields in 2,009 cognitively unimpaired adults aged 50 to 90, recruited among Hispanic, Black and white residents through the University of North Texas [1]. Of these, 630 were classed as having depression [1].

Depression went with a smaller composite region (CA2, CA3 and the dentate gyrus), but not with the CA1 or the subiculum [1]. The association was small and held after accounting for body mass index, physical activity, tau and the APOE4 risk gene [1]. With amyloid added it lost significance but kept its size, which the authors put down to fewer amyloid scans [1]. Symptom severity and age at onset showed no link [1].

Antidepressant users had smaller volumes than non-users with depression, but also fewer current symptoms [1]. The authors suspect indication bias: people with more severe depression are more likely to be prescribed [1]. Self-reported diagnoses, one snapshot in time, no data on dose or duration: the design cannot show cause, and the authors ask for randomised trials [1].

A finding that rules amyloid and tau out is useful in its own way. It narrows where to look. Nothing here is medical advice.
