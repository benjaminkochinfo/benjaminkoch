**The podium.** On Thursday at the White House, Donald Trump was asked about public reaction to the Iran war. "Well, in one way they love it because I'm doing what should have been done by other presidents," he said. "And in another way maybe they don't." He called the conflict "a price to pay" to stop Iran getting a nuclear weapon: "we can't let Iran have a nuclear weapon" [1].

**The print.** The latest Reuters/Ipsos survey interviewed 1,679 US adults from 16 to 19 October, with a margin of error of plus or minus 2.4 points [2]. Asked whether they approve of US military strikes against Iran, 34% said yes and 61% said no [2]. Weighing costs and benefits, 23% said the action has been worth it and 54% said it has not [2]. A quarter, 25%, approve of the president's handling of Iran. Just 16% think the administration has a clear strategy, against 58% who think it does not [2]. And 78% expect US involvement to go on "for an extended period", against 16% who expect it to end within weeks [2].

[[chart:us-iran-war-poll-23oct]]

**The gap.** The quote contains its own correction. "In one way... in another way maybe they don't" is a two-sided sentence, and the panel shows which side is bigger. The love is real, but it is partisan. Among Republicans, 74% approve of the strikes; among independents, 24%; among Democrats, 11% [2]. Desk arithmetic: disapproval outweighs approval by 27 points overall, so "they" in the first half of the sentence describes roughly one American in three. The president is not inventing a mood. He is quoting his own room.

**Verdict.** The podium was half right and said so itself. The print gives the proportions: a third approve, three in five do not, and very few see a plan. None of that settles whether the war is right; polls measure consent, not wisdom. It does settle the word "love". A democracy gets to check a president's sense of the room with a random sample, published with its margin of error and every question in full [2]. That dull PDF is one of the better things about the system, and it will be read closely in the weeks before the November midterms [3].
