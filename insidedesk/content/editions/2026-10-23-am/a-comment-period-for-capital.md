On Thursday the US Treasury published a notice of proposed rulemaking for its Outbound Investment Security Program [1]. It turns the COINS Act, passed with the 2026 defence authorisation, into regulations [1]. The idea is narrow. It is not about trade in goods. It is about American capital, and the know-how that travels with it, flowing into technologies that could help a rival's military, surveillance or cyber capabilities [1].

**What it does.** US persons would have to notify Treasury of some investments and would be barred from others [1]. Five countries are covered: China, Cuba, Iran, North Korea and Russia [2]. Congress named five sectors: semiconductors and microelectronics, AI systems, quantum, high-performance computing and hypersonics. Treasury has added a sixth, biotechnology [1]. In AI, covered investments in those countries would be banned outright [2]. Treasury's reason is candid: after two years of rapid progress, it doubts anyone can reliably "distinguish the most advanced models" [2].

**What it does not do.** It does not ban all investment in those countries [2]. Investments in publicly traded companies are excepted, as are deals worth less than $50,000. Investors can also ask for exemptions in the US national interest [2]. Licensing deals by US drugmakers with China are not included, a Treasury official said, which meets a key request from the industry [2]. One name has left the list: Venezuela. Treasury says Maduro's government "no longer exists" [2].

**Why it matters to you.** If your pension fund, or a fund you hold, invests in private Chinese tech, the change shows up as compliance work long before anything is sold. Investors must make a "reasonable and diligent inquiry" before a deal, and the rule sets penalties for violations [1]. Assistant Secretary Chris Pilkerton put it this way: "It's not just about the money. It's about the network, the supply chain" [2]. Nothing here is advice.

**The dull part, which is the good part.** Comments are open until 7 December [2]. Only the final rule will set an effective date [1]. That is slower than a tweet and much harder to reverse. In a year of waivers announced from podiums, a rule with a docket, a deadline and a definitions section counts as stability.
