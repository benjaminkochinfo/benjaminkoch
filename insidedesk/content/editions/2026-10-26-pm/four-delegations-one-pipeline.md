On Monday the defence minister of Saudi Arabia, Prince Khalid bin Salman, Pakistan's army chief, Field Marshal Asim Munir, and Turkey's intelligence chief, Ibrahim Kalin, met Iran's top security officials and negotiators in Tehran [1]. Iranian state television showed the Pakistani delegation sitting with President Masoud Pezeshkian and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi [2]. Saudi Arabia's deputy minister for public diplomacy, Rayed Krimly, called it part of the Makkah Defence Alliance's efforts "for de-escalation", in support of the mediation by Qatar and Pakistan [2].

It is the first high-level contact between Iran and all three members of that alliance since Saudi Arabia was drawn deeper into the conflict [2]. That is the news. The rest is a list of things that have not happened yet.

**What the room was for.** Iran's foreign ministry spokesman, Esmaeil Baqaei, said the "top priority" was the consequences of what Tehran calls the US-Israeli aggression [2]. AP reports that many Iranians are anxious about a return to all-out war, as Israeli and American leaders sharpen their threats before elections in both countries [1]. Four delegations in one room do not make a ceasefire. They do make a channel, and channels are dull, slow and the only thing that has ever ended a war.

**What the desert did.** While the delegations met, the Houthis said they had struck pumping stations on Saudi Arabia's East-West crude pipeline and Riyadh airport; Saudi Arabia did not immediately confirm damage [4]. The pipeline matters because it is the route around the straits: it carries Saudi crude to the Red Sea coast. It was shut for a time in September after drone attacks on pumping stations, then resumed [4]. Saudi Arabia said on Sunday it had intercepted more missiles and drones [3].

**What the barrel said.** Brent futures fell $1.90, or 1.63%, to $114.82 by 09:59 GMT, after a week in which Brent rose 6% [3]. CNBC had it at $115.86 by mid-morning New York time [5]. That is a modest discount for a meeting, on top of what one investment chief told CNBC is a risk premium of "about $20 a barrel" [5]. A PVM analyst put the trader's view plainly: dips back to last week's lows "will find grateful buyers" [3].

**What the official series says.** The EIA's daily Brent spot price, the public-domain record, peaked at $135.51 on 2 October and stood at $115.22 on 20 October, its latest published value [6]. It lags the futures by several days, so read the chart as the shape of the autumn, not as today's price. The shape is the point: the premium arrived in late September and has eased, not disappeared.

**The ledger view.** Saudi Arabia also said on Monday it will start storing crude in Singapore from the second half of 2027 [5]. That is not a headline about war. It is a headline about inventory, which is what serious people do when they expect the next disruption to be ordinary. Storage contracts, pipeline repairs and insurance policies outlast speeches. So, usually, do the meetings that produce nothing on the first day.
