Philip Lane, the ECB's chief economist, presented "Climate change and monetary policy" to the Irish Department of Finance's Climate Economy Group in Dublin on Monday; the ECB published the slides [1].

Three exhibits stand out in the summary [2]. First, a six-month-ahead forecast of unprocessed food inflation that includes weather alongside commodity and producer prices, and estimates of the 2025 summer heatwave's effect on food prices after twelve months. Second, data through 19 November 2026 showing that electricity prices react less to higher gas prices in countries with more renewable and nuclear generation; other modelling finds that lower reliance on fossil fuels reduces inflation volatility. Third, bank lending survey evidence on how climate risks affect credit standards and loan demand [2].

The careful part is the caveats. The heatwave scenarios for 2035 and 2060 are sensitivity exercises, not forecasts; the policy simulations hold the policy rate constant and so do not prescribe a monetary response [2]. That is how a central bank should talk about climate: as a source of price shocks to be measured, not a cause to be campaigned for.
