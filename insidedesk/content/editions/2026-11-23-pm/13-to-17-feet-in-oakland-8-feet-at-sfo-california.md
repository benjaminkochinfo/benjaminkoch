The first king tides of the Californian winter, the year's highest astronomical tides, run from 22 to 24 November, just as Americans travel for Thanksgiving, the Guardian reports [1]. This year they come on top of water that is already high: a Kelvin wave and a marine heatwave have raised coastal levels by 6 to 12 inches or more, according to NOAA as quoted, and in late October Los Angeles, San Diego, Santa Barbara and La Jolla broke their all-time observed water levels [1].

The National Weather Service forecasts "major to extreme coastal flooding" in San Francisco this week, with up to 2.8 feet of water above ground level [1]. Mayor Daniel Lurie asked residents to avoid unnecessary travel from Monday afternoon to Wednesday [1]. Daniel Swain, a climate scientist, said the tides are expected to be half a foot to a foot higher than October's, and that a moderate storm could add 6 to 12 inches of surge [1].

The most useful paragraph is about concrete. Oakland airport recently raised the sea wall around its perimeter to 13 to 17 feet and expects to withstand the flooding; the highest-risk parts of San Francisco airport's barrier are only 8 feet high, the San Francisco Chronicle reported, though the airport says its tarmac is protected by sheet-pile walls, seawalls and berms, with sandbags and pumps ready [1]. San Diego is handing out sandbags, ten per household; Huntington Beach has warned residents in flood-prone areas of a $1,000 fine if they do not clear drains or set barriers; San Rafael bought water-weighted walls for its bayfront levee [1].

Chance decides the rest: if rain or storm waves miss the peak tides, the risk falls [1]. Mark Merrifield of UC San Diego called the advance warnings from the weather services a positive sign [1]. That is the constructive lesson from a frightening week: the warnings went out weeks early, and the places that raised their walls before the record are the ones that can sleep.
