Two numbers in Monday's news look like mirror images. Nvidia's shares "had shed nearly 33%" from the day Jensen Huang admitted an affair on 8 November, CNBC wrote; on Monday they "surged 33%" on his return [1].

Your mind adds them up and gets zero. The arithmetic does not. Desk arithmetic: a share at 100 that loses 33% stands at 67. A 33% gain on 67 takes it to about 89. To get back to 100 from 67 it needs to rise by about 49%. Falls and rises are measured from different starting points, so a fall always needs a bigger rise to undo it.

Investopedia's live page shows how quickly the language runs ahead of the sums. The stock "regained almost all of that ground in less than an hour", it wrote, and in the same entry: at about $168 it "remains well below" its record close of $239 on 6 October [2]. Both statements are true. They measure from different days, and the feeling of the morning attaches to the first one.

The same asymmetry runs through the indexes. The Nasdaq fell 10% last week; a 7% gain on Monday does not undo it, because 10% off 100 leaves 90 and 7% on 90 is 96.3 [2].

None of this says what a share will do next. It is a small habit of mind: when a headline puts two percentages next to each other, ask "of what?" before you feel anything. Rebound days are when that question is least popular and most useful.
