This morning Markus Söder had a podium: cold progression, he said on Sunday, must be adjusted "urgently". By lunchtime he had a reply, and it came from his own side of the table. "Cold progression was simply not the instrument of choice," Hendrik Wüst, the CDU premier of North Rhine-Westphalia, told ZDF; the coalition committee, which Söder attended, had chosen the electricity-tax cut instead, "even if we would all wish for more" [1]. Gordon Schnieder, the CDU premier of Rhineland-Palatinate, called the demand "simply not serious if you don't say honestly how it is to be financed" [1]. The finance ministry noted "very, very different" responses from the Länder and called it, in effect, a discussion among the Länder [1]. (Quotes translated.)

That is how budget week begins. From Tuesday the Bundestag debates the 2027 federal budget in second reading; on Friday at 12:40 the third reading starts, and after 90 minutes of debate comes the vote [2].

**The size of the thing.** Core spending is €560.92 billion, 6.9% more than the €524.54 billion planned for 2026 [2]. Net borrowing in the core budget rises from €97.96 billion to €122.97 billion. Add the two special funds, €57.84 billion for infrastructure and climate neutrality and €29.65 billion for the Bundeswehr, and new borrowing comes to €210.46 billion, according to the Bundestag's own press service as cited by AktienCheck [2]. Of the core borrowing, €84.55 billion falls under the exemption for defence and security; the €38.43 billion that counts against the debt brake sits, in the press service's words, "exactly at the ceiling" [2].

**The size of the quarrel.** A full cold-progression adjustment would cost the federal government and the Länder about €1.8 billion each next year and the municipalities about €400 million, Reuters reports; Berlin refuses to carry all three shares, and only Bavaria and Hessen have offered to pay theirs [1]. Desk arithmetic: the federal €1.8 billion is about 0.9% of the €210.46 billion the federal government plans to borrow. It is not the amount that is hard. It is that the debt-brake part of the budget is already full, so every new euro of relief needs a euro found somewhere else, and the electricity-tax cut was where the coalition chose to spend its euro [1][2].

That cut is not free either. It takes the tax on household and business power from 2.05 to 0.1 cents a kilowatt hour for 2027 only, at a cost of €5.4 billion, paid from unneeded money on the renewables (EEG) account; if that account runs short, the coalition has promised a package of measures, from cutting subsidies to raising other taxes [3]. For 2028 the plan already shows a gap of about €25 billion, which a permanent cut would widen to about €30 billion [3].

**What you can actually watch.** The interesting part of this week is not Wednesday's general debate, however lively ZDF expects it to be [4]. It is the paperwork: whether the electricity-tax cut, agreed after the budget committee had finished, is written into the budget before Friday's vote, as it has to be [3]; and whether any Land other than Bavaria and Hessen puts a number next to its name for cold progression [1]. A demand with a financing line is a policy. Without one, as Schnieder put it, it is a speech [1].

The calm reading is not that Germany cannot afford €1.8 billion. It is that a budget at its ceiling forces politicians to rank their promises, in public, with numbers attached. That is uncomfortable for a party leader on a Sunday. It is exactly what a parliament is for on a Friday.
