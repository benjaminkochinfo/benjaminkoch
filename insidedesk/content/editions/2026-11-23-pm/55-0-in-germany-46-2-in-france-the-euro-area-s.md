Two surveys, collected over the same week from 12 to 19 November, describe two different countries [1][2].

**Germany.** S&P Global's flash composite index rose to 55.0 from 53.4, the highest since March 2022 and the fifth month of growth in a row [1]. Factory output ran at 57.4, its strongest in more than five years; services rose to 54.0; new orders grew at the fastest pace in more than four and a half years, helped by export orders [1]. The dullest line is the most interesting one: manufacturing employment rose for the first time in almost three and a half years, ending what S&P Global calls the longest run of factory job cuts in the survey's history [1]. Firms hire when the order book stops being a rumour.

**France.** The composite fell to 46.2 from 46.6, the sharpest drop in activity since May; the manufacturing PMI fell to 47.9, a twelve-month low [2]. Joe Hayes of S&P Global said the data "raised the odds of France entering a technical recession in the fourth quarter", and the outlook index was the gloomiest since late 2012 if the first pandemic months are left out [2]. Firms cited weak domestic demand and clients hesitating ahead of next year's presidential election [2]. Selling prices rose at an eight-month low pace, because customers were asking for discounts [2].

**The average.** For the euro area as a whole the composite was unchanged on the month, at 51.7, and growing for a fifth month [3][4]. Bond markets noticed the gap anyway: the French ten-year yield fell 8 basis points to 4.8% on Monday afternoon, but its premium over the German Bund was still 150 basis points, a spread that has widened sharply this year, Reuters reported [3].

The constructive reading is not that one country is winning. It is that a currency union is an insurance contract: a German order book and a French one, priced in the same money, smooth each other out. That works as long as the weaker partner's problem is demand and politics, which can change, and not its invoices, which have to be paid either way.
