Sunday was a speech and Monday was a curfew map. Tuesday is the day the institutions book their questions — in writing, with dates.

The Senate's law committee decided on Monday to open a cross-party fact-finding mission on the events of 17 and 18 October [1]. Its own press release does not mince words: the policing plan "a rapidement été débordé par les événements", and the mission will establish "la chaîne des événements", judge the policing set-up and the penal response, and determine administrative, institutional, legal and political responsibilities [1]. Every Senate group sits on it; it may ask for the powers of a commission of inquiry, and its rapporteurs are to be named "mercredi prochain" [1].

At the Assembly, Yaël Braun-Pivet proposes this morning, at the Conférence des présidents, a commission of inquiry into the disorder, the methods of the violent groups and the public-order strategy, including "le dimensionnement et la doctrine" of policing [2]. Socialist, LFI and Ensemble deputies backed the idea on LCP on Monday evening; the RN said it would decide its position on Tuesday morning [3]. One dull constraint matters: with the presidential election ahead, parliamentary work ends on 28 February, so any inquiry may report early next year rather than run its usual six months [3].

The government's own table is set for Wednesday. Matignon has invited the CFDT, CGT, FO, CFE-CGC and CFTC for 10:30, with the prime minister, the interior minister and the labour minister, according to the invitation letter seen by franceinfo [4]. Separately, the Senate's long-running mission on the future of public order — launched in January — was due to close its work on Monday and present its report to the press on Wednesday [5]. Coincidence of calendar, says Public Sénat. Useful coincidence, says this desk: a report written before the weekend is a baseline nobody can accuse of hindsight.

You do not have to pick a side in the argument to notice what changed overnight. Questions now have owners, rooms and dates. That is how a country that lost control of some streets for a weekend starts taking back the paperwork.
