**The podium.** The day after he signed the order, the President told reporters at his Mar-a-Lago club: "A lot of you don't know what my decision is yet" [1]. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the President would use "the 60-day period of time" to work out what sanctions and tariffs would mean for the global and US economy [1]. On Tuesday morning oil desks were still describing the situation as markets assessing an order that "waived some sanctions and tariffs" [4].

**The print.** The order is short and specific. Section 2 raises the duty on goods from Russia by 500% ad valorem, and by 100% on goods from the five largest buyers of Russian crude and gas if they make new purchases on or after 18 October [2]. Section 4 then waives those duties, together with a long list of the Act's sanctions provisions [2]. The waivers "shall remain in effect until such time as the President or the Secretary of State" decide that further action is appropriate [2]. The text gives no 60-day limit. The law firm Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer read it the same way: the waivers "do not have a specified expiration date", and existing Russia sanctions, including the 2022 ban on importing Russian oil into the US, still apply [3].

**The gap.** The podium describes a decision still to be made. The document describes one already made, which can be reversed at any time and has no deadline. Both can be true, but only one is binding. The 60 days come from a briefing; they are not in the text of the order.

**Verdict.** Read the order and treat the remarks as commentary on it. If the review becomes an official document, it will have a date on it. Until that happens, buyers of Russian oil face no 100% tariff, and the existing pre-Graham sanctions are unchanged.
