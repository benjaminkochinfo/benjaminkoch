This morning the mines on the road to Oleshky had been cleared. By Friday afternoon people were on the buses. "Evacuation and distribution of humanitarian aid have begun," Ukrainian negotiator David Arakhamia said, while Russia's human rights ombudsman posted video of residents waiting to board [1]. A convoy crossed from Kherson region into Crimea, witnesses said, and Arakhamia later wrote that evacuees had left for the Russian city of Anapa with a Ukrainian delegation, to fly on to Belarus and come back into Ukraine by bus [1].

It is a long way round for people going home, and that is the plain cost of a truce that covers one town and nothing else.

The figures deserve the same care. Ukraine's prime minister, Sergiy Koretskyi, told parliament that 527 people would be brought to Kyiv-controlled territory, then withdrew the number: "There are no exact figures at the moment. The rescue operation is ongoing, and the data is changing" [1]. Officials on both sides say some residents may choose to stay [1]. The town had 24,000 people before the war and about 2,000 now [1].

The truce itself has held. Kherson's regional governor reported no shelling or strikes on civilian or critical infrastructure over the past day, though one person was injured by a Russian mine [2]. The Kremlin, for its part, highlighted the high level of coordination between the two sides' rights commissioners [1].

For you, the thing to watch is dull and decisive. Does every bus that left Oleshky arrive at the Ukrainian border, and do the published figures finally match the passenger lists?
