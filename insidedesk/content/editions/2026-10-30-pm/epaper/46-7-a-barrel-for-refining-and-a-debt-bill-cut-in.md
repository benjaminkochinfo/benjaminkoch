BP reported an underlying replacement-cost profit, its proxy for net profit, of $8.5 billion for the third quarter, against $2.2 billion a year earlier and an LSEG consensus of $6.19 billion [1]. The company credited gas and marketing trading and "significantly higher" refining margins [1]. Its refining indicator margin was $46.7 a barrel for the quarter, which chief executive Meg O'Neill called "incredibly strong" on CNBC [1].

**Where the money came from.** Outages and damage at refineries, from the Russia–Ukraine war to the Middle East, have tightened fuel markets this year, so diesel, jet fuel and petrol have risen faster than crude [1]. Shell and TotalEnergies said on Thursday that their refining margins had also reached historically high levels [1]. For you at the pump, that margin is the gap between the price of a barrel and the price of a litre.

**Where the money went.** Operating cash flow for the first nine months was $28.55 billion, up from $16.89 billion; net debt fell to $13.87 billion from $26.05 billion [1]. Desk arithmetic: debt down by $12.18 billion, or 47%. O'Neill said BP will also set cash aside for the $5.9 billion it still owes over the 2010 Deepwater Horizon spill [1].

**The sentence worth keeping.** "Oil prices today are strong. They will not always be strong," O'Neill said, adding that margins are already softening a little as fourth-quarter demand drops off [1]. A chief executive paying down debt in a boom is the dull machinery doing its job. Nothing here is investment advice.
