At COP30 last year, governments agreed to "at least triple" finance for climate adaptation in developing countries by 2035 [1]. The last-minute deal left out the parts an accountant would ask for first: the baseline year, a headline amount, who pays and how the money is delivered [1].

Ghana, which chairs the African Group of Negotiators this year, has now written to UN climate chief Simon Stiell asking for three extra agenda items at COP31, which opens in Antalya on 9 November: tripling adaptation finance, trade and climate change, and the financing arrangements of the Green Climate Fund [1]. "A dedicated item is needed," the group's chair, Antwi-Boasiako Amoah, wrote [1]. At June's talks in Bonn, wealthy countries led by the EU, the UK and Japan opposed anchoring the target in the adaptation negotiations, and diplomats left without agreement [1].

The least-developed countries want the goal defined as $120 billion a year by 2030, which would triple an earlier target of around $40 billion that was due in 2025 and has likely been missed [1]. Their lead finance negotiator called adaptation finance "a homeless issue" [1]. The EU's agreed position reaffirms the tripling but folds it into the broader post-2025 finance goal, while some donors, including Germany and France, have cut aid budgets [1]. In May the UK told the Green Climate Fund it would deliver only half of its £1.62 billion pledge [1].

Any government can object to extra agenda items, and disputed ones usually go to consultations run by the presidency [1]. That sounds like procedure. It is: a target without a baseline is a promise, and a baseline is where it becomes an invoice.
