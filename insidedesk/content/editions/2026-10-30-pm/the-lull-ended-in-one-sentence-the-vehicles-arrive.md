This morning's question was whether Ukraine had quietly paused its drone campaign against Russian refineries. By Friday lunchtime Moscow had answered it, in a single sentence. "We had a lull that lasted a certain time, but today the attacks on oil refineries resumed," Russia's deputy prime minister for energy, Alexander Novak, told state television [1]. He said three refineries and one gas-chemical complex had been attacked overnight [1]. Kyiv had made no immediate comment [1]. A fire was reported at the Novokuibyshevsk refinery in Samara region, which CNBC says produced jet fuel for the Russian armed forces, and the regional governor said two men died after a drone attack while clearing up afterwards [2][4]. Russia's defence ministry said its air defences had shot down more than 1,000 Ukrainian drones overnight [1].

The traffic ran both ways. Ukraine's air force said Russia launched ballistic missiles, four guided aerial missiles and 113 Shahed-type drones overnight, 83 of them jet-powered, and that three of the missiles and 96 of the drones were intercepted or suppressed [3]. It did not say how many ballistic missiles came or how many were stopped [3]. Desk arithmetic: 17 of the 113 drones were not reported as stopped, so about 85% were. That leaves out the ballistic missiles entirely, and they are the weapon that matters most.

That gap is the story Kyiv keeps telling. In his evening address President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Ukraine had intelligence that Russia was preparing new ballistic missile attacks: "We need the capabilities to shoot down this Russian evil and are awaiting the decisions" [2]. CNBC describes an acute shortage of interceptors, particularly for Patriot systems [2]. The bill for the shortage arrived in Dnipropetrovsk region. About 350,000 people lost electricity overnight, and by Friday afternoon the regional governor said power had been restored to most of them but "more than 200,000 subscribers remain without power" [1][3]. The mayor of Dnipro asked residents to keep one or two days of water at home [1]. Across the country, regional authorities counted at least 27 dead and 127 injured over the past day, including the strike on the Dnipro railway platform [3].

Now set this beside what Europe's defence ministries did this week, because it was not nothing and it was not fast. On Thursday NATO's secretary general, Mark Rutte, told the alliance's national armaments directors in Brussels to "turn commitments into contracts" and to make sure that higher defence investment turns into military strength [5]. On Friday two such contracts surfaced. The Netherlands agreed with BAE Systems Hägglunds to buy more CV90 infantry fighting vehicles, budgeted at €1.3 billion, to equip a new armoured infantry battalion; the package includes spare parts, maintenance kits, ammunition and communications equipment, and it is being bought jointly through a Nordic programme led by Sweden [6]. And OCCAR, Europe's joint procurement agency, ordered 69 more Schakal wheeled infantry fighting vehicles, 35 for the Bundeswehr and 34 for the Dutch army. That takes the programme to 291 vehicles [7].

Read the small print on the second contract. The Bundestag's budget committee approved Germany's share on 24 June 2026, and deliveries are scheduled between May 2032 and November 2033 [7]. Rheinmetall's vehicle division head said exercising the options gives industry and its suppliers "greater insurance" for planning [7]. That is the honest virtue of contracts like this. They do not stop a drone over Dnipro tonight. They give factories in Germany and Sweden a reason to hire, buy steel and book shifts through to late 2033, and that is how capacity actually gets built.

So you are looking at two clocks running side by side. One counts hours: interceptors fired overnight, substations repaired by afternoon, a refinery fire seen from orbit by morning [4]. The other counts years: a committee approval in June, a joint order in October, vehicles rolling off the line in the early 2030s. Speeches tend to blur the two, so that a pledge sounds like a shipment. The useful habit is to ask each announcement which clock it belongs to.

On the first clock the news is grim, but at least it is countable: Ukrainian officials publish the casualties, the outages and the intercepts every day. On the second the news is better than the rhetoric around it. Alliances are signing purchase orders with delivery dates, spare parts and ammunition in the same package, and they are buying in groups rather than alone. Neither clock will be settled by what anyone says in Abu Dhabi next month. Both can be checked.
