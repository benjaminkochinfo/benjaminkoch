The broker Aon has done the year's sums so far, and they are calmer than the headlines of the summer suggested. Insured losses from natural disasters reached an estimated $91 billion in the first nine months of 2026 [1]. That is slightly above the 21st-century average of $88 billion, the lowest for the period since 2019, and well below the $135 billion average of the past five years [1]. The third quarter alone cost insurers $29 billion, 31% below the long-term average, and no single event cost them more than $10 billion [2].

Quiet totals hide busy ledgers. Twenty-three events produced insured losses of at least $1 billion each, against a historical average of 14 [1]. Severe convective storms, the hail, wind and tornado kind, were again the costliest peril, accounting for roughly $55 billion of insured losses [2]. The costliest insured event of the year so far was not a hurricane but Europe's drought, at more than $8.5 billion; France alone recorded about $5 billion in insured losses, a record [1]. Subsidence is a slow peril: it cracks a wall one dry summer at a time.

[[chart:aon-cat-losses-30pm]]

Now the gap. Economic losses reached about $204 billion, and roughly 55% of that was not covered by insurance [1]. Desk arithmetic: $204 billion minus $91 billion leaves about $113 billion paid by households, firms and governments directly. The extremes sit in Latin America. Aon puts June's earthquake in Venezuela at about $25 billion in economic losses, with only a small insured share because cover there is thin; Colombia's August quake cost about $9.6 billion, and there established reinsurance programmes and reserves are expected to carry much of it [1].

"Moderate global totals can mask significant regional impacts and emerging concentrations of risk," said Michal Lörinc, Aon's head of catastrophe insight [1]. The weather helped, too: the Atlantic got through September without a single hurricane, only the fourth time since 1950, which Aon links to a very strong El Niño [1].

For you, the useful line is the dull one. Check what your home policy says about subsidence, hail and natural hazards (in Germany, "Elementarschäden"), because a quiet year is only cheap for the losses that were insured.
