Researchers from SWPS University in Katowice and the University of Silesia surveyed 323 adults in Poland online: 212 women, 107 men and four of another gender, aged 18 to 79 with an average age of 26 [1]. The paper appears in Current Issues in Personality Psychology [1].

They measured two sides of humiliation: memories of having been humiliated and fear of being humiliated in future [1]. People who reported more of either tended to be less happy and more prone to rumination, and fear of future humiliation was slightly more strongly linked to lower happiness than the memories were [1].

Rumination "partially mediated" the link: more humiliation went with more repetitive negative thinking, which went with lower happiness [1]. But that route explained only part of it. Humiliation stayed strongly linked to lower happiness even after rumination was accounted for [1]. Those who saw their own repetitive thinking as unproductive were the least happy [1].

The caveats are the usual ones, and they matter. All measures were taken at one point in time, so the study cannot show that humiliation causes rumination, which then lowers happiness [1]. It relies on self-report, and the sample, recruited through social media, was mostly young and female [1].

The useful takeaway is modest. The fear of the next humiliation seems to weigh at least as much as the last one. That is worth knowing when you find yourself replaying a meeting at 3am: the replay is a habit you can name, and naming it is where most help starts.
