If your pay stopped tomorrow, how long would your savings cover the bills? For 22% of working adults in Germany, less than four weeks, according to a representative Civey survey for the insurer Debeka, reported by Südwest Presse [1]. About half could cover their running costs for three months at most; only 23% would last more than six months [1]. Almost twice as many women as men said their household would last less than a month [1].

Single bills are a problem too. "An unexpected expense of €3,000" could not be paid at short notice by 37% without getting into financial difficulty, more often in households with children and among younger adults [1]. Federal statistics point the same way: in 2025 almost a third of the population could not meet a larger unexpected expense, there set at €1,300, from their own funds [1].

Under pressure, just over half would cut spending, and only 14% would cut their savings or pension contributions [1].

The advice is unglamorous and works. Consumer advisers suggest an emergency fund of at least two to three months' income, others say six [1]. If that sounds impossible, the advice quoted from Consors Finanz is to start with a first goal of €500 [1]. Set a standing order for payday, put windfalls such as a tax refund aside, and keep the money in an instant-access savings account, not shares and not the coffee tin [1]. The overdraft is the expensive alternative: 10% to 15% a year, depending on the bank [1].

Desk arithmetic: €500 is about €42 a month for a year. Small enough to miss; large enough to matter when the boiler breaks.
