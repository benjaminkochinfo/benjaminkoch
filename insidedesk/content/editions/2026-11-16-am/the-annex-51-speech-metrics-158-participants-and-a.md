The Annex reads the paper behind the headline. This week's headline, written up on Saturday, says simple word counts beat complex AI tools at spotting psychosis in speech [1]. The paper, published in npj Digital Medicine in August, says something narrower and more useful [2].

**Clause one: the test.** The team benchmarked 51 natural language processing metrics, the computer techniques used to analyse speech, on two datasets [2]. The German sample had 59 people with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder and 20 controls; the Russian sample had 31 people with those diagnoses, 30 controls and 18 people with mainly depressive affective disorders [2]. Desk arithmetic: 158 participants in all. Each completed speech tasks such as describing picture stories and recounting personal memories, and each metric was judged on two things: how well it tracked symptom severity, and how well it separated psychosis from healthy controls [1][2].

**Clause two: the result.** Verbosity, meaning word count, sentence count and mean sentence length, outperformed most of the sophisticated metrics across tasks and languages [2]. People with more severe symptoms tended to say less, consistent with the long-known poverty of speech in psychosis [1]. Only a handful of advanced measures, including speech-graph metrics, edged past the best simple baseline, and only in the German data; in the Russian data nothing beat it [1][2].

**Clause three: the size of it.** The strongest German baseline, mean sentence length, correlated with symptom severity at r = −0.28 [2]. Desk arithmetic: squared, that is about 8% of the variation in symptoms. Real, and modest. No metric here reads a diagnosis from a recording.

**Clause four: the small print.** The best metrics were not specific to psychosis: what separated psychosis from healthy controls did much the same for depression, which suggests they may pick up general distress [1]. The samples were small, the tasks differed between languages, and the authors call the work exploratory and warn of the risks of wrongly predicting psychiatric symptoms [1].

**For you.** If an app, a clinic or a vendor tells you its AI can hear mental illness in your voice, the fair question comes from this paper: did it beat a word count? The authors recommend that every new tool be tested against simple verbosity baselines before claiming added value [1][2]. That is not hostility to technology. It is the oldest rule in measurement.
