The United States has lost its status as a country where measles is eliminated, KFF Health News reports, citing researchers with direct knowledge of the deliberations: the Pan American Health Organization concluded last week that measles has re-established regular transmission there [1]. The status was secured in 2000 [1]. PAHO had planned to announce it publicly on 17 November [1].

The rule behind the verdict is a dull one, and that is its strength. Elimination does not mean no cases; it means outbreaks end within a year [1]. An outbreak in Utah passed the one-year mark in June [1]. PAHO based its assessment on two analyses, one by the CDC and one by an independent group of measles experts [1].

The ledger is long. In the past two years more than 830 people have been admitted to hospital with measles in the US, more than 8,000 have fallen ill in outbreaks that cost roughly $125 million in care, public-health response and lost productivity, according to Johns Hopkins researchers, and states have reported 13 measles-associated deaths, among them infants too young to be vaccinated [1]. Only 3% of more than 7,600 cases confirmed since January 2025 were in vaccinated people [1].

The arguments over blame are loud: a spokesperson for the US health department, HHS, pointed to vaccination gaps that opened before this administration, while many scientists quoted by KFF criticise the federal response and messaging [1]. The arithmetic is quieter. Two doses of the MMR vaccine prevent about 97% of infections, and with at least 95% of a community immunised, outbreaks peter out [1].

The way back is also written down: current outbreaks must end, and future ones must stop within a year [1]. "Bigger fires create more embers," said Paul Rota, a former CDC microbiologist [1]. For you, the useful document is not a press release; it is the vaccination booklet in the drawer, and whether it shows two entries.
