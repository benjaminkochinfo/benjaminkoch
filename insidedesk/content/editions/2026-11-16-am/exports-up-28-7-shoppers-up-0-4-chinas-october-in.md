China's National Bureau of Statistics published its October numbers on Monday morning, and the headline is its own: the economy was "generally stable" [1]. The detail is less tidy. Retail sales of consumer goods rose 0.4% from a year earlier, to 4,646.3 billion yuan [1]. Analysts had expected 0.8%, and September had managed 1.1%, CNBC reports [2]. Industrial output grew 4.8%, against forecasts of 5.3% and down from 5.5% in September [1][2].

Now the other side of the ledger. Goods exports in October were worth 2,790.5 billion yuan, up 28.7% on a year earlier; imports rose 34.7% [1]. CNBC reports export growth of 35.2% on its measure and says the trade surplus had reached $1 trillion by October, the fastest it has ever got there [2]. Desk arithmetic: on the NBS yuan figures, October's goods surplus was 712.7 billion yuan, and exports were worth about 60% of everything Chinese shops sold at retail that month.

The weakest line is investment. Fixed-asset investment for January to October fell 8.1%, real-estate development investment by 21.6%, and private investment by 11.4% [1]. CNBC calls the slump unprecedented outside the Covid period [2]. Consumer prices rose 0.7% on the year, while factory-gate prices rose 5.7% and industrial purchasing prices 7.9% [1]. The urban jobless rate edged down to 5.1% [1].

Why it matters to you: when a large economy grows by selling abroad rather than buying at home, the adjustment lands on its trading partners. Pinpoint Asset Management's Zhiwei Zhang told CNBC that much of the export strength rests on AI-related demand, and that the risk of a pullback "has become even more serious" [2]. Citi Research sees further EU trade measures on China as "plausible" [2]. If you buy from or sell into China, the useful numbers this autumn are not the growth rate but the gap between the factory line and the shop line. Nothing here is investment advice.
