At Nato headquarters on Tuesday, where Ukraine's backers met to gather more arms and ammunition, the Pentagon's policy chief Elbridge Colby said Kyiv's strikes deep inside Russia "have not been effective in driving an end to the war, nor do they contribute to blunting Russia's advances on the front line", the Guardian reports [1]. Strikes on civilian targets such as the retailer Wildberries had, he said, "probably hardened" Russian resolve [1]. He added that this did not mean Ukraine should fight "with its hands tied behind its back", and that Washington would focus its support on the front lines and "associated midrange strikes" [1].

The answer from Berlin came quickly. Asked whether he agreed, Defence Minister Boris Pistorius said criticising strikes on "strategic military and military-used targets" in Russia was "quite a stupid thing" [1]. Better, he said, to talk about the "terroristic strikes every night and every day" on Ukraine's energy, water and heating [1]. Britain's defence minister, Wes Streeting, said the strikes "helped to degrade Putin's war machine, buying time and saving lives" [1].

The same night, Volodymyr Zelenskyy called the strike on the Kerch Bridge revenge for Russian attacks on Ukrainian bridges, railways and grain ships [1]. Oleksandr Danyliuk, president of the Dutch-headquartered start-up Destinus, told Reuters that seven of its Ruta cruise missiles hit the bridge, the only foreign-made weapons used in that operation, according to the company [1].

There is a temptation to read a public disagreement as a crack. The more sober reading is that the argument is now about which targets, not whether to help. That is an argument about procurement lists and range limits, the dull material that decides wars more often than speeches do. You should watch the delivery schedules, not the adjectives.
