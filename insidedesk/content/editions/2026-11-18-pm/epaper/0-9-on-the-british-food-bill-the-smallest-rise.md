British consumer prices rose 3.6% in the year to October, up from 3.5% and the highest in a year, official figures showed on Wednesday morning, Reuters reports [1]. Higher fuel prices and an earlier-than-usual rise in tobacco taxes did most of the pushing, the Office for National Statistics said [1].

The aisle told a different story. Food and non-alcoholic drink prices were up just 0.9% on a year earlier, the smallest increase since September 2021 [1]. Deutsche Bank's Sanjay Raja credited "fierce supermarket competition", while adding it was unclear how much longer that could hold prices off [1]. Core inflation held at 2.8% and services at 3.7%, where economists had expected services to rise [1].

The bill that does not yet show is energy. Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey said a fall in international energy prices early this month came largely too late to stop a roughly 20% rise in regulated household energy bills due in January, according to Reuters [1].

For you, the point is how differently prices travel: shelves are repriced weekly by rivals fighting for your basket, while a regulated bill moves on a timetable months behind the market. Two households with the same income can feel the same 3.6% very differently, depending on whether their money goes to the till or the meter.
