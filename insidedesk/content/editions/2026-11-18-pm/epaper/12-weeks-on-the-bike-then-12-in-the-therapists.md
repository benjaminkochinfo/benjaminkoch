A randomised trial published in Mental Health and Physical Activity tested whether exercise can prepare people with mild to moderate depression for cognitive behavioural therapy, Medical Xpress reports [1]. Participants trained for 12 weeks, then had 12 weeks of CBT [1].

The trial began with 120 adults with depressive disorder; 74 completed every phase [1]. Desk arithmetic: about 62% stayed the course. One group trained hard, at 55% to 80% of maximum heart rate; one walked, cycled lightly and stretched at 20% to 30%; a control group did no exercise in the first phase [1]. The hard trainers gained most in fitness, processing speed and visual attention, while specific memory tests showed no major difference between the two active groups [1]. The authors link the gains to the growth hormone IGF-1 [1].

The detail that matters: cognitive improvement was consolidated mainly during the therapy phase [1]. Exercise "acted as a biological preparation" for specific domains of cognition rather than amplifying every effect of therapy, said the first author, André de Oliveira Werneck [1]. He also points to a gap of more than ten years in life expectancy between people with severe mental disorders and the general population [1].

Seventy-four people do not rewrite a treatment guideline. But the design is the sensible kind: not exercise against therapy, but in what order. That is a question health services can actually schedule.
