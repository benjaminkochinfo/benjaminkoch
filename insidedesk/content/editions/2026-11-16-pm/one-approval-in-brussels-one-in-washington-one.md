The European Commission on Monday cleared UniCredit's takeover of Commerzbank under EU competition rules, without conditions, opening the way for the Italian lender to take control of Germany's second-largest bank [1]. The Federal Reserve's Board of Governors had approved earlier the same day, and UniCredit says one final regulatory approval is outstanding, expected by the end of November [2].

What made the day unusual was not the verdict but the tone. Executive Vice-President Teresa Ribera called a press conference to announce it, and said that if a national government sought to prevent the takeover, "we would react accordingly" [1]. "This is the biggest cross-border banking deal we have had since the financial crisis," she said [1]. And, more plainly: "Europe needs stronger banks that can compete, grow and support our businesses and families" [3].

The competition finding itself was dull, which is the point. The Commission saw a moderate joint market share in Germany and several credible rivals [1]. POLITICO noted that the two banks' combined market value would be only around a fifth of JPMorgan's [1]. A European champion, measured honestly, is still a mid-sized bank by American standards.

The politics are not finished. The German government still holds 13% of Commerzbank, a legacy of its 2009 rescue, and is asking for guarantees on jobs, the Frankfurt headquarters and the right to appoint two supervisory-board members, demands that chief executive Andrea Orcel has resisted [1]. EU rules strictly limit the grounds on which governments can intervene in mergers [1].

For you: if you bank with Commerzbank, Monday's decision is about who may own the bank, not about your account. What changes is who owns the balance sheet behind them, and that is decided by approvals, not speeches. Markets shrugged: UniCredit was down 0.6% in Milan at midday and Commerzbank 0.4% in Frankfurt [3]. Nothing here is investment advice.
