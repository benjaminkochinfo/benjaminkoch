Moscow arrived at this week with a list of demands, including a new security framework for Europe that Europeans read as an attempt to make NATO pull troops and equipment back westward [1]. Brussels answered on Monday with a list of its own. "In 2021, Russia's demands were a pretext for war. In 2026, they are pretext to keep the war going," the EU's foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas wrote before the meeting of foreign ministers [1]. "A ceasefire must come first," she added [1].

The answer came mostly as paperwork. Ireland's foreign minister Helen McEntee, whose country holds the EU presidency, said the bloc would agree 113 new listings against Russia on Monday, ahead of further talks this week on a broader 22nd sanctions package [2]. The measures target nearly 50 Russian banks and financial institutions, several businesspeople linked to the energy sector and the oil and petroleum trade, and dozens accused of spreading pro-Russian propaganda, who face travel bans and asset freezes [1]. France's foreign minister said Tatyana Kim, chief executive of the online retailer Wildberries, was among them [2].

Money moved too. Kyiv received $1.88bn from the European Commission on Monday to buy drones, ammunition and air defence, part of a larger loan approved in February, and the EU approved its largest-ever single payment under the Ukraine Facility, nearly €8 billion, Volodymyr Zelensky said [1]. France's Jean-Noël Barrot announced a conference in Paris on 4 December on the Russian "shadow fleet", the ageing tankers that keep crude exports moving around sanctions [2].

The words were sharp as well. "It is not for others to dictate to Europeans the terms of their own security," Barrot said, promising to defend Europe's security principles "tooth and nail and inch by inch" [2]. Sweden's Maria Malmer Stenergard called the emerging Russian demands "absurd" [2].

For you: speeches about security architecture are cheap to give and cheap to retract. Asset freezes, bank listings and a payment that has actually arrived are not. Judge the week by the 22nd package and by 4 December, not by who sounded toughest in Brussels.
