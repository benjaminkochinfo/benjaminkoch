The Annex reads the paper behind the headline. The headline, reaching German readers overnight, says people who go to bed and get up at irregular times more often report depression, anxiety and loneliness [2]. The paper, published in Scientific Reports on 12 November, says that too, and a good deal more about its own limits [1].

**Clause one: the sample.** The team analysed 22,894 adults in the TemPredict study who wore an Oura Ring, a commercial sleep tracker, and answered monthly mental-health surveys [1]. Participants contributed an average of 153.8 days of usable data [1]. Shift workers were excluded [1]. And the date matters: the wearable data run from January to October 2020, the first pandemic year [1].

**Clause two: the measure.** The Sleep Regularity Index asks how likely you are to be in the same state, asleep or awake, at the same clock time 24 hours apart; it runs from 0 to 100, and higher is more regular [1]. It says nothing about how long you sleep [1].

**Clause three: the result.** The gradient is consistent. Participants with severe depressive symptoms averaged 74.93, against 83.74 for those within normal limits; severe anxiety 78.94 against 83.67; elevated loneliness 81.79 against 83.75 [1]. Desk arithmetic: nearly nine points for severe depression, under two for loneliness. The associations held after adjusting for age, sex and average sleep duration [1].

**Clause four: the small print.** The analysis is cross-sectional, so it cannot say whether irregular sleep worsens mood, mood disrupts sleep, or both [1]. The cohort was mostly White, middle-aged adults from North America and Europe [1]. Chronotype, living alone and relationship status were not accounted for [1]. The work was partly funded by Oura Health, and two of the authors have received consulting fees from Ouraring Inc.; one also has a financial interest in it [1]. None of that makes the finding wrong. It makes it a finding to replicate.

**For you.** The authors' own clinical point is modest and old: keep a consistent wake time and protect a regular bedtime [1]. That advice was sound before the ring existed. What the paper adds is a measurable target, and the honest admission that nobody has yet shown that hitting it will lift your mood [1].
