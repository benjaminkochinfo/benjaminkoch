Friedrich Merz spent Monday morning in Munich, the first time he has attended a CSU board meeting as chancellor [1]. Markus Söder called it "not only a pleasure, also an honour", handed over a Bavarian bread board and promised "backing for Berlin" [1]. He also asked for a "Zusammenraufen in Berlin", a getting-along-somehow, and warned that AfD and Linke were "only waiting for the democratic centre to fall apart" [1].

The timing explains the warmth. On Tuesday the coalition committee of CDU, CSU and SPD meets, with both Merz and his vice-chancellor Lars Klingbeil under pressure from their own parties for not asserting themselves enough [1]. Söder, by contrast, arrives strengthened; the SZ reports his party credits him with pushing through Ilse Aigner as the Union's candidate for federal president [1].

One line did not move. Söder rejects cutting the mothers' pension, a CSU promise that is to pay out from 2028 and cost the federal budget about €5 billion a year, and which he says matters to "nine million women" [1]. The law passed the Bundestag in December 2025; revising it now, he said on ZDF, would make the coalition "ridiculous" [1]. Asked on Monday, Merz said it had not been discussed at the board: "That was not a topic" [1].

For you: a promise written into law is harder to cut than a promise in a manifesto. That is the point of law. If Tuesday's committee looks for savings, watch where it looks instead.
