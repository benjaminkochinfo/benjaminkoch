The case against August Hanning, the former head of Germany's foreign intelligence service who was arrested in early October, now has a description of the method [1]. According to reporting by the Süddeutsche Zeitung, NDR and WDR on Monday, his former office chief Manfred D. smuggled more than 2,000 strictly secret documents out of the BND's Berlin headquarters over almost a decade and handed them to Hanning [1].

The alleged route was ordinary. As personal assistant to four BND presidents, D. had access to the leadership's mailboxes and calendars and was allowed to print secret papers for briefing folders [1]. He could use his bosses' car service to get home; cars leaving the site were barely checked, security sources say [1]. At times, the reports say, he handed bags of documents to his bosses' bodyguards, who were not checked either and carried them unknowingly through the security gates [1]. His travel expenses, including weekend business-class flights between Berlin and Munich claimed as official trips, are also being examined [1].

Both men are presumed innocent. Hanning is under investigation for suspected espionage and attempted treason, D. for aiding them; Hanning's lawyer has rejected all accusations, and D.'s lawyer declined to comment [1].

For you: the weak point was not a firewall but a courtesy, the assumption that the people closest to the top need no checking. Germany has just given its services new powers. The test of that reform is whether it also brings the dull rule that everyone's bag goes through the scanner.
