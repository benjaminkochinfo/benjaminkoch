On Sunday the United States and nine partners in its Pax Silica initiative signed the San Francisco Declaration: Australia, India, Israel, Japan, South Korea, Qatar, Singapore, the United Arab Emirates and the United Kingdom [1]. Pax Silica was announced by Washington in December last year to secure supply chains for silicon and the minerals behind semiconductors and, by extension, AI [2]. The Hindu frames the signing as part of growing competition with China [2].

Read the text and the rhetoric is thin; the definitions are the substance. "Sensitive technologies" are goods, software, materials, knowledge, components and their means of production in AI supply chains whose theft or acquisition by hostile actors "would confer a meaningful military or intelligence advantage, or undue economic leverage" [1]. "Critical infrastructure" explicitly covers ports, terminals, transport, communications and logistics networks, the ICT and energy systems they run on, and the digital platforms that coordinate them [1]. That is a very wide net, wide enough to include the software that books a container.

The machinery comes next. The declaration resolves to set up five working groups: pro-innovation policy frameworks, adoption in key sectors, supply-chain cooperation across the stack, "safe harbors and supply chain security hubs", and security and resilience [1]. There is one telling footnote. The text speaks throughout of "Super Intelligence", and notes that "for some participants" this simply means artificial intelligence [1]; the US government uses the term after an executive order renamed AI [2].

For you: a declaration commits nobody to spend a cent. But a shared definition of what counts as critical is where export controls, investment screening and procurement rules later come from. Note, too, the list of signatories: no EU member state is on it [1].
