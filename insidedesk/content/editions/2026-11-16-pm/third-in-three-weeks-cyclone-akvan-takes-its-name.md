The deep depression over the Bay of Bengal became cyclonic storm Akvan on Monday evening, the India Meteorological Department said in its 17:30 IST bulletin (13:00 CET) [1]. It lay about 610 km east of Chennai and 660 km south-east of Machilipatnam, moving north-north-west at 10 km/h [1]. The IMD expects a severe cyclonic storm by the evening of 17 November and a very severe one around the morning of 19 November [1].

The forecast is unusually specific, and that is the point. Akvan is likely to cross the Andhra Pradesh and Yanam coasts between Nellore and Tuni around noon IST on 20 November, with maximum sustained winds of 120–130 km/h gusting to 145 km/h [1]. Squally winds of 40–50 km/h are expected off the Odisha coast from 18 November, and gale-force winds on coastal Andhra Pradesh on 19 and 20 November [1]. Chennai and parts of Tamil Nadu are on orange alert for very heavy rain on 18 and 19 November [1].

What makes this one heavier is the calendar. Akvan is the third cyclone in less than a month for the region, after Arnab, which made landfall in Andhra Pradesh on 28 October and brought devastating floods to southern Odisha, and Murasu earlier this month [1]. Wet ground floods faster, and tired crews work slower.

The response so far is the dull, correct kind. Odisha has ordered early evacuation, kept 24-hour control rooms open in every district and put disaster and fire services on standby; in Ganjam, officials have mapped flood-prone areas and landslide pockets [1]. Andhra Pradesh's chief minister, N. Chandrababu Naidu, told officials to deploy national and state disaster response teams in advance and to keep electricity crews ready to restore power [1]. Officials' weekend leave had already been cancelled [1].

Nobody can stop a cyclone. You can, however, decide four days ahead who sleeps where, and that is how a storm stays a weather story.
