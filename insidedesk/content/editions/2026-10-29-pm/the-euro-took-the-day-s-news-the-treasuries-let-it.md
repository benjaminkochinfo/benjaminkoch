Thursday handed markets three pieces of news, and only one of them moved a price much. The euro edged down to $1.1125 after the European Central Bank held its deposit rate at 2.50%, Reuters reports [1]. It has lost almost 2% against the dollar this month and 2.5% against sterling [1]. Investors hold their biggest short position on the euro in a decade, almost $19 billion [1].

**What moved it.** Two Frankfurt items came on the same day: a hold that read as cautious, and Christine Lagarde's announcement that she will leave on 7 February 2027 [2]. Markets had gone into the meeting pricing about a 76% chance of a December rise, ActionForex notes, so a statement stressing stable underlying inflation read as relatively dovish [2]. CNBC, citing LSEG data, had money markets at about 67% for December after the announcement [3]. Euro-area government bond yields edged lower too [1]. ActionForex is careful on cause: because the resignation came before the rate decision, the two effects "cannot be separated precisely" [2].

**What did not move.** The United States reported third-quarter growth of 3.4% annualised, above the 3.2% expected, and headline PCE inflation of 3.6% in September [2]. Either could have lifted yields. The two-year Treasury stayed near 4.687% and the ten-year near 5.00%, with a December Fed rise priced at about 83%, against about 85% a day earlier [2]. Desk arithmetic: the market had already paid for the news, so the receipt changed little.

**The calm point.** Europe's stocks did not join the euro's mood: the STOXX 600 remained about 1% higher as the ECB held, Reuters reports [1]. A weaker currency on a leadership date, with bonds and shares orderly, is a market doing its accounting in public, not a run. For you, a softer euro makes dollar-priced fuel a little dearer and European exports a little cheaper; both effects arrive slowly. Nothing here is investment advice.
