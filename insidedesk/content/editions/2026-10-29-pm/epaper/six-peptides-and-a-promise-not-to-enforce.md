The US Department of Health and Human Services said on Wednesday it will use "enforcement discretion": it will not take legal action against compounding pharmacies that make six unapproved peptides, BPC-157, epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, semax and TB-500, while the FDA works on a formal rule [1]. The notice is due in the Federal Register on Friday [2].

Normally a compounding pharmacy may only use ingredients on the FDA's bulk drug substances list, and adding one takes a proposed rule, public comment and a final rule [1]. An FDA advisory panel voted narrowly in July for adding six peptides [1]. FDA reviewers had argued against BPC-157, citing missing quality data, possible immune reactions and insufficient human evidence [3]. None of the six is FDA-approved [1].

HHS's case is that a regulated pharmacy beats the "research use only" grey market [1]. The conditions are the useful part: pharmacies must certify the identity and purity of the compounds and must now report side effects, which they did not have to before [1]. Critics are blunt. Dr Peter Lurie, a former FDA official, said the agency was "turning a blind eye" [1].

Whichever side is right, the adverse-event reports will be the first systematic data this market has had to produce. If you are offered one of these products, ask who made it, whether it came with a certificate of analysis, and where to report a side effect. A pharmacy that cannot answer has told you something.
