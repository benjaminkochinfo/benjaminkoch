Think of one hug you remember. Most people can. In a study of more than 15,000 people published in PLOS One on Wednesday, around 70% remembered a hug because of the positive feelings it inspired, most often because it made them feel comforted, understood or supported [1].

The data come from the Touch Test, a large survey of attitudes to touch, and this is the first study to look at autobiographical memories of hugging in a large sample of UK adults [1]. People who were more empathetic and more attuned to their own bodily sensations were particularly likely to recall such moments; people less comfortable with emotional closeness were less likely to [1]. Men and women differed in what they found most memorable [1].

The lead author, Anna Lena Dueren of the University of Bristol, said affectionate contact becomes part of lasting memory because it can "communicate comfort, support and understanding" [1].

The researchers list the limits themselves. Women made up 75% of participants, the findings may not carry over to cultures with different norms about touch and personal space, and the design cannot show cause and effect [1]. So the study does not prove that hugging changes anyone. It shows something more modest: that a few seconds of contact are, for many people, filed among the things worth keeping.

If someone near you is having a hard week, that is not a bad thing to know.
