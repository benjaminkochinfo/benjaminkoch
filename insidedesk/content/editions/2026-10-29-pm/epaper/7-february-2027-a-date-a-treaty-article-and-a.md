Earlier on Thursday, before the rate decision, the ECB published four sentences [3]. Christine Lagarde has told the Governing Council and António Costa, President of the European Council, that she will step down on 7 February 2027 [1]. Her successor will be appointed by the European Council under Article 283 of the EU treaty, and the Governing Council "thanked President Lagarde warmly and gave her a long standing ovation" [1].

**Why the timing gets read.** ActionForex puts the exit at about nine months ahead of schedule [3]. Reuters notes that it lets Emmanuel Macron and the other EU leaders fill the post before France's presidential election in the spring [2]. Berenberg's Holger Schmieding suggested the timing could reduce uncertainty around the appointment; ActionForex is careful to call that his interpretation, not an established motive [3]. "Clearly politics is having an increasingly important role for central banks," SEB Investment Management's Carl Hammer told Reuters [2].

**Why it matters less than it sounds.** Rates are set by a council, not a person, and today's hold was unanimous, Lagarde said [4]. "We think the institution is bigger than the person, but there's uncertainty," Deutsche Bank's Mark Wall wrote; he expects one more rise to 2.75% in December, while she is still in the chair [4]. The euro slipped to about $1.1125 after the decision [2], though ActionForex points out that the effects of the exit and of the policy news cannot be separated precisely [3].

For you, the rate on your savings and your loan is set by the council's decisions, not by who chairs it. A treaty article, a council vote and a date are the dull machinery that keeps it that way. Nothing here is investment advice.
