On Thursday the market read Alphabet's third-quarter results the way a headline reads: earnings per share missed, and the shares fell about 13% in early New York trading, on course for the company's worst reaction to results since it listed in 2004 [2]. The filing behind it reads differently. It is not the story of a business that stopped working. It is the story of a business that is spending faster than it collects, and of one line below the operating profit that swung hard.

Start with what went right, because the filing does. Revenue rose 25% to $127.6 billion [1]. Operating income rose 30% to $40.6 billion, an operating margin of 31.8% [1]. Google Cloud brought in $30.7 billion, up 103% on a year earlier, and its operating income rose from $3.6 billion to $11.1 billion [1]. Desk arithmetic: the cloud business now keeps about 36 cents of operating profit from every dollar of revenue, against about 24 cents a year ago. "We had a strong quarter and are now on a half-trillion dollar revenue run rate," said the chief executive, Sundar Pichai [1].

So why the miss? Net income fell from $35.0 billion to $26.2 billion, and earnings per share from $2.87 to $2.12 [1]. The reason sits in one line: "other income (expense), net" moved from a gain of $12.8 billion a year ago to a loss of $8.2 billion this quarter [1]. Desk arithmetic: a swing of almost $21 billion, larger than the whole fall in net income. That line holds things outside the day-to-day business, and it can move a long way in either direction. If you read only the earnings-per-share headline, you read the noisiest number on the page.

The more durable story is the cash, and the filing sets it out quarter by quarter in its own reconciliation table [1]:

[[chart:alphabet-cash-capex-29pm]]

In the last three months of 2025, Alphabet's operations brought in $52.4 billion and it spent $27.9 billion on property and equipment [1]. Since then the two lines have crossed. In the third quarter of 2026, operations brought in $42.3 billion and capital spending was $51.4 billion, leaving free cash flow of minus $9.1 billion [1]. Desk arithmetic: capital spending was about 121% of cash from operations, and more than twice the $24.0 billion spent in the same quarter a year earlier. Over the past twelve months free cash flow was still positive, at $19.7 billion [1], but the direction is plain.

The building is not finished, and part of it is waiting on other people. Alphabet's finance chief, Anat Ashkenazi, told analysts that outside computing power "came online only at the very end of Q3" [3]. Microsoft reported delays from third-party GPU providers the same evening and trimmed its own full-year spending forecast to $170 billion from $175 billion [3]. Alphabet kept its guidance of $195 billion to $205 billion for the year [2]. A quarter in which the bills for the building arrive before the rented capacity does is a quarter in which cash looks worst.

There is a payroll line too. Alphabet had 204,757 employees at the end of September, against 190,167 a year earlier [1]. Desk arithmetic: 14,590 more people. Whatever AI does to other companies' headcounts, the company building it is still hiring.

What should you take from it? First, that the filing is better reading than the reaction. A company whose operating profit rose 30% did not have a bad quarter in its business. It had a quarter in which it chose to spend more than it earned, plus a large loss outside the business. Second, that cash is the honest test of the AI build-out. Revenue growth shows that customers are buying; free cash flow shows whether the seller can pay for the next round without borrowing or running down its reserves. On that test, Alphabet's latest quarter came in below even the minus $2.7 billion analysts had expected [2].

The dull parts of the filing deserve the attention. The reconciliation table, the segment profit and the employee count do not move a share price in a morning. They tell you, more reliably than any keynote, what a company is actually doing with its money. Nothing here is investment advice.
