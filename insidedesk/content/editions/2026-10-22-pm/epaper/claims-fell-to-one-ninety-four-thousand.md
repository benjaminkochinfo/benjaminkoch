Initial claims for US unemployment benefits fell to a seasonally adjusted 194,000 in the week to 17 October, the Labor Department reported on Thursday [1]. Economists polled by Dow Jones had expected 200,000 [2]. The low for the year, 189,000 in the week of 18 July, was the lowest since the late 1960s; claims have averaged 212,000 over the past year, and the four-week average is now 197,750 [1].

That is one half of what CNBC calls a "low-hire, low-fire" labour market [1]. The other half is in the payroll data: non-farm payrolls rose by 29,000 in September and have averaged 69,000 a month in 2026, while the unemployment rate rose to 4.2%, largely because more people joined the labour force [1]. The week of 17 October is also the survey week for the next payrolls report, due on 5 November [1].

Jefferies' Thomas Simons put the dull version well: despite gloomy surveys and headlines about AI taking jobs, "the claims data suggests that labor market conditions are stable" [1]. For the Fed, which meets next week, that keeps the attention on inflation; markets expect no move in October and a quarter-point rise in December [1]. A weekly form filed at a state office is not exciting. It is, week after week, one of the most honest numbers there is. Nothing here is advice.
