A New Jersey food importer, Gellert Global Group, has sued in the US Court of International Trade over the 50% tariffs on Canadian goods [1]. Those tariffs were imposed under Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930, a Smoot-Hawley provision that no president had used for a tariff until this year [1]. The Liberty Justice Center, which brought the case, argues that Section 338 only allows duties to offset foreign discrimination against US commerce, and that Congress replaced it with the procedures of Section 301 in 1974 [1]. The complaint also points to the calendar. Canadian cheeses were added to the list on 8 September and duties were collected seven days later, although the statute requires a 30-day wait [1]. The importer wants the tariffs declared unlawful and repaid with interest, and has asked for a preliminary injunction [1].

A second case is about arithmetic. Express Fasteners, an Illinois importer of screws, told the same court that the 50% Section 232 duty on steel and aluminium derivatives is "void in its entirety" [2]. Its argument: US Customs never issued a rule explaining how it values the metal inside such products [2].

Neither filing asks whether tariffs are wise [1]. Both ask whether the paperwork was done. That is the narrower question, and usually the one that gets answered. Nothing here is advice.
