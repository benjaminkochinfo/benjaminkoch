**The podium.** On 16 October Emmanuel Moulin, governor of the Banque de France, told AFP that the ECB has instruments "when disorderly and unjustified market movements can disrupt the transmission of monetary policy", but that "today, those conditions are not met. We are not seeing financial fragmentation in the euro area" [1]. He blamed the rise in French borrowing costs mainly on "uncertainties over the budget vote", said banks and insurers could cope, and prescribed "fiscal consolidation" rather than central bank action [1].

**The print.** On Thursday morning France's 10-year yield rose 11.5 basis points to 5.11%, after touching 5.1415%, its highest since July 2002 [2]. The premium over German Bunds reached 173.58 basis points, the widest since November 2011, in the euro debt crisis [2]. German 10-year yields fell 4 basis points to 3.38% on safe-haven buying [2]. And France was not alone:

[[chart:ea-spreads-22pm]]

Belgium's spread widened more than 10 basis points to 111.28, its highest since 2012; Italy's rose 10.5 to 123, Greece's 8.5 to 102 and Spain's 7.5 to 71 [2]. By midday the French yield was still at 5.09%, according to Sharecast [4].

**The gap.** Desk arithmetic: in the week since Moulin spoke, the French yield has gone from "close to 5%" [1] to above 5.1% [2], and the move on Thursday came while the German yield fell. That is the shape of a country premium, not of a broken market. But Moulin's own test was fragmentation across the euro area, and on Thursday every spread on the Reuters list widened at once [2]. One day of that is a mood. A month of it would start to look like the thing he said he was not seeing.

**Verdict.** The podium holds, for now. Moulin did not promise calm; he set a threshold, and a threshold is more useful to you than a reassurance, because you can check it. Nordea's reading is the same in other words: unchanged rates next week, a December hike still likely, unless the market turns "clearly disorderly" well beyond France [3]. The fix Moulin named sits in parliament, not in Frankfurt [1]. Nothing here is advice.
