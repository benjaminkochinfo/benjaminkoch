Thursday produced a small inversion of the usual order. France's 10-year government bond yield rose 11.5 basis points to 5.11%, after touching 5.1415%, its highest since July 2002 [1]. The US 10-year Treasury yield went the other way, down more than 11 basis points to 5.032%, as investors bought bonds for safety [2]. Desk arithmetic: on those two snapshots, taken a few hours apart, Paris was paying about eight basis points more than Washington to borrow for ten years. Germany paid 3.38% [1].

The reasons were different on each side. In the US, yields fell with stocks: the Nasdaq was down 1.4% and the S&P 500 0.7% in the morning, as chip stocks sold off again and investors weighed Meta's and Tesla's spending plans [4]. In France, the yield rose for reasons Reuters lists plainly: worries about repairing the public finances, street protests, and Paris's difficulty passing a budget [1]. Investing.com adds the number behind the worry, a deficit of 5.4% of GDP [3].

Equities did not like either story. The STOXX 600 was down 0.7% in the afternoon, the CAC 40 1% and Italy's FTSE MIB 1.7% [3]. Brent crude rose 2.7% to $111.79 a barrel by midday after reports that the US is preparing renewed strikes on Iran and a Houthi drone attack on Saudi Aramco's Shaybah field [5]. The cushions were dull ones: Nestlé rose after its organic sales beat expectations, and SAP gained 1.7% after its results [3].

The constructive reading is not that France is in a crisis; Reuters quotes Citi's Giada Giani noting a consensus that European institutions "are stronger today than they were 15 years ago" [1]. It is that the bond market has stopped grading France on the euro area's curve and started grading it on its own. That is slower and more boring than a panic, and it is fixed by the same slow, boring thing: a budget that passes. Nothing here is advice.
