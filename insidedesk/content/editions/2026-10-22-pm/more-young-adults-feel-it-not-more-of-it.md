A study published in the American Journal of Public Health looked at nearly 11,000 Americans aged 18 to 25 who answered the National Health Interview Survey between 2020 and 2024 [1]. Its headline is plain: nearly one in five said they felt anxious every day in 2024, up from 15% in 2020 [1].

**The three lines.** The rise shows up everywhere you would look for it. Daily anxiety went from 15% to 19.5%; a reported anxiety diagnosis from 17.9% to 23.4%; prescription anxiety medication from 9.8% to 13.3% [1]. Desk arithmetic: each moved by roughly 30-36% in four years.

[[chart:us-young-adult-anxiety-22pm]]

**The line that did not move.** The intensity respondents reported "didn't change significantly overall" [1]. That is the quiet finding. More young adults are anxious every day, but the typical anxious young adult does not describe it as worse than in 2020. A society can respond to "more people" with capacity, and to "worse" with urgency; this survey points at the first.

**Who, not just how many.** The authors, led by Melissa Villodas of George Mason University, looked at groups in combination rather than one at a time [1]. In 2024, 44.6% of lesbian, gay and bisexual respondents reported a diagnosis, against 18.5% of straight respondents, and their medication use doubled from 12.9% to 26% [1]. Among women, 32.1% reported a diagnosis, against 14.7% of men [1]. And 31.3% of white respondents reported a diagnosis, against 12.7% of Black and 15.3% of Hispanic respondents, although Black and white young adults did not differ significantly in reported intensity [1]. The researchers point to access to care, stigma and how symptoms are recognised [1].

**The constructive reading.** The study cannot say what caused the rise after 2020 [1]. What it can do is tell a health system where to look: at groups whose anxiety is as strong but less often diagnosed. A survey that repeats every year, asks the same questions and gets published is unglamorous machinery. It is also how you find out that a gap is in the clinic door rather than in the person. Nothing here is medical advice.
