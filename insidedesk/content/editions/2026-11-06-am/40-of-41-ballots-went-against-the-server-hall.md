There were 41 data-centre ballot measures in ten US states on Tuesday [1]. Voters backed restrictions or rejected pro-development measures in 40 of them; Harwood, North Dakota, was the exception [1].

[[chart:data-centre-ballots-06nov]]

Ohio carried more than half the list [1]. SoftBank and SB Energy are developing what would be the world's largest planned AI campus there, with OpenAI saying it has secured 8 gigawatts at the PORTS-Pike site and Nvidia backstopping a project that could cost more than $500 billion [1]. That plan now faces a Democratic governor-elect who wants projects to cover 100% of utility costs and use union labour [1]. Texas, on track to become the world's largest data-centre market by 2030 on some forecasts, flipped its governorship on the same issue [1].

Thursday's chip rout was the financial translation of that politics: traders sold the build-out chain on the fear that permits and hearings will slow demand for servers and power gear [2]. The constructive point is narrower. Communities are using ordinary tools — ballots, utility-cost rules, labour conditions — to price local burdens into the boom. That is slower than a press release and more durable than a mood. Nothing here is investment advice.
