At least 34 flights across Europe were disrupted by bomb threats on Thursday, after seven similar alerts on Tuesday and one on Monday [1]. Aircraft from Ryanair, Aer Lingus, Wizz Air, Finnair, easyJet, Aegean, Iberia, Air Serbia and LOT were among those affected; all landed safely and searches found nothing suspicious [1]. Heathrow judged a threat "not credible"; Birmingham initiated full processes with emergency services [1][2]. Eurocontrol had about 27,400 flights scheduled in Europe that day — the disrupted share was tiny and expensive [1].

Fighter jets escorted aircraft in Italian, Austrian and Bulgarian airspace [1][2]. An Aer Lingus Dublin–Rome flight diverted to the Évreux military base in Normandy; passengers waited hours through sniffer-dog checks [1][2]. UK transport secretary Heidi Alexander called the wave a "malicious hoax" [2]. Italy's defence minister, Guido Crosetto, called the pattern "under-threshold warfare": designed to stay below the attribution line [1]. Investigators said publicly there was still no hard evidence of a hostile state behind the alerts [1][2].

Philip Baum, professor of aviation security at Coventry, notes that aviation faces multiple bomb threats every day, but that this week's feel different because authorities have classified them as specific [2]. The risk he flags is complacency: enough hoaxes, and a real one is harder to hear [2].

The constructive reading is unglamorous. Diversions, escorts and empty searches are the price of not guessing. Attribution will take longer than a news cycle. Until then, the right boring answer is the one already in force: treat the specific threat as real, clear the cabin, publish that it was empty, and keep the next flight's checklist unchanged.
