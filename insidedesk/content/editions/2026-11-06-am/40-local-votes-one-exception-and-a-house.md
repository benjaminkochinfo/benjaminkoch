Forty-one local ballot measures about data centres were on US midterm ballots across ten states [1]. Voters sided against data centres in every contest with results in hand except one: Harwood, North Dakota, where they rejected an added review of industrial projects [1]. Ohio alone accounted for 21 of the measures [1]. In a CNBC voter poll, 56% said data centres were a crucial or major factor in their decision [1].

The top of the ticket moved with the same current. In Ohio, Democrat Sherrod Brown defeated Senator Jon Husted after casting him as "the face of data centres", and Amy Acton won the governorship on a platform that includes a "conditional moratorium" unless projects cover 100% of utility costs and use union labour [1]. In Texas, Gina Hinojosa unseated Greg Abbott after blaming the data-centre build-out; she said on Thursday that deals with firms such as Elon Musk's would have to be "on our terms" and asked for a pause on a public-land sale tied to SpaceX [1].

Within hours of the polls closing, Rep. Greg Casar said AI company CEOs "should be called to testify under oath", and Rep. Robert Garcia, the presumptive next House Oversight chair, put it in two words: "Lawyer up" [1]. Silicon Valley spent heavily and still lost the races that mattered most on this issue [1].

The constructive reading is institutional, not apocalyptic. Local ballots, governors and congressional hearings are how a democracy digests a new industry's land, water and power claims. They are slow on purpose. Watch the docket, not only the share prices.
