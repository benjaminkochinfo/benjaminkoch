It is official. The CDU presidium and federal executive nominated Ilse Aigner, president of the Bavarian state parliament, on Monday morning, and she and Chancellor Friedrich Merz faced the press at the Konrad-Adenauer-Haus at lunchtime [1][2]. Merz said Germany would gain a president "with dignity and with heart" [1]. Aigner, 61, joked that "the chancellor made it exciting, for me too", and then said what she wanted the job to be: "I want to be a candidate of the centre" [1].

**The promise was about language.** "My language will be clear and understandable," she said, and she described herself as part of the citizens rather than above them [1]. She also struck the note this desk likes best: "Yes, we have problems in this country, but Germany is not a problem case" [1]. Merz tied the timing to the date itself, saying 9 November reminds Germans that democracy must be protected and "lives from those who carry it" [2].

**The support arrived before the vote.** SPD general secretary Tim Klüssendorf said Aigner can bring people together across party lines; the SPD presidium had already backed her [1][3]. Greens leader Franziska Brantner said her party was convinced Aigner could fill the role, and that putting up a rival or rejecting a Union proposal outright would not be the right answer "given the times we are in" [1]. The FAZ concludes that her election on 30 January is very likely, and by a broad majority [2]. Bundestag President Julia Klöckner noted that Aigner's election would put women in the two highest offices of state for the first time [1].

**Who claimed the win.** CSU leader Markus Söder called it "a historic day for the CSU"; he first floated her name in April [1]. The FAZ reports that Söder books the outcome as his success [2]. That is ordinary party bookkeeping, and harmless.

For you, the interesting part is the dull one. Three parties, in public and on the record, chose not to fight over the one office designed to be above the fight. That is the machinery working.
