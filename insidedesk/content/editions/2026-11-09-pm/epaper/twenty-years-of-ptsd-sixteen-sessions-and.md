Clinicians have long hesitated to offer trauma therapy to people with psychotic disorders, fearing that revisiting traumatic memories could worsen voices or paranoia, and such patients are still regularly excluded [1]. Researchers at Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam and Parnassia Group checked whether the reluctance was justified [1].

They followed 162 people with both PTSD and a psychotic disorder. Alongside usual psychosis care, participants were randomly assigned to prolonged exposure therapy, EMDR or cognitive therapy, while a fourth group received no trauma therapy during the study [1]. After six months, 45% of the prolonged-exposure group, 44% of the EMDR group and 56% of the cognitive-therapy group no longer met the diagnostic criteria for PTSD, against 25% in the control group [1]. Low mood, dissociation and negative thoughts about the trauma also fell; psychotic symptoms such as paranoia showed no clearly demonstrable change [1].

The participants had lived with PTSD symptoms for more than twenty years on average, and the course was no more than sixteen sessions [1]. "These treatments are effective and safe. Withholding them may actually be doing people a disservice," said lead researcher David van den Berg [1]. He also named the dull constraint: there is a shortage of therapists in psychosis services, so if the treatments work, "we must also ensure there is sufficient capacity to provide them" [1]. The study appears in Psychological Medicine [1].

For you: caution is a virtue until it becomes a waiting list. Testing it is how it stays a virtue.
