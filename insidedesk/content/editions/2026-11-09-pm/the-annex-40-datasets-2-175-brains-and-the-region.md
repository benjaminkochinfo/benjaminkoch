The Annex reads the method behind the headline. This week's paper, published in Nature and reported by ScienceAlert, tests a tidy story many textbooks tell: when you reinterpret an upsetting event, the front of your brain does the calming, so the people whose front brains work hardest should calm down best [1].

**The design.** Psychologists call the strategy cognitive reappraisal: changing a feeling by seeing the situation differently [1]. The team combined results from 32 research groups, 40 datasets and up to 2,175 participants, all following a standardised analysis plan registered in advance [1]. Participants viewed upsetting pictures in an fMRI scanner, simply watching some and trying to reinterpret others [1]. Three measures were compared with brain activity: how much relief people reported in the task, how often they said they used reappraisal in daily life, and how much activity fell in the amygdala, a structure involved in emotional responses [1].

**The results.** The measures did not line up as expected. Questionnaire scores showed no significant link with either relief in the task or reduced amygdala activity [1]. People who reported more relief did show larger amygdala reductions, but the link was very weak; the authors calculated a study would need 1,105 participants for an 80% chance of detecting it [1]. None of the three measures showed the expected link with activity in the established prefrontal networks, which were engaged both when people felt negative emotions and when they tried to change them [1]. "Simply engaging the brain regions involved in reappraisal does not necessarily mean that the reappraisal is personally meaningful or emotionally effective," said Maurizio Sicorello of Germany's Central Institute of Mental Health [1].

**The footnotes that matter.** The samples were non-clinical adults, so the results may not carry over to people with more severe symptoms [1]. Reinterpreting a picture in a scanner is not the same as getting through a breakup [1]. And the study did not test whether therapy builds emotional skills; Sicorello said existing evidence of treatment benefits remains relevant [1].

**For you.** Two lessons, both unglamorous. First, the size of this study is the point: a pre-registered plan across 32 groups is how a field checks its own favourite ideas, and effects this weak are easy for small studies to miss [1]. Second, a brain that is visibly working is not proof that the work is landing. The same is true of the friend who gives you good advice after a bad week. Whether it helps depends on whether it connects with your own experience, and that is still a conversation, not a scan.
