The weekend in the Strait of Hormuz produced claims from both sides. Iran's Revolutionary Guards Navy said two oil tankers caught fire after hitting mines while using an "unauthorised route", and the Fars agency reported a third tanker hit by an explosion [1]. The US military said its air force fired at the stern of a commercial vessel in the Gulf of Oman and disabled it as it tried to pass the US blockade of Iranian ports [1]. Reuters could not confirm any of the reported strikes [1].

**One ship, twice.** Open-source analysts have tentatively identified the burning tanker off Khasab, on Oman's Musandam peninsula, as the Navig8 Messi, an ADNOC-chartered product tanker [2]. It was hit once before in August, repaired, and sent back into the same trade lane [2]. Its crew was evacuated with no injuries reported, and at least two firefighting tugs were working on the blaze [2]. US Central Command said separately that it disabled the Iran-linked bulker Huda, built in 1999, after its crew ignored warnings [2].

**The number that matters.** The UK Maritime Trade Operations centre describes Iranian activity in the strait as constant, from drone overflights to VHF hailing [2]. Traffic is running at single to low double-digit transits a day, well below the escorted convoys seen early last week [2]. That is the figure that moves cargoes, freight rates and, eventually, the price of what you burn at home.

**The paper market looked the other way.** Brent fell $4.09, or 4.2%, to $94.23 a barrel by 14:17 CET today after President Trump said renewed strikes on Iran were "highly unlikely" while talks progressed [3]. UBS's Giovanni Staunovo pointed to weakness in the physical market from still-elevated Middle East flows [3]. Futures, ships and speeches are telling three different stories this evening; only the ships can be counted.

For you, the dull lesson holds: watch the transit tallies and the repair yards, not the claims. A tanker that is repaired and sent back is an act of stubborn commerce. Nothing here is investment advice.
