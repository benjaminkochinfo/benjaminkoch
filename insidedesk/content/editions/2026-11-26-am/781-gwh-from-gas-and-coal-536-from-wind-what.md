Thursday's electricity in the German-Luxembourg day-ahead auction averages €291.77 a megawatt hour, against €246.29 for Wednesday [1]. The quarter-hours run from €195.81 at 03:45 to €388.02 at 17:45 [1]. You have read those numbers already. What is worth reading this morning is the recipe behind them.

[[chart:de-power-day-ahead-26nov]]

Take Wednesday, the last full day on the meter. Energy-Charts counts about 1,624 gigawatt hours of German load [2]. Gas plants supplied about 370 GWh, lignite about 269 and hard coal about 142; onshore wind gave about 392 GWh, offshore about 144, and solar about 85 [2]. Desk arithmetic: fossil plants together delivered about 781 GWh, roughly 48% of the load; wind about 536 GWh, roughly 33%; the sun about 5%. On a late-November day that is not a crisis. It is the season showing its bill.

The point is mechanical, not moral. When the wind is moderate and the sun sets before five, the price of the last megawatt hour is set by the plant that burns something, and that plant's costs include fuel and carbon. A day with half the load on gas and coal will not clear at the prices of a breezy Sunday in October. That is why the chart climbed from late October and why the evening quarter-hours, not the night ones, carry the cost [1].

The oil side had its own day. Front-month ICE Brent for January delivery settled at $94.61 on Wednesday, up $3.21 or 3.51%, its largest one-day gain since 5 November and its highest settlement since 13 November, Dow Jones Market Data reports [3]. The trigger was the Houthi claim of a strike on Saudi Arabia's Ras Tanura refinery and reports of tankers hitting mines in the Strait of Hormuz [4]. "The broadening of the Middle East conflict threatens to halt the recovery in oil flows in recent weeks," ANZ's Daniel Hynes said, as quoted by Reuters [4].

Then read the line further down the same data sheet: month to date, Brent is still down 19.58% [3]. And the US Energy Information Administration counted crude inventories up 1.1 million barrels to 418.9 million in the week to 20 November [4]. Fear moves a price in an afternoon. Stocks, auctions and plant schedules move it the rest of the month.

For you, the useful distinction is between the two meters. A fixed electricity tariff absorbs Thursday's evening peak because someone hedged it months ago; a dynamic tariff rewards the dishwasher at 03:45. Neither is heroic. Both are contracts, and contracts are how a cold week becomes a manageable one.
