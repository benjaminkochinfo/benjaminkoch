California's coast flooded on Wednesday without a storm. Record-breaking high tides, boosted by this year's super El Niño, pushed seawater into beachside communities and onto roads and businesses up and down the state, the Guardian reports [1]. Los Angeles and Santa Barbara tied or broke all-time high-tide records at 8.19 ft and 8.21 ft, La Jolla set a record at 8.11 ft, and preliminary data suggested Monterey might have done so at 8.03 ft, according to the National Weather Service [1].

The sea had help from the calendar. The seasonal "king tides" coincided with unusually high Pacific water levels this year, including the lingering effects of a large Kelvin wave that travelled up the coast [1]. In Pacifica, south of San Francisco, the tip of a pier that had already been closed for structural damage collapsed into the sea; the weather service warned of waves around the Bay Area as high as 24 ft [1]. "This El Niño is unprecedented with its strength," the San Mateo county supervisor Ray Mueller told the Los Angeles Times; it happened "on a beautiful day", he said [1].

Then look at what did not happen. Most of the state avoided the worst case, coastal flooding amplified by intense winter rain [1]. San Francisco's mayor said officials were "only aware of minimal impacts" in the city; the airport, built on low-lying land, deactivated its emergency operations centre at noon after no impact on Tuesday or Wednesday [1]. In Sunset Beach, Orange County, a local fire chief said damage had been mitigated by how aggressively homeowners had fortified their houses in advance [1].

That is the quiet lesson for you, wherever your coast or river is. Preparation is invisible when it works: sandbags filled for weeks, airlines offering free changes, crews deployed before the water arrived [1]. Governor Gavin Newsom called the day "a preview of things to come" and asked for lasting fixes rather than moment-by-moment responses [1]. Records make the headline; maintenance budgets decide the damage.
