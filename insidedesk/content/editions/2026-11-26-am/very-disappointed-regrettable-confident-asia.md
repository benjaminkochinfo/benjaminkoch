On Thursday the answers came in from Asia, and they came in the dull language of procedure. Singapore said it was "very disappointed", Japan called the proposed duties "regrettable", South Korea asked for more talks, Vietnam pushed to close a trade deal and Taiwan said it was confident its agreed 15% would hold [1]. Nobody announced a counter-tariff.

Singapore's Ministry of Energy, Trade and Industry said the findings ignored the structure of an open, market-oriented economy: "Singapore's economic policies do not protect our domestic market or subsidise manufacturing for export" [1]. It will submit further comments during the consultation and urge the US trade office to reconsider; the proposal puts Singapore at up to 20%, with exemptions for some products [1]. Japan's chief government spokesman Minoru Kihara said Japan "does not have any non-market policy practices or structural excess production capacity" [1].

The more interesting answers were the practical ones. Vietnam said it would register for and attend the public hearing, tighten rules on forced labour, transshipment and origin fraud, and finish a reciprocal trade agreement "as soon as possible" [1]. Its capped rate would be 20% with a deal and could rise to 35% without one, Reuters reports [1]. India's trade ministry said it had no structural excess capacity and wants the question settled in its bilateral talks; Greer said India's 25% would be capped at 17% with a deal [1]. Thailand said its proposed rate of up to 18% was in line with earlier negotiations [1].

Taiwan's Vice Premier Cheng Li-chiun put it most plainly: "The US has one main goal, which is to rebuild the legal basis for its reciprocal tariff policy. It is not to change the content of the agreements themselves" [1]. China's embassy in Washington said the "so-called issue of 'industrial overcapacity' does not really exist", but stopped short of threatening retaliation and urged both sides to deliver on the Trump–Xi agreements [1].

Read together, the replies say something reassuring about the machinery. A tariff that runs through a statute, a comment period and a hearing invites lawyers, not warships. You may not like the rates. But a process with a docket gives exporters a calendar, and a calendar is what a purchasing department needs to plan a quarter.

Nothing here is investment advice.
