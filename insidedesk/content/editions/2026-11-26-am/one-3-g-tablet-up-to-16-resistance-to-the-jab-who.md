The World Health Organization has conditionally recommended zoliflodacin, a new oral antibiotic, as an additional single-dose treatment for gonorrhoea [1]. The interim guideline suggests one 3 g oral dose for uncomplicated infections of the genital and urinary tract in adolescents and adults [1]. It does not cover infections of the throat or rectum, or severe infections, and on current evidence it is not recommended in pregnancy [1].

Notice what did not change. A single 1 g intramuscular injection of ceftriaxone remains WHO's recommended first-line treatment [1]. The new drug is an additional door, not a replacement. A second new oral antibiotic, gepotidacin, was assessed and not recommended for general use, though it may be considered when the first-line option cannot be used [1].

Why a second door matters is in the report published alongside. WHO's 2026 Enhanced Gonococcal Antimicrobial Surveillance Programme collected data from 10 countries across five WHO regions [1]. Resistance to ceftriaxone and cefixime was detected in four of them and resistance to azithromycin in eight; the highest country-level shares of resistant samples reached 16% each for ceftriaxone and azithromycin, and 47% for cefixime [1]. The bacterium, WHO says, has developed resistance to successive classes of antibiotics [1]. There were an estimated 82.4 million new infections among adults worldwide in 2020 [1].

"Gonorrhoea has repeatedly demonstrated its extraordinary ability to develop resistance to medicines," said Dr Tereza Kasaeva, who directs WHO's department for HIV, tuberculosis, hepatitis and STIs; a treatment "that can be administered without an injection" could simplify delivery [1]. A tablet needs no needle, no trained injector and no cold clinic room. That is a logistics gain as much as a medical one.

The unglamorous half of the announcement is the one that keeps the gain. WHO asks that the new drugs come with continued surveillance, antimicrobial stewardship and timely investigation of suspected treatment failures, and it has launched an online compendium of stewardship tools for STI care [1]. You do not protect a new antibiotic with a press release. You protect it with lab reports, sampling schedules and the doctor who writes down when a treatment did not work.
