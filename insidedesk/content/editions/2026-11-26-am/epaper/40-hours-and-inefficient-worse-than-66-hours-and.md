Physicians who see their clinical practices as efficient report less burnout, more professional fulfilment and less intention to leave their jobs, whatever their weekly hours, according to a Stanford Medicine-led study published on 24 November in Mayo Clinic Proceedings [1]. The researchers analysed survey answers from 16,649 physicians at 14 US academic health systems [1].

The striking line: physicians who reported low practice efficiency and worked 40 hours or fewer a week experienced more burnout than those who reported high efficiency and worked 66 hours or more [1]. The questions were plain ones: do operational problems interfere with care, is patient information available, are tasks delegated properly, does the clinic feel chaotic? [1]

"You may not need to 'balance' those two goals, one against the other," said lead author Bryan Bohman, meaning productivity and well-being [1]. The study has limits that its authors state themselves: it relies on perceptions rather than objective measures, it is a snapshot, and it cannot show that improving efficiency would cut burnout; nor does it say long hours are harmless [1].

Still, its picture is familiar to anyone in an office: the operating room not ready, the missing scan, the paperwork after the last appointment [1]. Burnout often looks like a personal failing. Quite often it is a scheduling problem with a human face, and scheduling problems can be fixed by people with clipboards.
