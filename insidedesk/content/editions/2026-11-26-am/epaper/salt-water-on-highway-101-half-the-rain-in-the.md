Western Washington is living through flood and drought at once, KUOW reports [1]. King tides, given a boost of a foot or more by El Niño in recent weeks, have combined with powerful surf to flood roads and some homes in Grays Harbor County on the outer coast [1]. On Wednesday the Westport tide gauge peaked at 12.8 ft above typical low water, 22 inches above the predicted high tide [1]. The Quinault Indian Nation evacuated the two streets nearest the seawall in Taholah; residents of First Avenue are waiting to move into new homes a mile inland in January [1].

None of that salt water helps the taps. Seattle Public Utilities said on Monday its reservoirs were so low that it was deploying a diesel-powered pump to draw water from the bottom of its main reservoir on the Cedar River [1]. Since 1 October, the Cedar and Tolt watersheds, the area's two main supply sources, have received 7.5 inches of rain, half their usual amount [1]. Flows on the Green River have been at all-time lows, according to the managers of the Howard Hansen Dam, and the University of Nebraska-Lincoln's Drought Monitor lists the North Cascades in severe drought [1].

For now, KUOW reports, customers are not being asked to cut back [1]. The village moving inland and the utility moving a pump are the same kind of answer: unglamorous, planned, and paid for before the emergency.
