Surveys about money usually measure mood. This one, from the European Investment Bank's annual climate survey, measures behaviour, and that makes it worth reading slowly [1].

**The ledger.**
- 66% of people in Germany say they can afford noticeably less because energy prices rose over the past year, against an EU average of 60% [1].
- 42% have turned the heating down [1].
- 40% are saving on food or health spending [1].
- 44% have cut necessary car journeys, for shopping, doctors' appointments or visits to public offices; the EU average is 31% [1].
- 28% now drive more slowly to save fuel [1].

Some of these are good housekeeping. Others are the warning lights. A colder living room is a choice; a skipped trip to the doctor or the town hall is a cost deferred, often to a moment when it is larger. The EIB's vice-president, Nicola Beer, called high energy prices "an enormous burden" for millions, as quoted by Bild [1].

**What the dull advice is worth.** According to the consumer advice centres, every degree less room temperature cuts heating energy use by about 6%; rooms should not stay below 16 °C, to avoid damp and mould [1]. The Federal Environment Agency puts the saving from a water-saving shower head at €100 to €200 per person a year in hot-water costs [1]. It also says a car at 100 km/h instead of 120 uses about 15% less fuel on the same stretch [1]. In Euronews's worked example, a petrol car using 8 litres per 100 km at 120 km/h, at €2.11 a litre, saves about €2.53 per 100 km; over 5,000 motorway kilometres a year that is around €127 [1]. Your car, route and traffic will change the figure [1].

**What it means for you.** Take the cheap, reversible savings first: a degree in rooms you barely use, short bursts of airing instead of tilted windows, a switchable power strip for the television corner [1]. Keep the appointments. If the budget truly no longer stretches, the institutions built for that moment, from the consumer advice centre's energy check to your local housing-benefit office, are cheaper than a cold flat or a missed diagnosis. Nothing here is investment advice.
