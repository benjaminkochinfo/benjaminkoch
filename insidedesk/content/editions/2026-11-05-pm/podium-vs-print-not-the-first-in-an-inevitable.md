**The podium.** At 13:00 CET the Bank of England raised Bank Rate by 0.25 percentage points to 4%, its first rise in more than three years [3]. The vote was 8–1, and the one dissent wanted more: Catherine Mann preferred 0.5 points, to 4.25% [1]. The Bank's reasoning is unusually plain. UK inflation rose to 3.5% in September and is expected to reach about 5% by the middle of next year; evidence of knock-on effects on wages and other prices is still "limited", but the pressure "in the pipeline" now warrants a rise [1]. Then the Governor drew a careful line: today's increase "shouldn't be seen as the first in an inevitable long sequence of hikes. But, unless the conflict in the Middle East is resolved, neither does it seem likely to be the last" [2]. The minutes add: "Monetary policy is not on a pre-set path" [1].

**The print.** The market heard the second sentence louder than the first. Reuters reported that traders priced around 76 basis points of further tightening by the end of 2027, up from roughly 67 before the decision, and that two-year gilt yields rose about 3 basis points to 4.77% [3]. Sterling barely moved, at about $1.3468 [3]. Desk arithmetic: 76 basis points is roughly three more quarter-point rises, which would take Bank Rate to about 4.75%.

**The gap.** It is smaller than it looks. "Not inevitable" is not "not likely", and the Governor tied further moves to one condition he does not control. The market simply attaches a probability to that condition and writes it down. Not every reader agreed with the pricing: BlackRock's UK strategist told Reuters markets price "too many" rises and sees room for one more, and Schroders' chief economist expects a peak at 4.25% [3]. That disagreement is the system working: the Bank publishes its reasons, the minutes name each vote, and the price moves in public.

**For you.** If you are fixing a mortgage or a savings rate in pounds, you are buying the market's number, not the Governor's sentence. The Bank meets again on 17 December [1]. Nothing here is investment advice.
