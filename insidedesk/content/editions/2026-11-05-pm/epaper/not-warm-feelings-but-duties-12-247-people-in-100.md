For three decades, questionnaires about collectivism kept producing odd results; one meta-analysis even concluded that Japan was less collectivistic than the United States [1]. A team led by Thomas Talhelm of Arizona State University argues the surveys were measuring the wrong thing [1]. Classic scales mixed "warm fuzzy" items, such as enjoying time with others, with items about binding duties to specific people. Western respondents agreed more with the first kind, cancelling out the second [1].

The team built a 14-item scale of what it calls "responsibilism": concrete obligations in close relationships, paired with weaker obligations to strangers [1]. After quality checks, 12,247 participants, mostly students, in 100 cultures covering 90% of the world's population took part; the scale was translated into 45 languages [1]. The gap between East Asian and Western cultures came out at more than a full standard deviation [1]. Responsibilism scores lined up with census measures such as household size, with blood donations to family rather than strangers, and with fewer returned lost wallets in field experiments; the older scales did not [1].

The caveats: the samples are students, the country links are observational, and the main study was not preregistered, although data and code are public [1].

The useful lesson is about measurement. Ask people how they feel about "others" and you get pleasant noise. Ask what they owe a particular friend and you get something that predicts behaviour.
