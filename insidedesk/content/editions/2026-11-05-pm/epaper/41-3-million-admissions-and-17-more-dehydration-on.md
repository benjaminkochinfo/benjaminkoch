A hospital is meant to be the safest place to be ill. On very hot days, a new study suggests, it is a little less so, and the reason is as basic as a glass of water.

Researchers at the RWI Leibniz Institute analysed billing data from a large German statutory health insurer covering 41.3 million hospital admissions in 2,108 clinics between 2005 and 2025 [1]. They looked only at patients with no dehydration recorded on arrival. Compared with stays on days of 15 to 20 °C, the probability of becoming dehydrated in hospital rose by 0.63 percentage points on days above 30 °C, a 17% increase on a baseline of 3.7% [1].

The effect was clearly stronger in older patients and in people with dementia [1]. The researchers also checked acute kidney failure, a harder, measurable complication linked to dehydration: above 30 °C its risk was 0.08 points, or 4.7%, higher than at 15 to 20 °C, on a baseline of 1.7% [1].

The authors conclude that hospitals cannot fully protect patients during extreme heat, and that heatwaves affect safety inside clinics, not only demand at the door [1]. Their list of remedies is pleasingly unglamorous: better cooling, structured drinking and hydration plans, closer monitoring and staffing plans for the most vulnerable groups [1].

None of this needs to wait for summer. Hydration charts, fans and rotas are decided in winter budgets. If someone you love is in hospital next July, ask who checks their drinking, and how often.
