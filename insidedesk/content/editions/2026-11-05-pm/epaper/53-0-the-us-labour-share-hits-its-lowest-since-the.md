The US Bureau of Labor Statistics published its third-quarter productivity figures on Thursday, and the top line is the kind central bankers like. Output per hour in the non-farm business sector rose at an annual rate of 3.6%, as output grew 3.7% and hours worked only 0.1% [1]. Unit labour costs, the wage bill per unit produced, fell 0.7% [1].

For anyone worried about a wage-price spiral, that is reassuring: hourly pay rose 2.9%, but each hour produced more, so the cost of labour in each item fell [1]. Over four quarters unit labour costs are up 2.4% [1].

**The line further down.** The same release says the labour share, the percentage of output that goes to workers as pay and benefits, was 53.0% in the third quarter, "the lowest level in the series, which begins in the first quarter of 1947" [1]. Real hourly compensation, after prices, rose 1.1% at an annual rate in the quarter and 1.0% over the year [1].

Desk arithmetic: if productivity rose 2.1% over the year and real pay 1.0%, the gap between the two is about one percentage point, and that is where the share went.

None of this is a scandal; it is a ledger. Productivity gains are real and welcome. Who receives them is settled later, in wage rounds, prices and tax. The BLS revises this release on 8 December [1]. Nothing here is investment advice.
