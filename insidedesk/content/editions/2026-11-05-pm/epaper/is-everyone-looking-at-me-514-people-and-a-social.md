Some psychologists have proposed that people with social anxiety have a wider "cone of gaze": they judge more angles as someone looking straight at them [1]. A team led by Madeleine Moses-Payne at University College London pooled ten earlier studies with 403 participants and found a modest effect, but the studies were small and inconsistent [1]. So they ran three new online experiments with 514 people [1]. Combined, they found no overall link between social anxiety and the cone of gaze, and angry faces did not widen it either [1]. Participants were not clinically diagnosed and testing was online, so a lab setting might differ [1].

A finding that fails to replicate is not a failure. It is the system working: one fewer shaky marker on the way to the clinic, and a reminder for you that feeling watched is not the same as seeing it.
