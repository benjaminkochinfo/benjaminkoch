The Federal Reserve announces its decision at about 19:00 CET, an hour and a half after this edition goes out. So this is not a report of what it did. It is a list of what the committee had in front of it when it sat down, and almost all of that is public. You can read the same papers.

**The shop price.** US consumer prices rose 3.7% in the year to September, driven by energy, CNBC reports [1]. Desk arithmetic from the Bureau of Labor Statistics index levels gives the same 3.7%, up from 2.4% in January and February [2]. It is one of the longest runs of annual readings above 2% on record [1].

**The wholesale bill.** The producer price index for final demand, which tracks what businesses charge each other, rose 6.3% in the year to September, the steepest twelve-month rise since 2022 [1]. Desk arithmetic: it was 3.1% in January, so the rate has roughly doubled in eight months, and it now runs 2.6 percentage points above the shop price [2]. That gap is the interesting number. A wholesale bill that rises faster than the shelf price is either squeezed out of margins or passed on later.

[[chart:cpi-ppi-28pm]]

**Tomorrow's print, already half known.** The personal consumption expenditures index, the Fed's preferred measure, is published on Thursday. Because economists build it from the CPI and PPI, its shape is largely known: headline inflation is expected to rise to 3.7% from 3.4%, and core inflation to 3.1% [1]. CNBC's Steve Liesman notes that the three-month annualised core rate, which fell to 2.05% last month and was cited by doves, would jump to about 2.7% with a 0.3% monthly rise [1]. A measure that moves that much in one month is a weather vane, not a compass.

**What the market expects.** Futures price less than a 10% chance of a rise tonight, after September's quarter-point increase to 3.75%–4%; traders expect the next rise in December [1]. Goldman Sachs reads the committee as hiking every other meeting and expects little new from the press conference [1]. JPMorgan's analysts expect at least two dissents [3]. Michael Feroli, JPMorgan's chief US economist, puts the awkward question plainly: why hike after a 0.2% core print in September but hold after an expected 0.3% [1]? Before the decision, the two-year Treasury yield was up 3.7 basis points at 4.774% and the ten-year at 5.03% [1]. Those yields are the market's vote, cast in money.

**A neighbour moved first.** At 14:45 CET the Bank of Canada raised its policy rate by a quarter point to 2.5% [4]. Its reasoning is worth reading for the dull machinery in it: businesses "have mostly absorbed higher input costs so far", but the Bank's surveys suggest more of those costs will be passed on over the next year [4]. It raised rates "to reduce the risk" that high energy prices spread to everything else [4]. That is the wholesale-bill argument, made by a committee that has already voted.

**Frankfurt reads loan books.** The European Central Bank decides on Thursday. Its own data, published on Tuesday, show adjusted lending to euro-area households growing 3.1% a year in September and to firms 4.2%, both unchanged; broad money, M3, grew 3.5% [5]. Deposits placed by companies grew 5.3%, up from 4.7% [5]. Credit is still flowing at a steady pace. Whatever the Governing Council decides, it will not be reacting to a lending freeze.

**What this means for you.** Over the next 36 hours you will hear a great many adjectives. The useful information is in the documents: the producer price index, Thursday's PCE, the two-year yield, and the line in each statement about costs being passed on. A committee that watches invoices is doing its job; the statement is how it explains the bill. If you have a variable-rate loan or are about to fix one, the two-year yield is often a better guide than any adjective at the press conference. Nothing here is investment advice.
