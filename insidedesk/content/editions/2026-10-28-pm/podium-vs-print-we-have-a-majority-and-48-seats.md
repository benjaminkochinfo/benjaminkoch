**The podium.** Yair Golan, who leads the centre-left Democrats, spent Wednesday morning refusing to call the result a tie. "I know how to count to 120. 65 is more than 55. That is how Israeli democracy works. Period. We have a majority," he said, and called for "a change government in partnership with Arab citizens of Israel" [1]. "The vote of an Israeli citizen casting a ballot in Tira is worth exactly the same as the vote of an Israeli citizen casting a ballot in Givat Shmuel," he added [1].

**The print.** With 93% of ordinary ballots counted, 85.6% of the total, the Central Elections Committee gave Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc 53 seats, the bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot 48, and the Arab parties 19, a historic high [2]. Sixty-one is a majority [2]. The committee says the last 7% of ordinary ballots need extra checks, about 600,000 double-envelope votes are still to come, and it does not expect to update the figures soon [2].

**The gap.** Desk arithmetic: 48 plus 19 is 67, which is Golan's majority, now a little larger than his "65". Without the 19, the change bloc is 13 seats short. And the 19 are the part in dispute. Eisenkot has said he would partner with Arab parties only if they recognise Israel as a Jewish state and Hamas as an extremist group, conditions the Guardian says effectively rule out a deal; more right-wing parties in his bloc refuse altogether, and the Democrats are the only ones openly willing [1]. Netanyahu, at 53, has called the idea treasonous [1].

**The verdict.** Golan's sum is correct, and his point about equal votes is the kind a democracy should be glad to hear said out loud. But a majority in a parliament is not the number of seats that dislike the same man; it is the number that will vote for the same budget. That is decided in talks, not statements, and Herzog has asked everyone to keep "an open mind" [1]. You can treat the next fortnight's speeches as opening bids. The figure to watch is the one the committee publishes when the envelopes are in.
