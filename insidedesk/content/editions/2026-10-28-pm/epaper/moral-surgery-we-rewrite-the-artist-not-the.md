What happens to your memory of a favourite show when its star is disgraced? A study in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology suggests that your memory does some tidying, and it is selective [1].

Across five experiments, Sally MyungJin Shin of the University of South Florida, with Paul Rozin of the University of Pennsylvania and Gal Zauberman of Yale, asked people about creators and their work before and after they learned of the creators' wrongdoing [1]. People lowered their present opinion of both. In hindsight, though, they "selectively downgrade past views of the creator while keeping past enjoyment of the creation shielded", Shin said [1]. The examples were Bill Cosby, Ray Charles and John Lennon. Told about Charles's parental neglect or Lennon's history of physical abuse, listeners liked the men and the music less today, but remembered loving the music just as much as before [1].

The authors call it "moral surgery" [1]. By lowering their view of the person after the fact, people protect their nostalgia. "The ability to separate art from the artist exists only when we look back in time," Shin said [1].

That is worth knowing the next time an argument about cancelling an artist goes round your family. The question "can you still enjoy the work?" has two answers. One is about today. The other is about the memory, and the study suggests memory makes its own arrangements.
