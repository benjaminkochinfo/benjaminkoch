This desk's job on a Sunday is to separate what the week decided from what it merely hoped. The oil market spent the week hoping, and with some reason. The supply routes spent it fighting.

**What the vote did.** Brent fell 8% on Wednesday alone after the Democrats swept the midterms, settling at two-month lows [1]. The result was read as a rebuke of the war with Iran and as a step towards de-escalation, which could mean more crude leaving the region [1]. "A bit of unwinding of the war premium seems to be what's going on," said Phil Flynn of Price Futures Group [1]. Spot premiums for Middle Eastern crude benchmarks have returned to pre-conflict levels, Reuters reports, a sign of an emerging surplus [1].

**What the routes did.** On Thursday Tehran said three more ships had been struck in the Strait of Hormuz [2]. On Friday a Greek ship manager said a seafarer had been killed in a 5 November explosion on an oil-products tanker in the Gulf, believed to have been a missile attack, and UKMTO data suggested attacks on oil-carrying ships were still running close to a record pace [1]. On Sunday morning Iran said two more oil tankers had struck mines and caught fire while trying to cross the strait on unauthorised routes; it gave no further details [4].

In the Red Sea corridor, the Saudi-led coalition said it had destroyed three Houthi ballistic missiles fired at the kingdom, two of them towards Riyadh [3]. The Houthis claimed six operations against targets including Aramco oil facilities and Riyadh airport [3]. Saudi Arabia shut its East-West crude pipeline, the main bypass of Hormuz, from 11 to 22 September after drone attacks, and the Houthis claimed further strikes on its pumping stations in late October [2]. The UN counts more than 1,600 people killed and almost 300,000 displaced in Yemen since a 2022 ceasefire collapsed in July [3].

**Why both can be true.** A barrel's price is a forecast of next quarter's flows; a missile is this morning's fact. Rystad's Janiv Shah put it carefully: the crude market is loosening towards oversupply on paper, but longer routes and ship-to-ship transfers near Hormuz still separate what refiners can take from what is available [1]. "The market is still looking for evidence of a firm deal being signed," he said [1].

**For you.** Last week's fall in crude is real and has reached the forecourt in small steps. But the routes that carry the oil were no calmer on Sunday than on Monday. When you hear that energy is getting cheaper, the honest addendum is: for now, on paper, until the next tanker.
