Yesterday this desk could only quote the overnight screens. This morning there is an official number. On Wednesday, the first full trading day after the US vote, the Treasury's closing par yield for the ten-year note was 4.90%, down from 4.95% on election day [1]. The two-year closed at 4.58%, from 4.61%, and the thirty-year at 5.28%, from 5.32% [1].

[[chart:ust-vote-vs-october-05nov]]

Set beside the month, those moves look small. On 1 October the ten-year closed at 5.24% and the two-year at 4.78% [1]. Desk arithmetic: since then the ten-year has fallen 34 basis points and the five-year 32, against 5 each on the day after the vote; the two-year has fallen 20, against 3 [1]. A basis point is a hundredth of a percentage point. In plain terms, most of the easing in American borrowing costs happened before anyone counted a ballot. Only the shortest bills went the other way: the three-month yield was 4.21%, up from 4.17% on 1 October [1].

What moved it on Wednesday was less the vote than the oil. Brent crude settled 8.7% lower at $94.62 a barrel, its lowest in about two months, and Investing.com reported that lower energy prices led investors to scale back expectations of further Federal Reserve rate rises, with Treasury yields falling across the curve [2]. Cheaper oil today means smaller inflation prints later, which is what bond buyers are paid to anticipate.

Your own rates are set closer to home. At 13:00 CET the Bank of England announces its decision; economists polled by Reuters expect a quarter-point rise to 4.0%, its first since August 2023 [3]. Whatever London decides, the habit is the same: wait for the close, and compare it with the month, not with the night. Nothing here is investment advice.
