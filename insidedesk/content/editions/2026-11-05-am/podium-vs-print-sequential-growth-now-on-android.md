**The podium.** Qualcomm's chief executive, Cristiano Amon, had a good line ready on Wednesday evening. Sales of Android handsets, he told Reuters, had bottomed out: "We're starting to see sequential growth now on Android" [2]. He added that the company had "called this memory thing early" and prepared for it [2]. On the data-centre side he went further: "We don't have a demand issue; it's a supply issue" [1].

**The print.** The quarter itself tells a harder story. Revenue from the handset unit, Qualcomm's core business, fell 26% on a year earlier to $5.14 billion, below the estimates of both StreetAccount and Visible Alpha [1][2]. That happened even though Qualcomm raised its prices in September [1]. Adjusted group revenue fell 7% to $10.44 billion; it still beat forecasts, as did adjusted earnings of $2.17 a share [1][2]. Behind the phone numbers is the wider market: global smartphone shipments fell 20% in the September quarter to a 14-year low, according to Counterpoint Research, as dearer memory chips pushed up device prices [2].

**The gap.** Sequential and year-on-year are different questions, and both answers can be honest. A business can grow on last quarter and still be a quarter smaller than a year ago. What the release does not yet show is the recovery itself; it shows a guidance range. Adjusted earnings for this quarter are guided at $2.15 to $2.35 a share, whose $2.25 midpoint sits below the $2.27 analysts expected [2]. Bernstein's Stacy Rasgon called the margin outlook "lousy" [2]. Desk arithmetic: if $5.14 billion is 26% below a year earlier, the same quarter last year brought in roughly $6.9 billion, a gap of about $1.8 billion.

**What the market did.** The shares fell about 7% in extended trading [1]. By Thursday morning the mood had travelled: chip shares slid in Seoul and Tokyo, and South Korea's KOSPI fell as much as 5.2%, triggering a five-minute trading curb, with Investing.com citing disappointing results from Qualcomm and Arm [3]. The bright parts are real too: car-chip revenue rose 89% to $1.99 billion [1]. For you, the habit holds: when a chief executive reaches for "sequential", look up the year-on-year line before you believe the turn. Nothing here is investment advice.
