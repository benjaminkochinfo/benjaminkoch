**The document.** "Social Isolation, Loneliness, and Biological Aging Acceleration", by Rui Mao, Xin Meng and colleagues from several Chinese medical institutions, in *Health Psychology* [1]. It draws on 309,746 adults in the UK Biobank, recruited between 2006 and 2010, with an average age of about 56 at the start, followed for a median of 14.4 years [1]. Biological age was estimated with PhenoAge, a formula built from routine blood markers of inflammation and organ function [1].

**The line.** After adjusting for age, income, education, smoking, sleep, diet, exercise and a long list of illnesses, the most isolated group was biologically about 0.33 years older than expected, roughly four months, and the lonely group about 0.24 years, roughly three months [1]. People who were both isolated and lonely showed the largest gap [1]. Each one-point rise on the isolation scale went with a 13.9% higher risk of death from any cause over the follow-up; each point on the loneliness scale with 8.9% [1].

**The lines around it.** Looked at item by item, living alone went with 0.27 years, and lacking someone to confide in with 0.22 years, more than "often feeling lonely" at 0.14 years [1]. "Not having someone to confide in showed an association at least comparable in magnitude to reporting feelings of loneliness," the authors told PsyPost [1]. Genetic risk did not multiply the social effect; the two simply stacked [1].

**The caveats.** The authors call these "modest average differences in an aging indicator, rather than estimates of months of life lost or predictions for an individual" [1]. Biological age was measured once, so the study cannot show that a change in someone's social life changes their biology, and illness may itself make people withdraw [1]. Isolation and loneliness were captured by a handful of short questions, and the sample was mostly of European ancestry [1]. Only about 5 to 7% of the link with mortality ran statistically through biological age [1].

**The constructive reading.** The finding that matters most is the cheapest to act on. Not the number of contacts but whether one of them is someone you can tell things to. The authors warn against reading this as proof that joining a club makes anyone younger, and say social circumstances are shaped by health, caregiving and access to community resources, "so the implications extend beyond individual choices" [1]. That is a case for the dull institutions: the sports club with a Tuesday slot, the parish hall, the GP who asks who is at home. They do not reverse ageing. They keep a name in the diary.
