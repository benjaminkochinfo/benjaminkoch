Democrats will open the 120th Congress on 3 January with at least 54 of the 100 Senate seats, and two races are still undecided [1]. What decides them now is not campaigning but procedure.

In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom led Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by 1,138 votes on Wednesday night, with about 96% of ballots counted and both candidates on about 49%, according to The Associated Press [1]. Secretary of State Michael Watson counted roughly 3,600 mail-in ballots still to be tallied, plus an "unknown" number of affidavit ballots, the provisional votes cast by people whose eligibility must be checked [1]. Mail ballots count if they arrive within five business days of the election [1]. "Counties have 10 days from the date of the election to submit their certified results to our office, which is November 13," Watson wrote [1]. Mississippi has no formal provision for an automatic recount; a candidate would need a court order [1].

In Alaska, Democrat Mary Peltola led Republican Senator Dan Sullivan by 637 votes on Wednesday night, per AP, with nearly a quarter of votes still uncounted [1]. If nobody passes 50%, ranked-choice rounds follow, and the Division of Elections says those results will not be published until 18 November [1]. A Justice Department suit to stop certification was dismissed on Sunday; the department has said it will appeal [1].

For you, the reassuring part is the dullness. Deadlines are set in advance, envelopes are checked one by one, and disputes go to judges. A count that takes until the 13th or the 18th is not a count in trouble. It is a count doing its job.
