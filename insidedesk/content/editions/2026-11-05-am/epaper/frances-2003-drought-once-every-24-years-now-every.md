Drought conditions in France comparable to 2003 were historically about a one-in-24-year event and have become roughly a one-in-four-year event under today's climate, according to Swiss Re's analysis, cited by Marcel Andriesse, its head of agriculture and parametrics [1]. "We cannot price tomorrow's drought insurance using yesterday's climate," he said [1].

His remedy is not a new slogan but a different kind of contract. Parametric cover pays a set amount when an objectively measured trigger is hit, rather than after a loss adjuster has visited. Andriesse said it is "gaining traction" in reinsurance but should "earn its place in a program on its merits", complementing traditional indemnity cover rather than replacing it [1].

The machinery is improving. Radar satellites can now map flooded areas to a few metres, at night or through cloud, so payouts can follow actual flooding rather than rainfall or river levels alone [1]. He named low river levels disrupting transport, wildfire and heat hitting tourism, and weather-related business interruption without physical damage as areas where such triggers could be used [1].

The most constructive idea is timing. A fire-weather trigger could release money for extra patrols or vegetation clearing before a blaze; a drought trigger could pay for emergency irrigation or water for livestock, as Swiss Re already does with the World Food Programme [1]. He also warned that "a softer insurance market should not lead us to underestimate the hazard" [1]. Cheaper premiums are not smaller risks.
