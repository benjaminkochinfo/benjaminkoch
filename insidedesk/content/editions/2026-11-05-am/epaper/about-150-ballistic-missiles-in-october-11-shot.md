Russia launched around 150 ballistic missiles at Ukraine in October, Ukraine's air force said, and air defences shot down 11 of them, about 7%, Euromaidan Press reports [1]. Desk arithmetic: that leaves roughly 139 that were not intercepted.

The reason is stock, not skill. Ukraine has around eight Patriot batteries, and by October they were out of missiles as often as not [1]. On 31 October the US State Department approved the sale of 500 new PAC-3 missiles to Ukraine in a $2.1 billion deal financed by European allies, but the first are not expected for several years [1]. It takes Lockheed Martin nearly four years from payment to deliver a PAC-2 or PAC-3, though the company is raising output from 600 a year to 2,000 [1]. Zelenskyy has asked partners to hand over missiles from their warehouses now and refill them later from the contracted production [1].

The outlook is attributed and sober. Analysts Fabian Hoffmann and Anastasiia Lapatina estimate Ukraine "could face an average of 130 to 190 ballistic missile projectiles per month" through the winter [1]. Ukraine's military intelligence says Russia holds up to 700 ballistic missiles and can build up to 110 a month [1]. Alternatives exist but are thin: two Franco-Italian SAMP/T batteries, and a Ukrainian anti-ballistic missile, Freyja, whose Ukrainian half its maker Fire Point says is ready while the European seekers, radars and integration are not [1].

For you, the dull lesson is about inventories. A defence contract is a promise; a missile in a depot is a capability. The swap Zelenskyy proposes, today's stock for tomorrow's production, is the sort of logistics decision that decides winters.
