Psychologists call it delay discounting: the more a reward is delayed, the less it seems to be worth. Someone who prefers $20 today over $50 in a month discounts steeply [1]. Researchers at Columbia, Yonsei and Gachon universities asked whether the shape of the brain carries clues about who does this, and published the answer in *JAMA Psychiatry* [1].

They scanned 146 college-aged adults, average age about 22.6, measured the volumes of grey and white matter region by region, and trained machine-learning models on 80% of the group to predict the discounting scores of the remaining 20% [1]. The white matter model explained on average 22% of the variation; the grey matter model 19% [1]. Two areas mattered in both: the right accumbens area, involved in reward and motivation, and the right frontal pole, involved in planning and weighing consequences [1].

The authors are careful. It is a proof of concept on one scanner, each test set held only about 29 people, accuracy varied a good deal between splits, and the design cannot say whether brain structure shapes the habit or the habit shapes the brain [1]. "Our findings should not be interpreted as suggesting that brain scans can determine how an individual will behave," said Wi Hoon Jung, one of the senior authors [1]. Delay discounting, he added, can change with circumstances and through learning or intervention [1].

Read it the useful way round. Desk arithmetic: 78 to 81% of the variation sits outside these volumes, about four-fifths of what separates the patient from the impatient. It lives in circumstances, habits and the arrangements around a choice. That is the part a standing order, a savings plan or a shopping list can reach.
