On 21 November the Sparta II, a 121-metre Russian-flagged cargo ship, was reportedly hit by two drones in the eastern Gulf of Finland, west of Gogland Island, Euronews reported [1]. It caught fire, lost the ability to manoeuvre and drifted into Finnish territorial waters; its 16 crew were evacuated and one was slightly injured [1]. By Sunday evening Russian vessels were towing it away, and Finland's president Alexander Stubb said the ship had left Finnish waters [1].

The details matter. The ship is operated by Oboronlogistika, a company associated with Russia's defence ministry, was sailing from St Petersburg to Kaliningrad and is under US sanctions [1]. Its cargo has not been publicly established; Finnish authorities said military equipment could not be ruled out [1]. Finnish military officials assessed that Ukraine was likely responsible; Ukraine had not confirmed it [1].

Early on Sunday, the Guardian reported, the Russian corvette Merkury was struck by suspected Ukrainian sea drones while docked in Benghazi, Libya; satellite imagery showed it had sunk [2]. A Russia-linked cargo ship, the Ascalon, was hit off Benghazi later; no one claimed responsibility, and the UN mission in Libya called for an independent investigation [2]. Estonia's navy commander said further attacks in the Gulf of Finland could not be ruled out [2].

What should you take from this? Not alarm, and not a scoreboard. The Baltic now has emergency crews from Finland, Russia and Estonia working on the same fire [2]. That kind of cooperation is the dull machinery that keeps a narrow sea usable. It is worth noticing while it still works.
