AFP listed it this morning among the reasons traders are nervous: Washington's move to close a loophole that let Chinese companies reach powerful American AI chips remotely [3]. The rule itself is less dramatic and more interesting. It is 30 pages in the Federal Register, it took effect on 19 November, and its comment period runs until 19 January 2027 [1].

What it does is simple to state. Exporters now need a licence when they know that advanced chips will sit in a data centre, anywhere in the world, that gives more than 12,000,000 "aggregate TPP" (the summed processing performance of the chips) to companies headquartered in China, Macau or other countries in Country Group D:5 [1]. Linked data centres count together, "including digitally-linked infrastructure", and data centres, foreign foundries and chip exporters must check who owns and controls their customers [1]. And chips already shipped are not exempt: if a data centre later starts serving such customers, its operator must seek authorisation for the change of use [1].

The Commerce Department's press release says the rule was written "by and at the direction of" the Treasury and the National Security Council [2]. That tells you where it sits: not in industrial policy, but in sanctions practice, where the work is know-your-customer files, ownership charts and audit trails [1][2].

For you, the practical lesson is about where power lies. A rule like this is enforced by compliance departments, not by speeches. Its success will show up, if at all, in licence applications and refused contracts, which are dull documents. Its costs will show up in the same place.
