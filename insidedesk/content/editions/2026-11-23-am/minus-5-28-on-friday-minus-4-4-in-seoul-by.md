You wake up to a market that has already had two arguments without you. The first was in New York on Friday. The second is still going on in Asia as you read this. Neither is finished, and neither is the whole story.

**New York first, settled.** On Friday the Nasdaq Composite lost 5.28% to 22,617.73 and is now down 2.7% for the year [1]. The S&P 500 fell 3.7% to 6,910.39 and the Dow 949.73 points, or 1.93%, to 48,147.33; both closed 10% below their records, which is what the word "correction" means and nothing more [1]. The Nasdaq-100 fell 5.6% and was briefly 20% off its recent high [1]. Over the week the Dow lost 8%, the S&P 500 8.6% and the Nasdaq about 10%, its largest weekly fall in more than six years [1].

[[chart:us-friday-close-23nov]]

The causes CNBC lists are worth reading slowly, because none of them is a single day's news: Nvidia down about 14%, with selling increasing after Reuters reported that the SEC is exploring possible accounting issues at the company, AMD down more than 13%, Micron almost 14%, Anthropic 8.5% lower on its second day, plus the prospect of more Fed rate rises and questions about the pace of the AI build-out [1]. Walmart, Costco, Johnson & Johnson and Procter & Gamble rose [1]. When money leaves the story stocks, it goes to the shops that sell toothpaste. That is not panic; it is a preference for revenue you can see.

**Then Asia, unsettled.** Tokyo is closed for Labour Thanksgiving Day [2][6]. By 03:30 CET Seoul's Kospi was down 4.4% at 4,658.16, the Hang Seng 1.3% at 22,111.67 and the Shanghai Composite 1.4% at 3,485.47, AFP reported, with SK hynix and Samsung each about 4% lower [2]. RTHK noted that Nasdaq-100 futures had bounced nearly 3% in early trade and S&P 500 futures 2% [3]. A bounce in futures and a fall in cash shares at the same hour is the honest picture: nobody yet knows which one is right.

**The weekend's rulebook.** Taiwan's Financial Supervisory Commission banned short selling of any stock that fell in the previous session from today, the first such blanket restriction since 2015, and raised the short-sale margin from 130% to 150% [4]. Its NT$500 billion stabilisation fund has been authorised to buy since 6 November [4]. AFP reports that China made it easier to borrow to buy shares and that South Korea is preparing a stabilisation fund not used since the financial crisis [2]. Measures like these are dull, legal and temporary. They change who may sell and when, not what the companies earn.

**Bonds and gold did the quiet work.** The US 10-year yield slipped below 5% and spot gold was up 1.5% at $3,590.01 by 03:52 CET, after a 14-month low on Friday, Investing.com reported; futures markets still price a 92% chance of a quarter-point Fed rise in December [7].

**Today's paper.** Flash PMIs come from 09:15 CET for France, 09:30 for Germany, 10:00 for the euro area and 10:30 for Britain; the US flash PMIs follow at 15:45, and the Fed's John Williams speaks at 22:00 [5]. Germany's Ifo index is due tomorrow at 10:00, and the US data for the week are squeezed into Wednesday before Thursday's holiday [5][6].

What you can do with this, if anything, is the boring thing: note the figures, read the invoices when the companies print them, and let the futures argue among themselves. Nothing here is investment advice.
