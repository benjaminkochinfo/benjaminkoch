Most health news is about something new: a molecule, a device, a programme. This one is about a calendar. Researchers led by Sean O'Leary of the University of Colorado and Children's Hospital Colorado tested what happens when paediatric practices offer the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine at ages 9 to 10 instead of 11 to 12 [1]. The study was presented at IDWeek, the Infectious Diseases Society of America's annual meeting, held in Washington from 21 to 24 October [1].

The design was sturdy for a question this practical. It was a cluster-randomised trial in 31 paediatric practices in Colorado and California, running from 2021 to 2026: 16 practices were assigned to start at 9–10 and 15 at 11–12 [1].

The results are the kind you can read without a statistician. By age 13, 77.1% of children in the earlier arm had started the series and 61.6% had completed it, against 67.0% and 45.1% in the later arm [1]. Desk arithmetic: 16.5 percentage points more children fully vaccinated by 13. The median age at completion was 12.2 years in the earlier arm and 13.3 in the later one [1].

Why would two years make that much difference? The report does not say, and this desk will not guess beyond the obvious: a longer runway leaves more room for the second appointment, the missed visit and the rescheduled one. A series is only as good as the share of people who finish it.

"Lowering the age of vaccination holds promise for increasing the number of people protected against HPV, particularly as related cancers are on the rise," O'Leary said [1]. The vaccine is the same, the price is the same and the doctors are the same. What changed is when the reminder goes out. If you have a child approaching ten, it is a fair question to put to your paediatrician.
