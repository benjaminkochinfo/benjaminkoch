On Sunday, CENTCOM commander Gen. Brad Cooper said the main shipping lanes in the Strait of Hormuz were "safe and free of Iranian mines" [1]. On Tuesday, Iran's navy said an oil tanker had struck mines east of the strait, caught fire and been damaged, after switching off its tracking systems and trying to enter a "restricted and unsafe" area [2]. It gave no name, flag, crew details or casualty figures, and did not say who laid the mines [2].

**A pattern of claims.** Iran says this is not the first. The Revolutionary Guards said on Friday that two supertankers outside designated routes had hit mines; on Monday Iran said three more had [1]. Desk arithmetic: one plus two plus three is six tankers in about a week, if every claim is accurate, and none of the vessels in this latest case has been identified [1]. Tehran's message is the same each time: the strait is "unsafe and closed" to ships that do not coordinate with its armed forces [2]. Its Hormuz authority also said it would take action against a shipping company, without naming which or what [3].

**What the market heard.** Brent rose $1.48, or 1.3%, to $115.36 a barrel by 02:40 CET on Wednesday, Reuters reported, though that move followed the Houthis' claimed strike on a Saudi refinery rather than the mine report [3].

The temptation is to pick a side. You do not need to. A shipowner does not have to decide whether Gen. Cooper or Iran's navy is right; the owner has to decide whether a war-risk underwriter will cover the voyage and at what price. That is why the most useful facts this week will be dull ones: a named hull, a classification society's damage survey, a flag state's casualty report. Statements set the mood. Survey reports set the premium.
