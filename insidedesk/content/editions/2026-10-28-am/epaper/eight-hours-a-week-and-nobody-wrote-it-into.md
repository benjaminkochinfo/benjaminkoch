Nobody hires a maths teacher as a counsellor. Many of them become one anyway. RAND's 2026 State of the American Teacher Survey, a nationally representative sample of 829 K–12 public-school teachers questioned in January and February, found that nearly all provided at least one kind of mental-health or well-being support to pupils in the 2025–26 school year [1]. On average that took about eight hours a week, 17% of their working hours [1].

It costs them something. Among teachers who frequently provided more support, 62% reported high emotional costs from teaching, against 38% of those who did not frequently provide any [1]. Half said the work had become harder since autumn 2024, citing thin school resources, families' economic hardship and a divisive political climate; those teachers were more likely to report stress, burnout and symptoms of depression [1]. More than 90% said their school faced at least one challenge to pupils' well-being, with apathy, unpreparedness to learn, lack of parental involvement and absenteeism the most common [1].

The recommendation is pleasingly unheroic. "Schools can ease this strain by connecting students and families with counselors, social workers, and community services that teachers cannot provide on their own," said Elizabeth D. Steiner, a co-author [1][2]. RAND also suggests more adolescent-specific resources for secondary teachers, whose schools reported more challenges [1].

Care that depends on one exhausted adult is not a system. A referral pathway, a social worker's phone number and a counsellor with an empty slot are.
