Storms are named one at a time. Insurance policies are not always so tidy. Storm Boelo hit the UK and Ireland a day after Storm Austen, and no insurer or ABI estimate for the pair had been published by Monday morning [1]. For scale, the ABI put the February 2022 run of Dudley, Eunice and Franklin at around 177,000 claims and £497 million in payouts [1].

The awkward question is arithmetic. Home policies usually apply an excess to each claim, so a house damaged by both storms could face two, unless the insurer treats the damage as a single loss [1]. Insurers face the same question one level up: catastrophe reinsurance often groups windstorm losses within 72 hours into one event, and whether Austen and Boelo count as one or two decides how much each insurer keeps before its reinsurers pay [1]. The practical advice for you is equally dull: photograph damage, keep receipts for emergency repairs and note which storm did what [1].

In the Netherlands, where Boelo was the first official autumn storm, the Achmea brands Interpolis and Centraal Beheer had about 1,000 damage reports by Monday lunchtime, Noord-Brabant worst hit [2]. Their diagnosis is wonderfully unglamorous: wind piles leaves on flat roofs, gutters block, rain cannot drain and comes indoors [2]. The Dutch insurers' association will publish final figures only next year [2].

A climate-adaptation plan can run to hundreds of pages. Some of it is a ladder and a clean gutter.
