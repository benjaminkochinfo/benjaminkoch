The Federal Reserve announces its decision this evening, Berlin time, and markets overwhelmingly expect no change after September's quarter-point rise, CNBC reports [1]. The interesting part is the chairman. Kevin Warsh has said he is highly reluctant to signal future moves, as he seeks to move the Fed away from the "forward guidance" habit of most of this century [1].

That puts pressure on the wording. Brett Ryan of Deutsche Bank expects Warsh to signal that recent inflation data reinforce the case that "further tightening will likely be needed over time" [1]. Krishna Guha of Evercore ISI sees "material execution risk", while saying Warsh ought to be able to "show a calming steady hand" [1].

The data the Fed is weighing are not gentle. Consumer prices rose 3.7% in the year to September, driven largely by energy; core prices rose 2.6% [1]. Layoffs are near their lowest since the late 1960s, but people out of work struggle to find jobs [1]. The Chicago Fed's financial conditions index has tightened slightly since September [1]. Guha reads the committee as moving at a "baseline pace of once per quarter" [1].

There is something healthy in a central bank that refuses to pre-announce. It hands the job of judgement back to lenders, borrowers and savers, who have the data too. The cost is that every adjective in tonight's statement will be weighed like a rate. If you want the signal without the theatre, watch the two-year yield on Thursday morning. Nothing here is investment advice.
