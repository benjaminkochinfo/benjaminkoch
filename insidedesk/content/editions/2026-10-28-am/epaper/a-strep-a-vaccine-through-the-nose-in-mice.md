Most infections with group A streptococcus, Strep A, are mild; serious complications can mean long-term disability or death [1]. The MHRA estimates more than 600 million infections a year worldwide, and complications responsible for more than 500,000 deaths, mostly in low- and middle-income countries [1]. There is still no licensed vaccine [1].

On Tuesday the UK's medicines regulator described research by its own scientists, with the GSK Vaccines Institute for Global Health, published this month in Human Vaccines & Immunotherapeutics [1]. The candidate uses GMMA, tiny bubbles that bacteria naturally shed, as both the carrier for the Strep A carbohydrate and the adjuvant that boosts the immune response [1]. Given through the nose or under the tongue, it produced immune responses both at the mucosal surfaces where infection begins and throughout the body [1].

"This approach could make vaccines easier to administer and more accessible globally," said Dr Fatme Mawas, the MHRA's head of conjugate vaccines [1]. She also points to a quieter benefit: infections that do not happen do not need antibiotics, which helps against antimicrobial resistance [1].

Now the honest part, which the MHRA states itself: this was a mouse study, a pre-clinical model [1]. Mice are where vaccine ideas are tested, not where they are proven. Between here and a pharmacy shelf lie human trials, manufacturing and a regulator's approval, possibly the same regulator.

It is worth noticing who did the work. A medicines regulator that runs its own vaccine science is not a glamorous institution. It is a useful one.
