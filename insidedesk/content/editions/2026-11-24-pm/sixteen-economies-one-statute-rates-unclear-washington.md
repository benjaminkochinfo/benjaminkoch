The United States is preparing higher duties on imports from China, the European Union and 14 other economies over their industrial subsidies, Quartz reported on Tuesday, citing The Wall Street Journal and Bloomberg [1]. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer could announce the findings of his investigation as soon as Tuesday, though the timing could change; tariff rates and an effective date are unclear [1].

The legal route matters more than the headline. The investigation runs under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, which lets a president act alone against unfair trade practices, and it was opened in March, after the Supreme Court invalidated the earlier worldwide tariffs in February 2026 [1]. One door was closed by a court; another, older one requires an investigation and a finding before it opens.

What is expected: duties on the bulk of goods from the targeted economies; rates close to those of 2025; 15% or lower for partners with trade agreements, including the EU, Japan and South Korea; steeper rates for China; steel, aluminium and cars left out because they already carry security-based duties [1]. The new duties would stack on top of July's forced-labour tariffs of 10% to 12.5% on about 60 economies, which are being challenged at the Court of International Trade [1].

Markets noticed. CNBC counted the report among the reasons for Tuesday's downbeat mood on Wall Street [2]. For Europe the useful question is not whether 15% is fair but whether it is final, and what the finding says about subsidies the EU may want to defend in writing.
