A new study by the Federal Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (BAuA) and the Leibniz research centre IfADo in Dortmund measured, for the first time in a representative German sample, how often people feel lonely at work [1]. Nearly 7,800 employees aged 25 to 65 were surveyed in 2023 and 2024 [1][2].

The results come in three sizes. 26.3% said they felt alone at work, missing the company of colleagues, at least sometimes; around 17% felt excluded; just under 8% felt lonely [1]. Very few said "always". "Quite often" was more common: 5.7% felt alone, 3.7% excluded and 1.9% lonely that often [1].

The strongest link was the plainest one. People who work with others less often feel alone, excluded or lonely significantly more often [1]. Temporary contracts mattered too: those employees reported exclusion and loneliness more often than permanent staff, while managers felt excluded less often than people without staff responsibility [1]. Women reported exclusion more often; employees aged 46 to 65 reported loneliness less often than younger ones [1].

The authors draw a modest conclusion: contact with colleagues "can be actively influenced", so prevention should create enough chances for social contact during working hours, with particular attention to people on fixed-term contracts [1]. It is a cross-sectional study, so it shows what goes together, not what causes what [2].

That modesty is the useful part. Nobody needs a wellness app to act on this. A standing meeting that is actually attended, a shared lunch, a new colleague given a real task with someone rather than alone: these are management habits, cheap and boring, and the data say they are where the effect is.
