Germany's companies are in a better mood than at any time since the spring of 2023. The ifo business climate rose to 90.6 points in November from a revised 90.1 in October, its seventh rise in a row, tagesschau reported [3][1]. Firms rated their current business at 89.4, up from 88.8, and their expectations at 91.9, up from 91.5; economists polled by Dow Jones had expected the headline to reach 90.5 and expectations to slip [2].

On the same day Frankfurt's shares went the other way. The DAX closed the Xetra session at 25,005.51, down 1.31%, after Monday's gain to 25,337.27 [4]. The MDax of mid-sized companies lost 1.57%, the Euro Stoxx 50 1.17% and Milan's FTSE MIB 1.83%, while Paris's CAC 40 ended 0.05% lower and London's FTSE 100 slipped 0.13% [4].

[[chart:europe-tuesday-24nov-pm]]

The reasons for the fall had little to do with Germany. Jensen Huang's return to Nvidia was a "Befreiungsschlag" for markets, wrote Andreas Lipkow of CMC Markets, but no solution to their underlying problems: inflation and rate worries had not gone away, nor had doubts about whether the vast AI investments will pay [5]. The ifo came in roughly as expected and gave buyers no new reason; investors favoured defensive sectors [5]. In New York the Nasdaq was 2.5% lower by midday, with Nvidia down more than 7% a day after its 31% jump [6].

The ifo itself is more careful than its headline. "Die Erholung der deutschen Wirtschaft kommt voran, es fehlt jedoch an Breite," said its president Clemens Fuest: the recovery is advancing but lacks breadth [1]. In manufacturing the index fell back after six rises in a row and expectations turned negative again, with consumer goods makers reporting a worse mood [1]. Services improved on both counts and the recovery in logistics continued; retail rose, wholesale fell, and construction slipped slightly [1]. Klaus Wohlrabe, who runs the survey, told Reuters there was "noch ein gewisser Weg zu gehen" before the upswing sustains itself [3]. Thomas Gitzel of VP Bank was blunter: the lament about the supposedly poor state of the German economy, he said, "geht mittlerweile an der Realität vorbei", now misses reality [3].

So which is it, a recovering economy or a falling market? Both, because they are different instruments. The ifo asks about 9,000 managers once a month about their own business: orders, staffing, the next six months [2]. The DAX prices forty large companies, many of them global businesses, minute by minute, in a market that on Tuesday was taking its cue from chipmakers in California. A survey of a logistics firm in Kassel and the price of risk in New York can move in opposite directions on the same morning without either being wrong.

Desk arithmetic puts the day in proportion. The DAX's high this year was 27,001.67 and its low 21,863.81, Boursorama shows [4]. At 25,005.51 it stands about 7.4% below that high and about 14.4% above that low. A fall of 1.31% is noticeable; it is not a verdict on the German economy, any more than Monday's rise was.

The contrarian point is not that markets are foolish. It is that slow, dull numbers deserve more of your attention than fast, exciting ones. Seven monthly rises in a survey of thousands of firms is a trend. A one-day swing in forty share prices is weather. If you want to know whether the country is getting back on its feet, the ifo, the order books and, in time, the tax receipts will tell you more than any closing bell. Nothing here is investment advice.
