WHO's new Health inequality monitoring atlas, drawing on self-reported data from 161 member states, is the first global picture of how countries track health inequalities [1]. 89% of participating countries have commitments on health inequality in their national health plans, but only 65% have a health-ministry unit mandated to use inequality data, and just one in five published a dedicated national report in the previous decade [1].

Most countries break their health data down by age, sex and place; far fewer by income, education, ethnicity, disability or migration status [1]. High-income countries generally have better systems, but WHO says every country has room for improvement [1]. "Health inequality monitoring is an essential function," said Ahmad Reza Hosseinpoor, who leads WHO's team on the subject [1].

This is the least exciting kind of health news and among the most important. You cannot close a gap you do not measure, and measurement is an institution: a unit, a mandate, a published report every few years. The atlas is a baseline. The next edition will show who built the machinery.
