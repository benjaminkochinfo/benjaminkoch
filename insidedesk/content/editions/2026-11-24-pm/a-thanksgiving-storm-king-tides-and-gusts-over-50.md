A coastal storm will bring strong winds, coastal flooding and soaking rain to the eastern United States over Thanksgiving, The Weather Company forecasts [1]. It ramps up in the Southeast on Wednesday, a busy travel day, reaches the mid-Atlantic, the Ohio Valley and New England on Thursday, the day of peak impact in much of the Northeast, and lingers into Friday [1].

Gusts over 50 mph are possible along the coast from the Virginia Tidewater to New England and in parts of the interior, enough for power cuts and fallen trees [1]. Desk arithmetic: 50 mph is about 80 km/h. Onshore winds coincide with this week's full-moon king tides, so moderate to major coastal flooding is possible from Georgia and the Carolinas to Long Island; in coastal Delaware and Ocean City, Maryland, water levels on Thanksgiving morning could approach those of the late-September nor'easter [1].

One line in the forecast deserves more attention than the maps. The storm "will move in and move out rather quickly, instead of stalling out" [1]. Speed matters more than strength for flooding: a fast storm brings one or two bad high tides, a stalled one brings five.

There is a European lesson in the American timetable too. The worst outcomes of a well-forecast storm are usually logistical: a car parked in the wrong street at high tide, a flight not rebooked, a generator without fuel. The forecast has done its part three days early. The rest is the dull work of reading it.
