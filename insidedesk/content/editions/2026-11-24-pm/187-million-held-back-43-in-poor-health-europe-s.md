People across WHO's European region are living longer and healthier lives than earlier generations, the organisation's second Health Equity Status Report says, but an estimated 187 million, one in five, are being held back by insecure work, poor access to care and unaffordable housing [1]. They include 35 million children and adolescents, 19 million young people, 99 million working-age adults and 34 million older people [1].

For some the gap is widening. Since 2010 the share of low-income working-age women reporting poor or fair health has risen from 36% to 43%, and among men from 32% to 42%, while rates among the most affluent stayed broadly stable [1]. Among working-age adults in the least affluent group, 34% of women and 33% of men live with a long-term illness that limits daily life, against 15% and 12% of the most affluent [1]. Children in the poorest households are twice as likely to die before the age of five [1].

The report's most useful pages are about what already works. Without pensions and social transfers, 86% of older women and 83% of older men in the region would live in poverty; pensions alone cut that to 23% and 19% [1]. Poland's Family 800+ benefit is linked to a fall of almost ten percentage points in child poverty and social exclusion between 2015 and 2023; England's Frome Model, which links GP surgeries with community support, was associated with a 30% drop in emergency admissions among people with three or more long-term conditions [1].

"Money is just part of the story", said Christine Brown, who heads WHO's Venice office for investment for health [1]. The rest of the story is unglamorous: pension systems, benefit offices and community nurses, the machinery that turns a statistic into a longer life.
