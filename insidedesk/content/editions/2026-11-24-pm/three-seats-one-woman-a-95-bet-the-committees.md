France has nominated the economist Laurence Boone to the European Central Bank's executive board, Reuters reported via RTÉ, and she is expected to take the markets portfolio [1]. That puts Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos on track to succeed Christine Lagarde as president in February, while Germany's Markus Brunnermeier is likely to become chief economist when Philip Lane leaves on 31 May [1].

Three of the bank's most important posts change hands within months. The report notes one awkward detail: the reshuffle leaves the 27-member Governing Council with just one woman, because all 21 national central banks are led by men, and confirmation "could still hit a snag" over it [1]. That is a fair argument to have in public, and it is better had before confirmation than after.

Across the Atlantic, the mood swung the other way within a day. After Monday's rally had trimmed bets on a December rise, traders on Tuesday morning again priced a 95% probability that the Federal Reserve raises rates by a quarter point, and 5% for half a point, Yahoo Finance reported; the AI rally had stoked inflation worries [2]. American consumers expect prices to rise 5.7% over the next twelve months on average, down for a second month but still high, the Conference Board said [3].

The contrast is instructive. Market probabilities flip in hours; central bank boards are built to change slowly, by nomination, hearing and vote. You may find the second process tedious. It is also why a new president in February will not mean a new policy in February.
