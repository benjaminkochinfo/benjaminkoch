Event 3 January 2026

A president removed, a barrel unimpressed

Venezuela changed hands over a weekend. The oil market read the supply sheet and went back to its glut. It was right about Venezuela, and about nothing that came next.

Geo-economics Dr. Benjamin Koch / The Desk· 2 min read·5 sources

ShareXLinkedInWhatsAppTelegramEmail

On Saturday 3 January the United States captured Venezuela’s president, Nicolás Maduro. A country holding the world’s largest proven oil reserves changed management over a weekend. On Monday, Brent futures settled $1.01 higher, at $61.76 a barrel1. That was the whole drama.

By Wednesday the price was lower than before the raid. Brent settled at $60.70 on Tuesday and $59.96 on Wednesday, as traders weighed an ample supply outlook and a deal, announced by President Trump, to import up to $2 billion worth of Venezuelan crude into the United States2,3. One broker, PVM’s Tamas Varga, put the market’s view in a sentence: “oil supply will be sufficient in 2026, with or without an increase in production from the OPEC member”2.

The market was right about Venezuela. Its exports averaged about 605,000 barrels a day in January, down from 780,000 in 2025, according to Kpler data cited by Reuters4. That is a loss you can measure, and a small one against a world that the International Energy Agency then expected to be oversupplied by 3.7 million barrels a day in 20264. Regime change is a political event. Supply is a logistics event. The screen only pays for the second.

January still had a move, just not that one. On the EIA’s spot series, Brent went from $61.35 on 31 December to $72.25 on 30 January, a rise of about 18% on the desk’s arithmetic5. Reuters’ Ron Bousso listed the causes at the time: a power outage that halted Kazakhstan’s giant Tengiz field on 18 January, a winter storm that cut US output by up to 2 million barrels a day, and President Trump’s increasingly explicit threats against Iran4. Even so, his column argued that the price was “stuck in a narrow band”, and that a break into triple digits “would likely need to be a doomsday scenario”4.

Hindsight, used honestly. The column was a fair reading of the evidence on 30 January. It was also, as it happened, a description of March. Four weeks later the United States and Israel struck Iran; by 31 March the same spot series read $126.695. There is no prize for pretending to have seen it coming, and this desk claims none. The lesson is narrower and more useful: the market had correctly learned to ignore noise, and the price of that discipline is that it ignores the signal too, until the signal sinks a tanker.

For you, the January record is a reminder about where to look. Not at who holds the palace, but at who holds the export terminal, the pipeline and the insurance certificate. The first changes the news. The others change the invoice.

What it left behind

  • A working rule: markets price barrels lost, not regimes lost. Venezuela's January exports fell to about 605,000 barrels a day, against a 2025 average of 780,000, per Kpler4. That was a dent, not a hole.
  • The real move of January came from storms, a Kazakh power outage and Iran rhetoric, not from Caracas4.
Desk comment

A headline is a rumour about supply. The tanker is the fact.
