A reference forecast is a hope with a footnote

The IMF did not publish a baseline in April. It published a reference case and said, in plain print, that the world was drifting away from it. Read the footnote first.

Markets & Macro Dr. Benjamin Koch / The Desk· 2 min read·1 chart · 6 sources

ShareXLinkedInWhatsAppTelegramEmail

April began with the year’s highest print and a ceasefire. On the EIA spot series Brent reached $138.21 on 7 April, the day the United States and Iran agreed a two-week ceasefire6, and fell to $122.11 the next day4. A week later, on 14 April, the IMF published its spring World Economic Outlook under the title Global Economy in the Shadow of War1.

The honest part was the format. The Fund did not call its central number a baseline. It called it a “reference forecast”, predicated on a war of “limited duration, intensity, and scope, such that the disruptions will fade by mid-2026”, consistent with futures prices as of 10 March1. On that basis world growth would be 3.1% in 2026, 0.2 percentage point lower than in January, with headline inflation rising to 4.4%1. Without the war, the Fund said, it would have revised growth up, to 3.4%2.

Then it printed the alternatives. In an adverse scenario, growth falls to 2.5% and inflation rises to 5.4%; in a severe one, where disruptions extend into 2027, growth falls to 2% this year and next and inflation exceeds 6%2. Reuters reported the same day that the world was “already drifting toward a more adverse scenario”, and that the reference case assumed oil averaging $82 a barrel in 2026, well below the futures price of about $963.

Why this matters for you. A reference forecast is not a prediction; it is a conditional sentence with the condition in small print. If your budget, your pay round or your mortgage plan borrowed the 3.1%, it also borrowed the assumption that the strait would reopen by summer. The useful reading of April was to price the condition, not the headline.

Hindsight. The condition failed; the number, curiously, held. In October the IMF still projected 3.1% for 2026, “broadly unchanged from April, despite a more persistent energy market disruption than anticipated”5. The economy bent around the shock through rerouting, inventories and an investment boom5. The footnote was wrong and the headline survived, which is the most dangerous kind of right: it teaches people to skip the footnote.

What it left behind

  • The habit of reading scenarios as a range, not the middle number as a promise. The April range for 2026 world growth ran from 3.1% down to 2%2.
  • The oil assumption: the reference case used an average of $82 a barrel for 2026, against Brent futures of around $96 on publication day3.
Desk comment

A forecast tells you what the forecaster assumed. Read the assumption; it ages faster than the number.
