PSYCHOLOGY · DESK NOTE

A bright post wins the glance, not the memory

The platforms are summoned this morning. A new eye-tracking study is a useful reminder of what a feed can and cannot do to you.

Psychology & Behaviour Dr. Benjamin Koch / The Desk· Tuesday, 20 October 2026, 08:30 CEST· 2 min read·5 sources

ShareXLinkedInWhatsAppTelegramEmail

The government meets the main social networks this morning. L’Informé lists TikTok, Snapchat, Google’s YouTube and Meta among those summoned after the weekend’s violent scenes travelled across their feeds1; France 24 confirms a Tuesday-morning meeting with the “principales plateformes de réseaux sociaux”2. On Monday Gabriel Attal called for a “blocage temporaire, ciblé” of some platforms to “stopper l’effet de meute”3.

Before the meeting, a small piece of evidence worth having in your pocket. A study published this month in Computers in Human Behavior had 347 US adults scroll a mock feed while webcam eye-tracking followed their gaze4. The researchers changed one thing: whether the misleading post was the most vivid item in the feed, or the dull one among vivid neighbours4. When it was the brightest, people looked at it faster. But they did not look at it longer, and they did not remember its message better4. “Our eyes naturally jump toward bright, vibrant colors before our brain even starts evaluating the content,” the lead author, Jiyoung Lee, said5.

That is a narrower finding than the headlines about feeds usually allow, and that is its value. Feed design steers the first glance. The first glance is not the same as being persuaded. The authors’ own conclusion is about layout, not switches: platforms and media-literacy programmes should look at how visual contrast across a whole feed directs attention5.

Attention, meanwhile, can be counted. Médiamétrie measured an average of 16.7 million viewers for Sunday’s eight-minute presidential address across the channels that carried it3. A country that sat down in those numbers for a speech is not only a country of reflex scrollers.

The constructive habit is boring: when a clip arrives first and brightest, let the second look — the source, the date, the official count — do the deciding. The meeting this morning will be judged the same way: not by how it looks, but by what it writes down.

What to watch

  • What comes out of this morning's platform meeting: named measures and deadlines, or a photo opportunity.
  • Whether any platform publishes takedown or reach figures for the weekend's videos.
  • Whether the 'temporary block' idea survives contact with the people who would have to run it.
Desk comment

The eye is quick and the memory is choosy. Build for the second.
