Resilience is an inventory

Six months after April's warning, the IMF's growth number had not moved and its inflation number had. The Fund's own explanation is a list of things that run down.

Markets & Macro Dr. Benjamin Koch / The Desk· 2 min read·1 chart · 4 sources

ShareXLinkedInWhatsAppTelegramEmail

On 13 October the IMF published its autumn World Economic Outlook, Resilience Under Strain, Urgent Choices2. The headline number was the same as in April: world growth of 3.1% in 20261,3. In the Fund’s words, the forecast “remains broadly unchanged from April, despite a more persistent energy market disruption than anticipated”1.

What did move. Headline inflation for 2026 was now forecast at 4.7%, against 4.4% in April, and at 4.1% for 2027, against 3.7%1,3. Core inflation was put at 3.9% this year1. Growth for 2027 was raised to 3.4%, from 3.2% in April1,3. Put simply: the world produced about what the Fund expected and paid more for it.

The explanation is a list. The IMF credited two sets of factors. The first: “the rerouting of energy supplies, increased energy production in other countries, inventory drawdowns, substitution to other sources of energy, including renewables, and higher energy efficiency”1. The second: an investment boom driven by artificial intelligence1. Read the first list slowly. Rerouting has a cost, production elsewhere has a ceiling, and inventory drawdowns have a floor.

The Fund said so itself. “Dwindling energy inventories could allow geopolitical shocks to transmit even more forcefully to energy prices,” the blog warned, and the investment boom was “concentrating risks”, making the expansion “heavily reliant on a small number of firms, sectors, and economies”1. Six days earlier the IEA had put the remaining public emergency stocks of its members at around 1.1 billion barrels4. That is a large cushion, and a smaller one than in March.

The constructive reading. The record of 2026 so far is not one of collapse. It is one of a system that bent: ships rerouted, producers elsewhere pumped, households and firms paid more and carried on. That is genuinely good news, and the desk is happy to say it. It is also news with a maturity date. Resilience built from stocks is a loan; resilience built from capacity is an asset. For the year’s last quarter, the question for you is which of the two your own budget is leaning on.

What it left behind

  • The cushion's size: IEA governments still held around 1.1 billion barrels of public emergency stocks on 7 October4.
  • The Fund's risk scenario, in which commodity prices and fiscal worries push up term premiums and inflation expectations1.
Desk comment

A buffer that is being used is not a buffer. It is a bill with a delayed due date.
