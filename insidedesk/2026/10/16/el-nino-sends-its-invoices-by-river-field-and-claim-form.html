CLIMATE · DESK NOTE

El Niño sends its invoices by river, field and claim form

Copernicus says September was the second-warmest on record and the El Niño ocean region hit an all-time high. The bill travels by barge, harvest and insurer.

Climate & Weather Dr. Benjamin Koch / The Desk· Friday, 16 October 2026, 16:36 CEST· 2 min read·4 sources

ShareXLinkedInWhatsAppTelegramEmail

The Copernicus Climate Change Service says September 2026 was the second-warmest September on record globally, at 1.63°C above the pre-industrial level and the second month in a row above 1.5°C1. The Niño 3.4 region of the Pacific, the patch of ocean used to track El Niño, reached an all-time monthly high of 29.70°C, above the peaks of the very strong events of 1982/83, 1997/98 and 2015/161.

In Europe the signal was dryness. It was the third-warmest September on record for the continent, and parts of the Rhine, Danube, Vistula and Garonne-Dordogne saw record-low September river flows, disrupting navigation, water supply and agriculture1. A river is a supply chain with a water level. Weather also set this week’s power prices: in the week of 5 October more wind pulled weekly electricity prices down in most European markets, while TTF gas futures rose on low storage, falling temperatures and the strait, settling at €84.35/MWh on 12 October, the highest since December 20222.

The same El Niño can be too dry and too wet at once. India’s monsoon fell short: Maharashtra has declared drought in 265 of its 358 talukas, Karnataka has notified 217 of 240, and Andhra Pradesh 388 of 688 mandals3. In Canada the problem was rain. By 21 September Saskatchewan’s harvest was 41% done against a five-year average of 82%, after more than three times normal precipitation in its main growing areas, and inspectors are finding sprouted wheat4.

Insurance is how weather becomes a price, and it is slow on purpose. The head of Agriculture Insurance Company of India said estimating claims now is premature, because yields are still being measured3. That is the constructive part: the bill is large, but it is also legible, and it arrives on a schedule. For households, the link runs through food, heating and insurance premiums over months, not days. That is a description, not advice.

What to watch

  • Official Canadian estimates of crop damage, due in mid-November4.
  • Yield assessments in India: final crop-insurance claims depend on them3.
  • EU gas storage against falling temperatures: the energy-weather link, every winter2.
Desk comment

Weather is local. Its invoice is global, and it arrives late.
