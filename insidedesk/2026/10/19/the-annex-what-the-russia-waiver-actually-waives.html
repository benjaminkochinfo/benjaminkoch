The Annex: what the Russia waiver actually waives

The headline says sanctions were lifted. The order says the duties were written, set at 500% and 100%, and switched off in the same document, with the older sanctions left standing.

Geo-economics Dr. Benjamin Koch / The Desk· Monday, 19 October 2026, 08:30 CEST· 2 min read·4 sources

ShareXLinkedInWhatsAppTelegramEmail

The annex this morning is the order itself, published by the White House on 16 October1. It is shorter than the commentary about it, and more precise.

Section 2 sets the duties. Goods from Russia: duty raised by 500% ad valorem. Goods from the five largest importers of Russian crude and gas, if they knowingly make new purchases on or after 18 October: plus 100%. Goods from the five largest facilitators of Russian oil sanctions evasion: plus 100%1.

Section 4 switches them off. Under section 115 of the act, the president waives “any duties” under sections 112 and 113, and the sanctions in a long list of other sections, having certified to Congress that the waiver is in the national interest1. The waivers “remain in effect until” the president or the secretary of state decides that further action is appropriate1. There is no end date. The waiver came two days before the law’s implementation deadline3.

What survives. “For the avoidance of doubt”, nothing in the order affects sanctions under the executive orders behind the national emergency on Russia, as continued on 24 March 20261. The older architecture stands; the new floor was built and not walked on. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the president would take up to 60 days to assess the economic impact2.

What it sits next to. A diesel deal: Russian exports to the United States and others starting at about 2.3 million barrels this month and rising to about 7.6 million barrels a month, according to Trump’s announcement as reported by Reuters2. It follows a temporary waiver of sanctions on Russian diesel exports through April 20273. On Sunday the president wrote that he could sign the sanctions “later, or any time that I want!”4. Zelenskyy called the waiver “wrong”3.

The reading. A law with a waiver clause is still a law. The duties exist on paper, the monitoring duty in section 3 exists, and section 5 names which department would pull which lever1. That is leverage stored, not leverage spent. Whether it is ever spent is a political question. Where it sits, and who holds the switch, is now a matter of public record, which is more than most podium lines leave behind.

What to watch

  • Secretary Rubio's 60-day assessment of the economic impact: whether it is published, and whether it recommends ending any waiver.
  • The Trade Representative's monitoring under section 3 of Russian crude and gas purchases by the five largest buyers.
  • Whether the next tranche of the diesel deal arrives on schedule: about 2.3 million barrels this month.
Desk comment

Read the annex: the switch is in section 4, and the wiring is still in the wall.
