Ebola reached an eighth Congolese province on Sunday. The headline counts provinces. The response counts tests, contacts and quiet health zones, and those are the numbers that end outbreaks.

Congo’s health ministry said on Sunday that the Ebola outbreak had reached Nord-Ubangi, an eighth province, with two confirmed cases, one of them fatal, both men in their twenties1. Authorities have recorded at least 9,400 cases and more than 4,500 deaths since the outbreak was declared in mid-May, the deadliest in the country’s history. It is caused by the Bundibugyo virus, for which there is no approved vaccine or treatment1. Reuters, citing the latest situation report, put the total at 9,415 cases and 4,536 deaths in the 24 hours to 16 October, with 67 new confirmed cases that day, 44 of them in Ituri3.

That is the headline, and it is grim. It is also the least useful number in the story. A province is a line on a map. An outbreak is beaten, or not, in smaller units: a sample in a laboratory, a contact visited at home, a burial done safely, a health zone that goes six weeks without a case.

Read the situation report, not the map. The ministry’s daily report for 14 October, published the next day, is a dull document in the best sense2. Laboratories analysed 506 samples, against 394 the day before, and the share testing positive fell to 12.8% from 17.3%2. More tests and a lower share of positives is the combination you want: it suggests the net is being cast wider, not that the virus is being found less because fewer people look. Of 4,048 alerts received, 98.5% had been verified, and every one of the 612 validated suspect cases had been investigated2.

Then the uncomfortable lines, printed just as plainly. Of 33,264 contacts to be followed, 25,956 were seen in the previous 24 hours: a follow-up rate of 78.0%, down from 81.8% the day before and below the required threshold of 95%2. Only 242 of the 612 suspects, 39.5%, had been transferred to transit or treatment centres2. Twenty-two of the day’s 37 confirmed deaths happened in the community rather than in a treatment centre2. And 269 front-line workers have been infected since the outbreak began2. Those are the gaps an outbreak travels through, and the report names them itself. A system that publishes its own shortfall every day is a system that can be held to fixing it.

Shrinking pockets, a wider map. The World Health Organization’s regional director for Africa, Mohamed Yakub Janabi, put it in one sentence on Friday: “We see pockets of Ebola generally going down, but unfortunately geographically it’s spreading out”1. Both halves are in the data. Twelve of the 65 health zones touched so far had gone at least 42 days, two full incubation periods, without a new confirmed case, and three more were between 22 and 42 days2. Yet the newest provinces sit along the border with the Central African Republic, where armed violence has forced thousands to flee and roads and phone networks are poor1. Distance is the virus’s ally. Logistics is the response’s.

The authorities in North Kivu, where the WHO says substantial transmission continues1, are answering with logistics. From today, people coming from Ituri may not enter the province for a week. Motorcycles may carry only the driver and one passenger, taxis four. Burials are limited to 20 family members, and checkpoints must screen temperatures and provide hand-washing. Today is declared a “Stop Ebola Day”, with only authorised services at work4. None of this is glamorous. Most of it is enforceable. That is the point.

What was caught, and where. In Kenya an eighth case was confirmed on Sunday: a 49-year-old patient attendant, likely exposed to the country’s first case, who was tested after developing symptoms in quarantine. He was one of 38 people tested that day, and the six other living patients were stable5. A case found inside quarantine is the system working: the chain is visible and the contacts are already known. A case found in a market is the system being surprised. Kenya’s cases, so far, are the first kind.

Why this leads on a Monday in Berlin. Because the same lesson keeps arriving from different desks this morning. Oil is moving through a contested strait on escorts and detours rather than a treaty. A protest movement in France is being answered, or not, by a list of meetings and a payroll of substitute teachers. What decides the outcome is rarely the announcement. It is the follow-up.

Seventy-eight per cent is not good enough, by the response’s own standard. It is also a number, published daily, against a threshold, published daily. That is what accountability looks like before anyone holds a press conference about it. Watch it climb, or not. It will tell you more about this outbreak than the next province on the map.

What to watch

  • The daily contact follow-up rate against the response’s own 95% threshold. It is the single best number for whether chains of infection are being closed.
  • Nord-Ubangi: whether its first cases have known contacts, or appear without a link to any earlier case.
  • North Kivu’s one-week entry ban for travellers from Ituri, in force from today, and whether new cases in the province fall in the fortnight after it.

The headline says sanctions were lifted. The order says the duties were written, set at 500% and 100%, and switched off in the same document, with the older sanctions left standing.

The annex this morning is the order itself, published by the White House on 16 October1. It is shorter than the commentary about it, and more precise.

Section 2 sets the duties. Goods from Russia: duty raised by 500% ad valorem. Goods from the five largest importers of Russian crude and gas, if they knowingly make new purchases on or after 18 October: plus 100%. Goods from the five largest facilitators of Russian oil sanctions evasion: plus 100%1.

Section 4 switches them off. Under section 115 of the act, the president waives “any duties” under sections 112 and 113, and the sanctions in a long list of other sections, having certified to Congress that the waiver is in the national interest1. The waivers “remain in effect until” the president or the secretary of state decides that further action is appropriate1. There is no end date. The waiver came two days before the law’s implementation deadline3.

What survives. “For the avoidance of doubt”, nothing in the order affects sanctions under the executive orders behind the national emergency on Russia, as continued on 24 March 20261. The older architecture stands; the new floor was built and not walked on. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the president would take up to 60 days to assess the economic impact2.

What it sits next to. A diesel deal: Russian exports to the United States and others starting at about 2.3 million barrels this month and rising to about 7.6 million barrels a month, according to Trump’s announcement as reported by Reuters2. It follows a temporary waiver of sanctions on Russian diesel exports through April 20273. On Sunday the president wrote that he could sign the sanctions “later, or any time that I want!”4. Zelenskyy called the waiver “wrong”3.

The reading. A law with a waiver clause is still a law. The duties exist on paper, the monitoring duty in section 3 exists, and section 5 names which department would pull which lever1. That is leverage stored, not leverage spent. Whether it is ever spent is a political question. Where it sits, and who holds the switch, is now a matter of public record, which is more than most podium lines leave behind.

What to watch

  • Secretary Rubio's 60-day assessment of the economic impact: whether it is published, and whether it recommends ending any waiver.
  • The Trade Representative's monitoring under section 3 of Russian crude and gas purchases by the five largest buyers.
  • Whether the next tranche of the diesel deal arrives on schedule: about 2.3 million barrels this month.

Nearly 2,400 arrests, burned town halls, a presidential address. The part that will matter by Christmas is smaller: substitute teachers, a round table with a date, an after-action report.

Saturday was one of the most violent days of unrest France has seen in years. The interior ministry counted nearly 2,400 arrests and almost 1,000 police officers and gendarmes injured, seven seriously; in Paris alone police reported a record 855 arrests1. Town halls were stormed in Limoges, where the 19th-century building burned, in Marseille and in Lille, and traders’ unions said more than 1,500 shops and businesses had been ransacked1.

On Sunday at 8pm Emmanuel Macron addressed the country2. He drew a hard line between the “plusieurs centaines de milliers”, several hundred thousand people, who marched with legitimate worries about the cost of living, fuel prices and schools, and those who “destroy, ransack and loot”1,2. Then he set out a method in three parts2. Prefects, with mayors, may ban certain gatherings. Parties and unions are asked to condemn violence without ambiguity. And this week the prime minister will bring pupils, students, teachers, unions, employers, local authorities and parliamentarians to one table to work on concrete proposals. The education ministry’s consultation platform has collected more than 100,000 contributions2. Macron also said he would travel to the Gulf states to secure fuel and gas supplies2.

Cynics will call a meeting a non-answer. The small print says otherwise. The government has already promised 3,000 more substitute teachers, a place for students in school decision-making, and has scrapped planned increases in some university registration fees3. That is the protesters’ original complaint, missing teachers and crumbling classrooms, answered in payroll lines rather than adjectives. The school protests began in late September, a week into the school year3, and they asked for staff, not slogans.

The harder question is the state’s own competence. The mayor of Limoges said his municipal police ran out of ammunition at about 3.30pm and called it “a colossal failure of the state”; politicians on the left and the right called for the interior minister’s resignation1. Macron asked the government to draw every lesson on intelligence and anticipation and promised full transparency2. That is a promise with a deliverable, an after-action report, and it should be held to one.

The constructive part is the calendar. France votes for a new president in April 20273, and Macron’s own framing on Sunday was that the argument belongs in public meetings, newspapers and peaceful rallies, not in the street2. Institutions that answer peaceful demands with budget lines and violent ones with courts are doing their job, slowly and without applause. …

What to watch

  • The prime minister's round table this week: does it publish a timetable with numbers in it, teachers hired and classrooms repaired, or only a communiqué?
  • The government's review of intelligence and anticipation failures on Saturday, which the president promised with ‘toute la transparence’.
  • How prefects use their latitude to ban gatherings, and how the courts treat those bans.

Your feed showed fire. The counts show several hundred thousand people walking. Before you form a view of a weekend, ask for the denominator.

Your feed this weekend showed fire: burning barricades in Paris, a torched town hall in Limoges, a wrecked carabinieri car in Milan2,3. Those images are real. They are also a biased sample, and the bias runs in a predictable direction.

By the French president’s own count, several hundred thousand people demonstrated on Saturday with legitimate demands1. The Guardian describes tens of thousands of peaceful marchers in major cities, parents, teachers and grandparents among them, separate from the groups that surprised the police and from the looters who followed2. Nearly 2,400 arrests is a large number. Set against several hundred thousand marchers, it is a small share. The camera, though, goes where the fire is.

Psychologists call this the availability heuristic: what comes to mind easily feels common. A burning building is easy to remember; a peaceful march of grandparents is not. The result is that a weekend gets remembered by its worst hour, and a cause gets judged by its worst participants.

The useful habit is the denominator. Lyon’s mayor, Grégory Doucet, asked for a “sense of nuance” and for “avoiding stereotypes”2. Milan’s mayor, Giuseppe Sala, called the reasons for the protest “understandable” and the violence “not justifiable”3. Both are denominator sentences: they keep the many and the few in separate columns.

Leaders know the bias, and lean on it in both directions. Portugal’s prime minister warned rivals against “importing” climates of instability that, he said, “have no support in Portuguese society”, while hundreds camped in Lisbon’s Rossio Square over rents3. Both can be true at once. The camp is small. The housing numbers behind it are not.

What to watch

  • Turnout figures from organisers and from police for the next marches. The gap between the two counts is itself a measure of trust.
  • How much airtime this week's round table in Paris gets compared with Saturday's fires.
Desk comment

The fire is the picture. The march is the data. Ask for the denominator before you form the opinion.

The admiral says the strait is open. The monitor counted ten ships attacked in a week. Both are true, and the second one is the one your fuel bill hears.

Podium
“The Strait of Hormuz is controlled by the United States and our regional partners, not Iran, and it remains open for business.”
Adm. Brad Cooper, commander of US Central Command, 16 October 2026
Print10

vessels attacked in or near the Strait of Hormuz in one week, the highest weekly count since the war began in February.

UK Maritime Trade Operations, reported by CBS News, week to 18 October 2026

The podium. In a video statement on Friday, Adm. Brad Cooper said about 400 merchant ships had sailed through the Strait of Hormuz in the previous seven days, and that US forces had helped Gulf partners ship more than 100 million barrels of crude in that period1. That brought the crude moved with US help in recent months to more than 1.3 billion barrels, he said. “Let me be clear,” he added: the strait “remains open for business”1.

The print. The UK Maritime Trade Operations agency counted at least 10 vessels attacked in or near the strait since the previous Sunday, a weekly high since the war began in February, and 23 attacks since the start of October, almost double the average2. It also said it was “struggling to accurately report” the true number, because most ships now refuse to report attacks2. The analytics firm Kpler said in a note on Friday that intensified attacks had cut Gulf crude flows to about 8.5 million barrels a day on a seven-day average last week, from more than 20 million the week before3.

The gap. Desk arithmetic: 100 million barrels in seven days is about 14.3 million barrels a day. That is well above Kpler’s 8.5 million, but the two figures come from different counters, with different definitions and slightly different weeks, so do not subtract one from the other. Read them together instead. Ships are moving, in large numbers, and they are moving under fire. Many now switch off their tracking systems to avoid detection, and still face attacks2.

Verdict. Not a lie and not the whole truth. “Open” is a word about access. Price is a word about risk. A strait you can cross only with escorts and dark transponders is open the way a road with checkpoints is open: you get through, at a cost. That cost reaches you in delivered fuel prices long before it reaches a podium. It is also worth noticing what moved Brent this morning: a sanctions waiver in Washington, not a change at sea3.

What to watch

  • UKMTO's weekly count. A week below ten attacks would be the first print to agree with the podium.
  • Kpler's seven-day average of Gulf crude flows, which fell to about 8.5 million barrels a day last week.
  • How many ships report attacks at all: UKMTO says most now refuse to, which makes every count a floor.
Desk comment

‘Open’ is a podium word. ‘Attacked: 10’ is a print. A strait can be both, and the bill is written by the second.

The Fed has gone quiet before its 28 October decision. That leaves the floor to inflation in London and Tokyo, surveys in Europe and a rate decision in Beijing.

The Federal Reserve has entered its communications blackout ahead of its 28 October decision1. For a week the most-quoted committee in the world says nothing in public, and that is a feature, not a gap. When the speakers go quiet, the data has the floor.

There is plenty of it. UK inflation for September arrives on Wednesday, after 3.1% in August; markets had priced a 95% chance that the Bank of England raises rates on 5 November, CMC Markets wrote on Friday2. The People’s Bank of China sets its lending rates on Tuesday, euro-area consumer confidence comes on Thursday, and on Friday the euro-area purchasing managers’ surveys and Japan’s September inflation2. CMC also notes that markets expect the Fed itself to raise rates sooner than the Bank of England, after hotter-than-expected September consumer and producer prices in the United States2.

Beijing is the quiet exception. Economists largely expect the People’s Bank to hold its benchmark lending rates through the rest of the year and leave the work to fiscal measures, such as the 550 billion yuan of unused debt quota passed to local governments in early October3.

Currencies are waiting too. The yen barely moved this morning at 157.89 per dollar, and the offshore yuan held at 6.68391.

The desk’s view is old-fashioned. A committee that talks every day trains markets to trade adjectives. A blackout sends everyone back to the invoice: what prices actually did last month, measured by statisticians who do not give interviews. You can do the same this week. Skip the previews. Read the releases.

What to watch

  • UK inflation for September on Wednesday, against August's 3.1%: the last big print before the Bank of England meets on 5 November.
  • Japan's September inflation on Friday, with the yen near 158 per dollar.
  • Euro-area consumer confidence on Thursday and the October PMIs on Friday: the first October evidence on how households and firms are carrying the energy bill.

Silence from the podium is not a lack of news. It is the week the print gets to speak first.

Ordinary & Inherited Life · 2 min

Gas and power arrive smoothed, by the month. Heating oil arrives by lorry, once a year, at the price of the day. This October that price has been falling while the pump has not.

If your home is heated with oil, your heating bill works differently from your neighbour’s. Gas and electricity arrive by the month, smoothed and averaged. Heating oil arrives by lorry, once or twice a year, and the price on the day of the order is the price of the winter.

That price, by the national average the comparison portal tanke-günstig publishes for a 2,000-litre order, was €177.59 per 100 litres late on Sunday1. The spread between the Länder is wide: €171.90 in Saxony-Anhalt, €183.91 in Hesse, €174.90 in Berlin1. On Friday the portal noted that heating oil had fallen by almost ten cents a litre since the start of October, unlike motor fuels1. ADAC’s last weekly reading still had diesel at €2.247 a litre2.

Part of the bill is not set by the market at all. The portal estimates that taxes made up about 40% of the end price in 2025, with the CO₂ levy alone at about 17.7%, more than the energy tax itself1. That part is known in advance and changes by law, on a date, which makes it the most predictable line on the invoice.

The ordinary lesson is not “order now”. This desk does not give that advice, and nobody can time a war. It is that a once-a-year bill rewards knowing your own numbers: tank size, last winter’s litres, what you paid per 100 litres. Adam: the warning is the infrastructure

Spain's storm reached its worst hours on Sunday, with red warnings on the Valencia and Alicante coast. The rain is weather. A warning that arrives in time and is followed is policy.

The storm Spain calls dana Adam, a cut-off low, entered its most adverse phase on Sunday, and the state weather agency AEMET expected it to last into Monday1. AEMET kept a red warning, its highest level, on the coast of southern Valencia province and northern Alicante, for 90 litres per square metre in an hour and accumulations of 200 litres in 12 hours and 300 in 241. The Balearics were lowered to orange on Sunday afternoon; Andalusia and Murcia stayed on orange1.

The gauges were already filling. On Sunday AEMET’s stations recorded 149.6 litres per square metre in Miramar, 135.4 in Pego and 131.7 in Oliva, and the regional association Avamet measured almost 250 litres in Barx by 18:501. On Saturday a woman in Cabrera de Mar, north of Barcelona, was swept away while crossing a flooded street with her family; her body was found on Sunday1. AEMET warned that much of the rain is falling on ground that is already saturated1.

There is a temperature story underneath. Before the cold front arrived on Saturday, temperatures across the northern half of the peninsula had been about ten degrees above normal; by Sunday they were back to normal or slightly below1.

Valencia has reason to know that the most important part of a storm is the instruction attached to it. AEMET’s spokesman, Rubén del Campo, called it “fundamental” to follow the civil-protection advice and not to travel unless strictly necessary1. A red warning is only infrastructure if it arrives in time, is believed and is acted on. That chain, forecaster to phone to person, is now being tested on the Safor coast.

The constructive part is that the chain exists, is public and is specific: litres per hour, named coasts, named hours. AEMET expects the instability to ease from Tuesday1. The second test comes then, in the claims and the clean-up, and it is just as dull and just as important.

Champi missed Guam by twelve miles

One of the year's eight or nine Category 5 storms passed just south of the island. Luck did part of the work. Preparation did the rest.

Super Typhoon Champi passed about 12 miles (20 km) south of Guam’s southern tip on Saturday as a Category 4 storm with 150 mph winds; its northern eyewall only grazed the island, which escaped catastrophic wind damage1. Six hours later, the Joint Typhoon Warning Center reanalysed it as a Category 5, with sustained winds of 165 mph (265 km/h)1. Sea temperatures along its path are an unusually warm 29–30°C1.

Yale Climate Connections counts at least eight Category 5 storms worldwide in 2026, perhaps nine if one is confirmed in review, against a 1990–2025 average of 5.3 a year1. Champi is the second major typhoon within 50 miles of Guam this year, after Bavi in July1.

The constructive note is in the island’s record. From 1948 to 2025, 27 typhoons of category 1 or above passed within 50 miles of Guam1. An island that has done this often builds, drills and warns accordingly. Twelve miles was luck. The fact that luck was enough is engineering.
