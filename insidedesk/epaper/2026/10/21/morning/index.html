Tehran says Hormuz stays shut until Washington accepts its terms. Washington says traffic is 80 to 100% of normal. The Revolutionary Guards say their mines stopped three tankers. Only one of these is a count.

On Tuesday, three official voices described the Strait of Hormuz. Each was confident, and they did not agree with one another. The lesson is not that one of them is lying. It is that a statement and a count are different instruments, and only one of them is built to be checked.

Tehran’s version. Foreign minister Abbas Araghchi told Iraqi television that indirect talks with the United States continue through Pakistan and Qatar, and that the strait will stay closed unless Washington accepts Iran’s conditions1. Iran has offered a seven-day plan under which the waterway would fully reopen on the sixth day. Araghchi said Washington had accepted the conditions but wanted “additional provisions”1. “The negotiating table is still there,” he said1.

Washington’s version. In an interview with Network18, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said that at any given time the traffic and energy moving through the strait are “80 to 100 per cent equal” to what they were before the conflict2. He also said US forces destroy the “overwhelming majority” of drones and missiles fired at ships each night, but that “every now and then” one gets through2. He said the onus is on Iran to stop, and that economic pressure will continue until it does2.

The Guards’ version. The same day, the Revolutionary Guards said their naval mines had “halted” two tankers at what they call an illegal crossing point in the southern part of the strait, and that a third tanker carrying more than two million barrels of crude had exploded and caught fire after hitting a mine3. AFP reported the claims from the Guards’ own statements; on Tuesday there was no independent confirmation in that report3.

What has actually been counted. A shut strait, a strait at 80 to 100% and a strait mined at its southern exit cannot all be true at once. The most recent independent measure in the record is less dramatic than any of them. The UK Maritime Trade Operations centre, a Royal Navy office that logs ships and incidents, counted 254 transits in the week to 14 October, 125 outbound and 129 inbound4. The Guardian put that at roughly 70% below pre-conflict levels4. UKMTO also recorded 12 attacks in the seven days to 16 October4. The shiptracker Kpler counted at least 18 vessels reportedly attacked over two weeks5.

So the strait is neither shut nor normal. It is open, thin and dangerous. That is a boring answer, and it is the only one with a method behind it.

What the price did with all this. The oil market heard all three versions on one day and reacted as you would expect. Brent touched $111.99, a five-month high, then settled at $108.79, down 76 cents on the day5. The swing was more than $6 within the session6. An unconfirmed report that a deal was close knocked prices down as much as 3.3%, and a diplomat involved in the talks then said no agreement was near6. By early Wednesday in Asia, Brent was back around $109.526. In other words, the price moved on the speeches and came back to rest on the risk. Analysts at ING called it a market “pricing in a significant risk premium”5.

Why the dull count matters more. Each statement on Tuesday served its speaker. Tehran needs the strait to look closed so that reopening it is worth something in the talks. Washington needs it to look open so that pressure on Iran looks cheap. The Guards need their mines to look effective. None of this makes the statements worthless. They tell you what each side wants to trade. But none of them tells you how many ships went through yesterday.

The transit count does. It is published by an office with no seat at the table. It comes out at a regular interval, and if it moves you can see by how much. The same is true of the insurance quotes shipowners pay and the queues of tankers at anchor. These are the invoices of the conflict, and they change more slowly and more honestly than the statements do.

The constructive reading. There is a real negotiation, with real intermediaries and a written plan with a numbered day for reopening1. Intermediaries, a written plan and a date are the dull scaffolding that agreements are built on. A deal, if it comes, will show up first in the weekly count, then in the price. If it does not come, the count will show that too. Either way, you will not need to take anyone’s word for it.

Until then, the useful habit is to treat every confident sentence about Hormuz as a bid in a negotiation, and the weekly count of ships as the receipt.

What to watch

  • The UK Maritime Trade Operations centre's next weekly transit count; the last one cited was 254 transits in the week to 14 October.
  • Whether any shipowner or flag state confirms the three tankers the Revolutionary Guards say were hit; on Tuesday the claim came only from the Guards.
  • What the 'additional provisions' are that, according to Araghchi, Washington wants added to Iran's seven-day plan.

The Dow rose 1% on 3M and a row of steady quarterly reports while the AI names drifted. After the bell, SpaceX printed its first quarterly profit and Seoul rose 3.6% on it. Earnings did what forecasts could not.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at 52,495.88 on Tuesday, up 529.85 points or 1.02%. The S&P 500 rose 0.28% to 7,712.57, and the Nasdaq Composite slipped 0.02% to 26,424.821. Healthcare led with a gain of 2.34%; only communication services and technology fell1. The VIX, the options market’s gauge of expected swings, dropped 6.13% to 17.311.

Who did the lifting. It was not the AI giants. Among companies worth more than $1 trillion, only Amazon and Tesla rose, by about 1%1. The work was done by older businesses reporting their quarters: 3M rose 5.8% after sales and profit beat expectations and it raised guidance, Northrop Grumman gained 4.57%, and Hasbro rose more than 11% on a 34% jump in sales of its Magic: The Gathering line1,2. A toy maker’s card game moved more than a chip index on Tuesday. That is what a rotation into receipts looks like.

After the bell. SpaceX reported its first quarterly profit, $4.4 billion, on revenue up 161% to $14.58 billion1,2. Its capital spending rose to $19.59 billion from $6.9 billion in the second quarter2. Netflix went the other way, falling nearly 5% after hours as it cut its advertising revenue forecast to $2.7 billion from $3 billion2.

Asia read the reports, not the worries. On Wednesday morning the Kospi rose 3.62% and the Nikkei 225 1.76%, and Nasdaq 100 futures were up 0.7%3. CNBC TV18 tied the move to the overnight earnings from the biggest beneficiaries of AI spending3.

Last week the AI story was told in fears about financing. This week it is being told in quarterly accounts, line by line. You do not have to like the valuations to prefer the second kind of evidence.

What to watch

  • CoreWeave, which rose 1.65% on Tuesday after falling 25% last week.
  • Whether the Kospi holds its gain after one of the country's worst IPO debuts on record.

At the government's investment summit, the economy minister spoke of more than €220 billion in commitments. Her ministry later said the figure covers all projects since January, not pledges made at the summit. The correction is the useful part.

On Tuesday the federal government held an investment summit in Berlin for about 200 chief executives from Germany and abroad1. The chancellor, Friedrich Merz, praised German punctuality and early rising1, and on the same day warned against Berlin plans to socialise housing2. Finance minister Lars Klingbeil was blunter with the guests: “I want your money”1.

The number. Economy minister Katherina Reiche spoke of investment commitments of more than €220 billion for this year1. It was the figure most likely to make the evening news.

The footnote. According to Der Spiegel, a spokesman for Reiche’s ministry later clarified that the sum had no direct connection to the summit. It is the general total of investment projects since the start of the year1. Which investments will result from the summit itself cannot yet be told1.

Why this is good news, quietly. Governments like large round numbers, and summits are built to produce them. What matters is that the ministry corrected the record the same day, on the record, when asked. A total since January is still a real figure; it is just a different one. The institution did its job: it said what the number was.

For you, the practical reading is simple. When a summit announces a total, ask what period it covers and whether a list exists. If there is no list, it is a mood. If there is one, it is a plan.

What to watch

  • Whether the economy ministry publishes a list of projects behind the €220 billion figure.
  • Which investments are later attributed to the summit itself; Der Spiegel says that cannot yet be told.

The three centre groups in the European Parliament reached a compromise on 2035 car CO2 rules. Renew's leader said she was 'not entirely sure how official it is'. That honesty is worth noting.

The European People’s Party, the Socialists and Renew Europe, the three groups that form the pro-European majority in the European Parliament, reached an agreement on the automotive package, Renew’s leader Valérie Hayer announced in Strasbourg1.

According to first reports, manufacturers would have to cut tailpipe CO2 emissions of new cars by 87.5% from 2021 levels by 2035, instead of 100%1. The remaining 12.5 points would be covered by a 7.5% allowance for green steel made in Europe and a 5% unconditional margin1. Combustion-engine and hybrid cars would remain allowed after 20352.

Hayer was candid about the status: “I’m not entirely sure how official it is, but an agreement was reached last night”1. That is the right way to describe it. A deal between group leaders still has to pass the groups, the committees and the plenary, and then be negotiated with the member states.

Supporters such as Forza Italia MEP Salvatore De Meo call it the end of “ideological bans”1. Whatever you think of that, the number that matters is 87.5, and it will be in the text.

A six-year study of more than 600 US workers found that people who fear AI will make their whole occupation obsolete are more likely to excuse small misconduct at work. The lead author's advice is aimed at employers, not workers.

A study led by researchers at Washington State University followed more than 600 workers across US industries, including finance and construction, over six years1. It was published in the journal Occupational Health Science1.

What it measured. Not ordinary job insecurity, the worry of losing one job at one firm, but what the lead author, psychology professor Tahira Probst, calls occupation insecurity: the fear that technology will transform or erase a whole line of work1. A bartender who loses a job can find another bar. A medical coder whose occupation is automated cannot simply move to another employer1.

What it found. Workers who worried that automation would make their occupation obsolete were more likely to justify behaviour they would normally call inappropriate, such as turning up late or taking a colleague’s lunch1.

What the author does not claim. The study was not designed to estimate how many jobs AI will replace1. Probst also told Spokane Public Radio that “a single study doesn’t prove anything” and asked for replication in places with different labour protections and AI rules1. That caution is part of the result.

The constructive point. Her main takeaway is about management: “how organizations roll out these AI tools matters enormously”1. If employees are left to make sense of the change alone, the fear does its own damage, whether or not it is justified1. A clear plan for the rollout, written down and explained to staff, is routine office work. It may also be the best protection against the fear doing harm.

What to watch

  • Replications in countries with stronger labour-market protections, which the lead author herself calls for.
  • Whether employers publish how they plan to introduce AI tools, the factor the study's author says matters most.
Desk comment

Anxiety left unexplained turns into small rule-breaking. Explanation is cheaper than either.

Psychology & Behaviour · Short item · ePaper extra, added 06:30 CEST

In nine experiments with 8,769 adults, both selfish and generous people bent their guesses about how their choices affected others. Only the selfish version was contagious.

Marius Vollberg and Jörg Gross of the University of Zurich gave participants a choice between 7 points for themselves or 5, knowing only a range for what another person would get: 1 to 3 points or 3 to 51. Then they asked them to guess the other person’s actual payout, and paid them for accuracy1.

People who took the 7 tended to report the two options as more similar for the other person than they were. People who took the 5 tended to inflate the good their choice did1. The distortions appeared even when the guesses were made before the choice, and even when the choice was forced, which points to preferences rather than after-the-fact excuses1.

The finding with consequences came last. When participants were shown a previous player’s estimates first, only the estimate that made selfishness look harmless changed what they did: they became more selfish1. The generous distortion did not spread1. The authors note limits: abstract tasks, mainly US participants, and large differences between individuals; fewer than 10% produced the full selfish pattern1.

The paper was published on 1 October; it appears here because its point sharpens the edition’s other stories about convenient numbers. When the consequences of a choice are vague, ask for the range, not someone’s reading of it.

United Airlines third-quarter 2026 results and outlook, 20 October 2026

The podium. In August, with jet fuel near $4 a gallon, United’s chief executive Scott Kirby told reporters: “I think we’ll recover 100% in fourth quarter,” though he stopped short of a firm commitment1. In July the company had said it expected to recover 80% to 90% of higher fuel costs in the third quarter and all of them in the fourth1.

The print. Tuesday’s results tell a different story about the same quarter. United now assumes jet fuel will cost $4.80 a gallon on average in the final three months of the year, against $3.90 in the third quarter and $2.43 a year earlier1,2. Third-quarter fuel expense rose 61.6% to $4.84 billion, $1.8 billion more than a year before1. The company guides to adjusted fourth-quarter earnings of 50 cents to $1.50 a share1. With $5.72 earned in the first nine months, even the top of that range gives $7.22 for the year, against July’s guidance of $9 to $111. The shares fell 1.5% after hours3.

The gap. Revenue is doing what the podium promised: it rose 17.4% to $17.9 billion, and United expects fourth-quarter revenue growth above 20%1. Fares are rising. The trouble is that fuel rose faster. A recovery measured in revenue is not a recovery measured in profit.

Verdict. The podium was not dishonest; it described a plan made at $4 fuel. The filing describes the world at $4.80. When a company talks about passing on costs, check the fuel line in its next report. It shows what actually happened.

What to watch

  • The Gulf Coast jet fuel price, which the $4.80 assumption follows.
  • Whether other airlines reporting this month make the same change to their fuel assumptions.

In her statement to the IMF's steering committee, the ECB president said the energy shock is too large to look through, but that rising long-term rates will slow growth and dampen pass-through. That is a reason for patience, written down.

The statement was delivered in Bangkok on 17 October and posted by the Bank for International Settlements on 20 October1. It is a formal text, which makes it more useful than an interview.

The numbers it rests on. Euro-area headline inflation rose to 3.8% in September, mostly on energy; core inflation rose slightly to 2.5%1. Wages “have so far shown no material response to the energy shock”1. The ECB raised rates by 25 basis points in June and again in September1.

The sentence that matters. “The energy shock is too large to ‘look through’ but a measured response remains appropriate”1. The reason given is specific: financing conditions have tightened, with long-term rates “rising notably” since the last meeting, which “will slow growth and reduce pass-through by more than projected” in September1. In plain words: the bond market has already done part of the ECB’s work.

How the reaction function is written. Lagarde lists three criteria: the inflation outlook, underlying inflation, and the strength of transmission1. You can check each one against published data. That is a better way to run a central bank than a speech about resolve.

Across the Atlantic. The US 10-year yield fell to 5.11% on Tuesday and futures priced about an 83% chance that the Fed holds next week, with about 73.7% for a December rate rise2. Two central banks, the same energy shock, and both are letting the long end of the curve do part of the work.

What to watch

  • The ECB's October policy meeting; the statement keeps every option open and names three criteria.
  • The Federal Reserve's meeting next week; futures put a hold at about 83%.
  • Euro-area wage data, which so far show 'no material response' to the energy shock.
Desk comment

A central bank that names its three tests is easier to argue with, and that is a compliment.

Money, FX & Committees · Short item · ePaper extra, added 06:30 CEST

Lagarde's statement also contained plumbing: a repo facility for foreign central banks, a settlement system for tokenised assets, and a deadline for the digital-euro law.

Most of the attention on Lagarde’s statement to the IMF’s steering committee will go to rates. The rest of it describes plumbing, which tends to last longer.

The first item is EUREP, the Eurosystem’s repo facility for central banks outside the euro area. The Governing Council set its operational features in July, and “more than 30 central banks have expressed interest” in the past six months1. A repo line lends euros against collateral. It is a dull tool and an important one, because it means that in a crisis a foreign central bank can get euros without selling in a hurry.

The second is Pontes, the Eurosystem’s system for settling tokenised assets in central-bank money, which went live on 21 September1. The third is the digital euro: the ECB plans a 12-month internal pilot in the second half of 2027, and says adopting the regulation by the end of 2026 is “key” to that timetable1.

None of this will move a market this morning. But the international role of a currency is built on pipes like these, not on speeches about it.

Ordinary & Inherited Life · 2 min

The Länder transport ministers meet today and tomorrow to discuss a smartphone tariff that charges each trip and stops charging at the Deutschlandticket price. The host minister says there is no money for it. Both points belong in the ledger.

The transport ministers of Germany’s sixteen Länder meet on Wednesday and Thursday in Schwangau, in Bavaria1. A draft resolution quoted by the Rheinische Post proposes an offer “for occasional users with nationwide validity, analogous to the Deutschlandticket”1.

How it would work. North Rhine-Westphalia and Hamburg propose a smartphone check-in and check-out tariff1. Each trip is recorded, the cheapest fare is worked out automatically, and over a month you would never pay more than a Deutschlandticket costs2,3. NRW already runs a similar scheme3. “Whoever only occasionally travels by bus and train must be able to do so just as simply and on fair terms as frequent riders with the Deutschlandticket,” said NRW transport minister Oliver Krischer1.

The ledger. The Deutschlandticket costs €63 a month and rises to €66.80 in January1. A single fare in Berlin is now €4, according to Der Spiegel, about 90% more than the €2.10 it cost almost twenty years ago3. Desk arithmetic: at €4 a trip, sixteen trips in a month cost €64 and the seventeenth takes you past the January ticket price of €66.80.

The objection. The host, Bavaria’s transport minister Christian Bernreiter, rejects the proposal; there is a shortage of money everywhere, he says2. That is not a speech. It is a budget line, and it deserves an answer in euros.

The constructive reading. A monthly cap is a dull idea. It needs a fare database, an app and a clearing system between transport associations. It would also help the people who are least well served today: those who ride the bus now and then.

What to watch

  • The conference's resolution on Thursday; according to tagesschau, North Rhine-Westphalia would work out the design.
  • Whether the Länder that oppose it, starting with Bavaria, name the funding gap in euros.
Desk comment

The fairest tariff is the one that remembers what you already paid this month.

Three accounts of one strait, and the one that keeps a ledger

Tehran says Hormuz stays shut until Washington accepts its terms. Washington says traffic is 80 to 100% of normal. The Revolutionary Guards say their mines stopped three tankers. Only one of these is a count.

On Tuesday, three official voices described the Strait of Hormuz. Each was confident, and they did not agree with one another. The lesson is not that one of them is lying. It is that a statement and a count are different instruments, and only one of them is built to be checked.

Tehran’s version. Foreign minister Abbas Araghchi told Iraqi television that indirect talks with the United States continue through Pakistan and Qatar, and that the strait will stay closed unless Washington accepts Iran’s conditions1. Iran has offered a seven-day plan under which the waterway would fully reopen on the sixth day. Araghchi said Washington had accepted the conditions but wanted “additional provisions”1. “The negotiating table is still there,” he said1.

Washington’s version. In an interview with Network18, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said that at any given time the traffic and energy moving through the strait are “80 to 100 per cent equal” to what they were before the conflict2. He also said US forces destroy the “overwhelming majority” of drones and missiles fired at ships each night, but that “every now and then” one gets through2. He said the onus is on Iran to stop, and that economic pressure will continue until it does2.

The Guards’ version. The same day, the Revolutionary Guards said their naval mines had “halted” two tankers at what they call an illegal crossing point in the southern part of the strait, and that a third tanker carrying more than two million barrels of crude had exploded and caught fire after hitting a mine3. AFP reported the claims from the Guards’ own statements; on Tuesday there was no independent confirmation in that report3.

What has actually been counted. A shut strait, a strait at 80 to 100% and a strait mined at its southern exit cannot all be true at once. The most recent independent measure in the record is less dramatic than any of them. The UK Maritime Trade Operations centre, a Royal Navy office that logs ships and incidents, counted 254 transits in the week to 14 October, 125 outbound and 129 inbound4. The Guardian put that at roughly 70% below pre-conflict levels4. UKMTO also recorded 12 attacks in the seven days to 16 October4. The shiptracker Kpler counted at least 18 vessels reportedly attacked over two weeks5.

So the strait is neither shut nor normal. It is open, thin and dangerous. That is a boring answer, and it is the only one with a method behind it.

What the price did with all this. The oil market heard all three versions on one day and reacted as you would expect. Brent touched $111.99, a five-month high, then settled at $108.79, down 76 cents on the day5. The swing was more than $6 within the session6. An unconfirmed report that a deal was close knocked prices down as much as 3.3%, and a diplomat involved in the talks then said no agreement was near6. By early Wednesday in Asia, Brent was back around $109.526. In other words, the price moved on the speeches and came back to rest on the risk. Analysts at ING called it a market “pricing in a significant risk premium”5.

Why the dull count matters more. Each statement on Tuesday served its speaker. Tehran needs the strait to look closed so that reopening it is worth something in the talks. Washington needs it to look open so that pressure on Iran looks cheap. The Guards need their mines to look effective. None of this makes the statements worthless. They tell you what each side wants to trade. But none of them tells you how many ships went through yesterday.

The transit count does. It is published by an office with no seat at the table. It comes out at a regular interval, and if it moves you can see by how much. The same is true of the insurance quotes shipowners pay and the queues of tankers at anchor. These are the invoices of the conflict, and they change more slowly and more honestly than the statements do.

The constructive reading. There is a real negotiation, with real intermediaries and a written plan with a numbered day for reopening1. Intermediaries, a written plan and a date are the dull scaffolding that agreements are built on. A deal, if it comes, will show up first in the weekly count, then in the price. If it does not come, the count will show that too. Either way, you will not need to take anyone’s word for it.

Until then, the useful habit is to treat every confident sentence about Hormuz as a bid in a negotiation, and the weekly count of ships as the receipt.

The convenient estimate is the one that spreads

In nine experiments with 8,769 adults, both selfish and generous people bent their guesses about how their choices affected others. Only the selfish version was contagious.

Marius Vollberg and Jörg Gross of the University of Zurich gave participants a choice between 7 points for themselves or 5, knowing only a range for what another person would get: 1 to 3 points or 3 to 51. Then they asked them to guess the other person’s actual payout, and paid them for accuracy1.

People who took the 7 tended to report the two options as more similar for the other person than they were. People who took the 5 tended to inflate the good their choice did1. The distortions appeared even when the guesses were made before the choice, and even when the choice was forced, which points to preferences rather than after-the-fact excuses1.

The finding with consequences came last. When participants were shown a previous player’s estimates first, only the estimate that made selfishness look harmless changed what they did: they became more selfish1. The generous distortion did not spread1. The authors note limits: abstract tasks, mainly US participants, and large differences between individuals; fewer than 10% produced the full selfish pattern1.

The paper was published on 1 October; it appears here because its point sharpens the edition’s other stories about convenient numbers. When the consequences of a choice are vague, ask for the range, not someone’s reading of it.
