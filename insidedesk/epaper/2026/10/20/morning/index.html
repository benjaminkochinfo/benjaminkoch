Météo-France warned on Monday that Gard and Hérault could go red. By 04:06 on Tuesday nine départements were orange and none red. A warning that overshoots is not a failed warning; it is a cheap one.

On Monday morning Météo-France wrote that if its scenario of very intense, possibly stationary storms was confirmed, “a move to red vigilance would then be expected” for the Hérault and the Gard3. The Hérault prefecture passed on the same message: a risk of the warning being raised to red from the middle of the day3. The agency’s episode note put the expected totals on the eastern Hérault and the Gard at 200–250 mm, locally 300 mm2.

At 04:06 on Tuesday the bulletin read differently. Nine départements are orange and none is red1. The Gard is orange for rain-flood, storms and floods; the Hérault is now orange only for floods, with its rain-flood and storm risks down to yellow1. The Aveyron and the Lozère are orange for floods. The new orange cells lie to the east: Ardèche, Bouches-du-Rhône, Haute-Corse, Var and Vaucluse1. Twelve more départements are yellow1.

The gauges were less dramatic than the models. By about 16:00 on Monday, Nîmes had recorded some 35 mm since the start of the day, Saint-Gilles 48 mm, Saint-Hippolyte-du-Fort 50 mm and Le Vigan 60 mm, “less than the weather forecasts feared”, as Midi Libre put it4. In mid-afternoon the models lowered the expected intensity, and the episode was calm enough that the emergency services in the Gard had not been called out4. By 18:00 the private network Météo Ciel showed 79.7 mm on Mont Aigoual and 73 mm at Le Vigan3.

The storm went elsewhere. On Monday evening Météo-France brought the Bouches-du-Rhône’s orange forward from Tuesday to that same evening, describing a rain-and-storm line over the Côte Bleue and Fos producing about 50 mm in an hour3. The local forecasting page Météo Méditerranée 34 wrote that the situation on the ground was “far from the scenario” the models had shown that morning3.

A vigilance map is not a promise of disaster. It is a probability turned into instructions. When the most likely bad outcome does not arrive, the honest reading is not that the warning was wrong. It means the precautions were bought at the low price, before anyone needed them.

The precautions are worth listing, because this is where the system works. The Gard closed eight departmental roads in advance, including submersible bridges on the Cèze, the Vis and at Anduze4. Nîmes closed its municipal and intercommunal facilities from 16:00 on Monday until Tuesday noon, crèches and leisure centres included, with emergency childcare and a minimum service kept open4. Montpellier closed its parks and the Lez reserve, banned parking on the quai du Pirée and opened two gymnasiums from 19:00 for anyone who needed a roof3. Speed limits on the motorways were lowered by 20 km/h for cars and to 70 km/h for lorries3.

None of this is glamorous. All of it targets the one behaviour that kills most people in these episodes. Météo-France’s own reminder is that the main cause of death in a flood is driving on submerged roads and crossing submersible bridges over rivers in spate3. A closed bridge costs a detour. A crossed one can cost a life.

Rain is the fast part of a Mediterranean episode, and rivers are the slow part. That is why the orange stays on the flood column for the Gard, the Hérault, the Lozère and the Aveyron this morning, even where the rain warning has eased1. The Lez, the upper Hérault, the Vidourle and the Vistre were placed in orange for floods until about midday on Tuesday3. The ground had a head start. The agroclimatologist Serge Zaka noted on Monday that soils in the area were already filled to 70–90% of their water-holding capacity after earlier storms3.

Across the border the same depression has already shown its other face. Spain’s weather agency named it Adam, the first name on the list it shares with Météo-France3. RTVE counts four dead in Spain, more than 450 people rescued and more than 1,500 incidents handled by Valencia’s emergency services. The state weather agency AEMET says the Safor district has seen nothing like it since November 19875.

Météo-France expects the rain, still intense, to move east on Tuesday afternoon and evening into Provence-Alpes-Côte d’Azur and Corsica. That would end the episode in the départements hit first, and the rain would clear completely the following night2. The machinery for following it is public and free. The vigilance map is updated at least at 06:00 and 16:00, and there is an ordinary-rate phone line, 05 67 22 95 001. Two automatic services, APIC and Vigicrues Flash, send SMS, email and voice alerts to subscribed prefectures, town halls and operators2.

You do not need to read a weather model. You need to read the colour of your département before you drive, and believe it a little more than the sky.

What to watch

  • Météo-France's 06:00 and 16:00 vigilance updates as the rain moves into Provence and Corsica on Tuesday afternoon and evening.
  • The river column: Gard, Hérault, Lozère and Aveyron stay orange for floods (crues) after the rain itself has eased.
  • Whether Nîmes and Montpellier reopen their closed facilities on schedule at Tuesday noon, and what the clean-up and insurance figures say later in the week.

At Mar-a-Lago the Russia decision was still open. In the executive order, every Graham Act duty is already waived, with no end date.

Podium
“A lot of you don't know what my decision is yet, but we'll figure it out, and you'll figure it out.”
President Donald Trump, speaking to reporters at Mar-a-Lago; Secretary of State Marco Rubio, 17 October 2026
Print500%

The order sets a 500% duty on Russian goods and 100% duties on large buyers of Russian oil and gas, then waives all of them, with the waivers in effect until the President or the Secretary of State decides otherwise.

Executive order 'Implementing the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026', section 4, 16 October 2026

The podium. The day after he signed the order, the President told reporters at his Mar-a-Lago club: “A lot of you don’t know what my decision is yet”1. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the President would use “the 60-day period of time” to work out what sanctions and tariffs would mean for the global and US economy1. On Tuesday morning oil desks were still describing the situation as markets assessing an order that “waived some sanctions and tariffs”4.

The print. The order is short and specific. Section 2 raises the duty on goods from Russia by 500% ad valorem, and by 100% on goods from the five largest buyers of Russian crude and gas if they make new purchases on or after 18 October2. Section 4 then waives those duties, together with a long list of the Act’s sanctions provisions2. The waivers “shall remain in effect until such time as the President or the Secretary of State” decide that further action is appropriate2. The text gives no 60-day limit. The law firm Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer read it the same way: the waivers “do not have a specified expiration date”, and existing Russia sanctions, including the 2022 ban on importing Russian oil into the US, still apply3.

The gap. The podium describes a decision still to be made. The document describes one already made, which can be reversed at any time and has no deadline. Both can be true, but only one is binding. The 60 days come from a briefing; they are not in the text of the order.

Verdict. Read the order and treat the remarks as commentary on it. If the review becomes an official document, it will have a date on it. Until that happens, buyers of Russian oil face no 100% tariff, and the existing pre-Graham sanctions are unchanged.

Fifteen ships hit in ten days. The UK's maritime office still counts every passage, and the count says the strait is open but thin.

Iran has attacked at least 15 container ships passing through the Strait of Hormuz in the past ten days, according to the Guardian’s count1. Some were hit by drones and others by mines. On Sunday one ship was struck by a rocket-propelled grenade fired from a small boat north-east of the Saudi port of Jubail1. The UK Maritime Trade Operations centre (UKMTO) recorded 12 attacks in the seven days to Friday 16 October1.

The more useful figure is how many ships still got through. UKMTO’s weekly overview counted 254 transits, 125 outbound and 129 inbound, in the week to 14 October1. That is roughly 70% below pre-conflict levels1. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards say they may attack any ship that crosses without their permission or with its transponder switched off1. Tehran has also offered to reopen the strait within six days in return for sanctions relief1.

On the diplomatic side, Russian foreign minister Sergei Lavrov visited Tehran. He offered to mediate and proposed a China–Russia–Iran summit on security in the strait1. That is a proposal for a meeting, not an agreement. The weekly UKMTO count will show whether anything changes at sea. You should trust it more than any statement from the region’s capitals.

After the speeches, the calendar: a Senate mission awaiting its rapporteurs, an Assembly inquiry proposed this morning, unions at Matignon at 10:30 the day after.

Sunday was a speech and Monday was a curfew map. Tuesday is the day the institutions book their questions — in writing, with dates.

The Senate’s law committee decided on Monday to open a cross-party fact-finding mission on the events of 17 and 18 October1. Its own press release does not mince words: the policing plan “a rapidement été débordé par les événements”, and the mission will establish “la chaîne des événements”, judge the policing set-up and the penal response, and determine administrative, institutional, legal and political responsibilities1. Every Senate group sits on it; it may ask for the powers of a commission of inquiry, and its rapporteurs are to be named “mercredi prochain”1.

At the Assembly, Yaël Braun-Pivet proposes this morning, at the Conférence des présidents, a commission of inquiry into the disorder, the methods of the violent groups and the public-order strategy, including “le dimensionnement et la doctrine” of policing2. Socialist, LFI and Ensemble deputies backed the idea on LCP on Monday evening; the RN said it would decide its position on Tuesday morning3. One dull constraint matters: with the presidential election ahead, parliamentary work ends on 28 February, so any inquiry may report early next year rather than run its usual six months3.

The government’s own table is set for Wednesday. Matignon has invited the CFDT, CGT, FO, CFE-CGC and CFTC for 10:30, with the prime minister, the interior minister and the labour minister, according to the invitation letter seen by franceinfo4. Separately, the Senate’s long-running mission on the future of public order — launched in January — was due to close its work on Monday and present its report to the press on Wednesday5. Coincidence of calendar, says Public Sénat. Useful coincidence, says this desk: a report written before the weekend is a baseline nobody can accuse of hindsight.

You do not have to pick a side in the argument to notice what changed overnight. Questions now have owners, rooms and dates. That is how a country that lost control of some streets for a weekend starts taking back the paperwork.

The platforms are summoned this morning. A new eye-tracking study is a useful reminder of what a feed can and cannot do to you.

The government meets the main social networks this morning. L’Informé lists TikTok, Snapchat, Google’s YouTube and Meta among those summoned after the weekend’s violent scenes travelled across their feeds1; France 24 confirms a Tuesday-morning meeting with the “principales plateformes de réseaux sociaux”2. On Monday Gabriel Attal called for a “blocage temporaire, ciblé” of some platforms to “stopper l’effet de meute”3.

Before the meeting, a small piece of evidence worth having in your pocket. A study published this month in Computers in Human Behavior had 347 US adults scroll a mock feed while webcam eye-tracking followed their gaze4. The researchers changed one thing: whether the misleading post was the most vivid item in the feed, or the dull one among vivid neighbours4. When it was the brightest, people looked at it faster. But they did not look at it longer, and they did not remember its message better4. “Our eyes naturally jump toward bright, vibrant colors before our brain even starts evaluating the content,” the lead author, Jiyoung Lee, said5.

That is a narrower finding than the headlines about feeds usually allow, and that is its value. Feed design steers the first glance. The first glance is not the same as being persuaded. The authors’ own conclusion is about layout, not switches: platforms and media-literacy programmes should look at how visual contrast across a whole feed directs attention5.

Attention, meanwhile, can be counted. Médiamétrie measured an average of 16.7 million viewers for Sunday’s eight-minute presidential address across the channels that carried it3. A country that sat down in those numbers for a speech is not only a country of reflex scrollers.

The constructive habit is boring: when a clip arrives first and brightest, let the second look — the source, the date, the official count — do the deciding. The meeting this morning will be judged the same way: not by how it looks, but by what it writes down.

What to watch

  • What comes out of this morning's platform meeting: named measures and deadlines, or a photo opportunity.
  • Whether any platform publishes takedown or reach figures for the weekend's videos.
  • Whether the 'temporary block' idea survives contact with the people who would have to run it.

The Fed decides on 28 October, the ECB on 29 October and the Bank of Japan on 30 October. The euro is near $1.12 and the dollar near ¥158.5 while markets wait.

The next fortnight’s dates are already published. The Federal Reserve’s committee meets on 27–28 October1. The ECB’s Governing Council meets in Frankfurt on 28–29 October, with a press conference on the 29th2. The Bank of Japan decides on 30 October3.
